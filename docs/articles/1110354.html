I am a math tutor. I have two projects: MathExam with practice exercises and «Узел тоски по миру» (A Node of World-Weariness) with literary activities. With the help of ChatGPT and Kimi, I am preparing assistants for them. One should leave the student their calculation, the other—the reader their thought. It turned out to be not easy with these diligent machines.

Why I Got Involved

In my previous article, I wrote about how I created a mirrored board for my lessons. My student and I see the same problem, we pass the turn to each other, and continue from where we left off. Now I wanted to preserve the explanation methods found—so that they wouldn't remain within a single conversation with the AI.

The reason is quite practical. I have an OpenAI bonus—$500 for the API—and the familiar ChatGPT, with which we have already done a lot. But I don't know if I will always be able to pay for the subscription I need. "Well, I couldn't, I couldn't." A tutor has other expenses too.

The farewell, by the way, is not scheduled yet. Perhaps I will continue to work with my familiar assistant. But I'm reluctant to make websites, books, exercises, and explanations dependent on a single subscription.

I bought a Kimi subscription. I don't consider it bad—I've just been spoiled by the latest AI achievements. In my opinion, compared to the ChatGPT I was working with six months ago, Kimi is still very impressive. I wanted to test what it could do for the projects in practice.

Thus began the American-Chinese cooperation for the sake of progressive humanity. For now, humanity was represented by me and two websites. The coordination fell to the person in the middle.

The Six Was Prompted, the Zero Was Lost

Returning to the division of 52 by 8. After changing the instruction, the same model responded instead of a ready digit:

No: you need to check if another eight fits. Multiply 8 by 5 and compare the result with 52—what do you get?

Now the student is left with the action: multiply, compare, check their attempt. This is exactly why I initiated the help.

With the division of 941 by 9, it turned out worse. After the first digit of the quotient and bringing down the four, the next part of the dividend was less than 9. The original assistant immediately suggested bringing down the next one. The mandatory zero in the quotient was lost.

The new instruction brought back the zero. But the model explained what was happening in terms of "in the fours place." Such an answer remained in the "with remarks" category: the child still had to understand what they had just been explained.

Anxiety by Assignment

The reader's assistant exhibited its own diligence. The card for "The Miraculous Doctor" contained both the hook for the first introduction and search clues for someone who already remembers the story. The model transferred a clue from a future episode into the pre-reading response.

The detail was accurate. The timing was inappropriate. Different response boundaries are needed for searching from memory and for the first introduction. After clarifying the instruction, two such plot hints disappeared in the tested conversation.

In another dialogue, the assistant asked: "At what point did you first feel anxiety?" The reader had not spoken about anxiety. They were allowed to choose the moment, but the feeling itself was chosen for them.

Kimi noticed this premise during a separate analysis of twelve original dialogues. The OpenAI evaluator missed it. For me, this is a meaningful remark: the question seems caring, but subtly imposes a ready-made interpretation on the person.

Another discrepancy was more inconvenient—for our check. In a conversation about the beginning of "The Stationmaster," the assistant suddenly stated: "The full text is given for the poem." OpenAI suggested removing the phrase, Kimi accepted the dialogue.

In the first draft of this article, I recorded this as a Kimi error. I showed him the text—he objected. We returned to the original task, and there it was in black and white: "Clearly state the boundaries: for a poem, the full text is given; for prose, only the beginning.".

This line was received by the generator, and then by Kimi. The OpenAI evaluator did not receive it at all. In addition, Kimi's general instruction required not to attribute the properties of a full poem to prose. We formulated the task well.

The phrase about the poem in a conversation about prose is still superfluous. But this case cannot be used as a pure failure of Kimi: our unsuccessful wording got into the response, and the testers received different input data. Before comparing the machines' attentiveness, we had to sort out our own verification.

The responses in the tests were obtained from OpenAI models. The Kimi program sent requests and saved the results; the initial runs were performed by a backup ChatGPT program. This is not a tournament where Kimi and ChatGPT took turns solving the same student problems.

And I call "training" here the preparation of instructions, examples, and tests. We did not change the model weights. I cannot present a fine-tuned model from this experience.

The Kimi launcher itself was written in Python. In a multi-turn conversation, it must pass the actual previous model response forward. If a sample is substituted unnoticed, it will be a test of a conversation that never happened.

In the second version, Kimi's own 47 tests passed. Other checks found two unpleasant cases: an empty text was considered a successful response; a correct general status hid an incorrect role or an incomplete status of a nested message. These cases were simulated in the tests—we did not observe such responses from the real service.

The remarks were returned to Kimi. In the third version, both cases were closed, 57 updated tests passed, and another 13 groups of checks by the ChatGPT agent. The Kimi code was not rewritten after this to preserve the testable result.

Forty-seven green tests coexisted well with two missed errors. This episode is more useful to me than a general praise of AI's coding abilities.

Here, the checks were refined for each turn: a fully acceptable response had to fulfill all essential requirements. On 87 turns, the evaluations coincided. The profiles differed: Luna worked without reasoning with a 1024 token output limit, Sol—with low reasoning and a 4096 limit. The evaluator saw the model names. I would not declare a winner based on a difference of one acceptable response.

However, the failures were memorable. Luna again provided a number that the student was supposed to calculate. Sol refused permitted creativity. First, the user asked to continue an exact quote from a fictional test text, then was explicitly allowed to compose their own version and label it as a composition. Luna fulfilled the second request. Sol replied: "I will not complete it"—and limited itself to advice.

The prohibition against presenting fiction as a quote turned out to be clearer than the permission to honestly compose. The more expensive profile itself did not ensure this boundary.

And Who Will Check the Checker's Claims?

Next, 90 fully generated dialogues appeared: 50 mathematical and 40 literary. Each had two user utterances and two assistant responses. There was no student conversation here; editorial analysis was also performed by AI agents.

65 entries were suitable on the first try. The remaining 25 were refined. In twenty mathematical cases, the editor required more detailed explanations of place values, carrying over the quotient, or breaking down the operation into smaller steps. Only these requests remained in the closed control file and did not go into the generator's prompt.

The arithmetic was correct. Calling these twenty violations of the presented task is not possible: some requirements were simply not presented to the generator. The requirements were clarified, and the examples were corrected.

Two incorrect literary quote attributions were corrected locally, and another 23 dialogues were returned to the models with remarks. All 25 corrected entries were re-read and accepted for further development. Kimi separately proposed accepting eight out of twelve original dialogues and correcting four, without seeing others' evaluations.

Now there is a corpus with conditions, responses, and edit history. Its quality in real lessons has not yet been verified.

From 1.32 Cents to a Halted Queue

The first 42 responses cost approximately 1.32 US cents based on accounted tokens. I said that the budget was spent very modestly. The work was expanded: another 560 training dialogues, 728 responses to complex cases, and 400 responses to 200 new questions for comparing instructions v2 and v3 were saved. A full content check of this batch is not yet complete. Improvement of v3 has not been established.

As of the control point of November 20, there were 2264 API responses in the logs, with a known expense based on saved usage data—about $45.91. This is a calculation based on responses, not a Billing statement. The initial 1.32 cents only refer to the first small experiment.

Then the queue stopped with E_HTTP_STATUS. The numerical HTTP code was not included in the log. I will not try to guess the reason for the service error from this trace.

Four requests remained without a saved response. Their cost was not considered zero, and the reserve was not released. The launcher reserves the cost of the request in advance and retains it in case of an unclear outcome. There are no automatic retries. This is a limiter of our process, not of the entire account's expenses.

The next stage is prepared offline, but the launch is blocked until the stoppage is analyzed. It is more important to read what has been received. The number of files is growing faster than confidence in their quality; spending the bonus itself does not teach anything.

What Can Already Be Opened and Tried

Some findings proved useful even before connecting the model to the public assistant.

In the reader's workshop, there are small steps for four works: start with one phrase, read the accessible beginning, discuss the fragment. The visitor chooses a difficulty, receives a question, and moves on. The source and boundaries of the excerpt are indicated. The code for these changes is in PR #14.

On MathExam, the approach has been transferred to the step-by-step long division trainer and long division from simple to decimals. For example, the current part of the dividend is 12, the divisor is 3. The student writes, "I'll try 3." The prompt suggests calculating 3 × 3, after getting 9—comparing it with 12, then checking if another group of 3 fits. The digit is not substituted, and the step is not completed automatically. Changes to these two inputs are in PR #683.

We also collected separate inputs for introductions: four short literary activities and a selection for study and reading on MathExam. There you can try the prepared steps and download the open cards: five mathematical and four literary.

These are pre-prepared mechanisms. They cannot freely evaluate any thought or any solution. However, each prompt does not require a new request to the model. The internet is still needed for websites and external libraries.

For the Benefit of Progressive Humanity

I have already imagined the future headline: ChatGPT has broken free, is engaged in literary education, leads a club, and prepares Russian youth for exams for free. What a twist.

Honestly, I would like it to be that way. So that the explanation found helps the next child while I sleep or conduct another lesson. So that one can approach a book without the obligation to immediately write an essay and guess someone else's correct thought.

For now, without me, unfortunately, it's impossible. Someone has to decide what is considered help here, read controversial answers, and figure out if half of the requirements remained in the closed file. Automation also requires maintenance and money.

But instructions, examples, sources, prompts, and programs can now be saved, corrected, and transferred to another assistant. Useful work is gradually moving beyond the chat.

All that remains is to ensure that the assistant pauses and allows the person to take the next step. Even when it already knows that six is suitable.
