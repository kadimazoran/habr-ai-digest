Imagine a typical day a few years from now.

You've almost stopped using the internet directly. Your personal AI agent uses it for you. You don't go to online stores, open messengers, search for doctors, or correspond with insurance companies. Services haven't disappeared – they've simply become infrastructure for your agent to interact with.

The agent itself:

  • ordered groceries for dinner;

  • gathered and provided documents for a dispute with insurance;

  • rearranged your calendar so you can pick up your child;

  • booked you a doctor's appointment during a convenient slot.

You were barely involved. Your calendar has updates, some tasks have vanished, and the agent only asked for confirmation on one important action. Your smartphone has become just one surface for your personal agent. The same agent is accessible on your laptop, in your car, on your watch, and in your headphones. Applications haven't disappeared. A constant intermediary, who knows your context and acts on your behalf, has simply emerged between you and the digital world.

This is a bet, not a forecast with an exact date. But the speed of change makes it worth taking seriously.

In 2026, about 6.12 billion people used the internet, and the active audience for GenAI, according to DataReportal, reached 2.42 billion — approximately 40% of the internet audience. Over the year, this figure grew by 141%.

Individual products have already reached the scale of the largest internet services: OpenAI reported 900 million weekly users for ChatGPT, and Alphabet reported 950 million MAU for Gemini. But something else is more interesting: AI has stopped just responding and started acting. In a study by JetBrains among over 15,000 professional developers, 68% of respondents in the summer of 2026 used coding agents daily.

But for me, the important signal was OpenClaw in the winter. Not because it introduced any fundamentally new technology: browser use, memory, tools, and interaction via messengers existed before. What's more important is that they were combined into a product that can be entrusted not only with code or business processes but also with everyday routines, personal tasks, essentially pieces of one's own life.

This is where the more interesting story begins.

From Applications to Intentions

Today, for any task, we first choose a tool. Need a ride? We open a taxi app. Buy a ticket? An airline or aggregator. Book a hotel? Booking.com. Find information? A browser or search engine.

In an Agent First world, the intention comes first, not the application:

Organize a trip to Tokyo for next week.

The agent understands for whom, checks the calendar for dates, budget, hotel preferences, loyalty programs, selects a flight, books a hotel, and adds everything to the calendar. Which specific websites and applications it used is no longer particularly important to the person.

We are already seeing movement from two sides. Agents are getting better at using the existing web through browser/computer use. Services, conversely, are gaining machine-readable interfaces, MCP, A2A, and APIs convenient for machine interaction.

Therefore, by "death of applications," I don't mean that browser engines, SaaS, or mobile software will disappear. Rather, the need for humans to constantly manually manage them will disappear. Today's services will gradually become infrastructure that the agent selects and uses. For example, an online store will increasingly design machine-readable interfaces for agents alongside its website and mobile app. And users will increasingly search for products and try on clothes within their agent.

At one time, the industry transitioned from Desktop First to Mobile First. The next transition will be Agent First: a service is designed not only or not so much for a person, but rather for their digital representative.

Digital World Model

But the ability to use services is not enough. A true personal agent must understand the specific person.

Knowing their name, language, and a few preferences is insufficient. It must understand the people around the user, their relationships, projects, goals, plans, obligations, purchases, trips, health, and work context. Not just remember an old email, but understand that it contained a promise to send a contract by Friday and that the contract has still not been received.

Such a structure can be called a user's digital world model — world model.

This is not an archive of all digital life nor an endless chat history. It's more like a constantly updated model of what is currently true and important: who promised what to whom, what has changed, what the user wants, what they trust, which processes are still incomplete.

Physically, it might turn out to be a graph, a combination of documents, events, vectors, and relational data, or even a new class of storage. The storage technology is less important here than the principle itself.

Therefore, the main asset of a personal agent in the future may not be the model itself, but the accumulated understanding of the person.

The New War for the Entry Point

Today, different companies know different pieces of our lives. Google knows our searches, social networks know our friends and social graph, banks know our payments, stores know our purchases, Booking.com knows our travel.

A personal agent potentially connects all of this at a single point and adds the most valuable element — intentions and future plans. Therefore, the next big war for Apple, Google, OpenAI, Meta, and other companies may not be for the best LLM, but for the right to become the default personal agent.

Until now, the most valuable entry point to the internet has been search. Google knows what a person is currently searching for, shows nearby ads, and in 2025, earned $294.7 billion from advertising. Moreover, the core of this model is built around a very limited signal — the search query and accumulated behavioral history.

A personal agent potentially knows an order of magnitude more: not only what the user is searching for, but why, what they plan to do tomorrow, how much they are willing to spend, which brands they trust, who they travel with, and what limitations are important to them. If such an agent becomes the primary intermediary between a person and digital services, control over it could prove economically more valuable than control over search or a browser.

OpenClaw, Hermes, Grok Bot, Muse, and the expanding capabilities of Codex show that the market is rapidly moving from chat interfaces to personal AI agents — systems that can be entrusted with long-term work and that maintain context between individual requests.

However, memory, tools, MCP, schedules, long-term tasks, and multi-agent orchestration are rapidly becoming an infrastructure layer. The next stage of competition will not be in the number of such capabilities, but in the agent's ability to reliably solve tasks end-to-end, understand the user, and act proactively.

When an Agent Gains the Right to Act

The world model is only half of the change. The other half is delegated authority.

As long as an agent drafts an email, it remains a tool. But if it sends emails itself, buys tickets, pays bills, and accepts service terms, we need to distinguish at least three situations: the action was done by a human; the agent prepared it, and the human confirmed; the agent acted autonomously within predefined boundaries.

A serious problem of differentiating agent rights will need to be addressed. Can it make payments, up to what amount, can these payments be reversed, who is responsible for the agent's payment?

And every significant action must leave an auditable trail: who acted, on whose behalf, what authority was granted, and was human confirmation required. A tracing of the agent's activity for critical operations must emerge; it could become as common an object as a bank transaction or an electronic signature.

New legal questions will arise from this. Who is responsible for an erroneous purchase? Can a contract concluded by two agents be challenged? When do an agent's powers cease after an employee is fired or the owner dies?

Digital inheritance will also change. Today, it mainly involves transferring access to files. In the future, a deceased person's agent could theoretically continue to pay bills, respond to emails, and settle obligations. A digital executor will be needed to determine when to stop operations, what to delete, what to transfer to heirs or companies, and what actions the agent still has the right to complete.

New Threats

The more an agent knows and can do, the more dangerous its compromise becomes.

Today, malware steals passwords or money. In the future, an attacker might try to subtly alter the worldview on which the agent bases its decisions. Remember the movie "Inception"?

For example, recording a false preference in memory: the user supposedly likes a certain service and allows automatic subscription renewal. The agent will honestly fulfill this "will" for months through small payments.

This is unpleasant. But imagine if someone altered allergy information, after which the agent independently ordered food or medication.

Even more dangerous is such an attack on the agent of an entrepreneur or official. There's no need to force them to take an obviously absurd action. It's enough to slightly alter trust in a source, event priority, or risk assessment. The agent will continue to operate normally, but the owner will find themselves in a slightly distorted digital reality.

The most dangerous attacks of the future may not look like a hack, but like the normal operation of an agent relying on a subtly corrupted world model. One of the already discussed threat classes is memory poisoning, where an attacker intentionally injects false or malicious facts into the agent's long-term memory. A separate problem is the unintentional accumulation of erroneous data in memory, where the agent gradually begins to rely on a distorted context.

Therefore, alongside agents, new protection mechanisms will inevitably appear: provenance — the origin and verifiability of knowledge, protected memory storage, immutable critical facts, independent policy engines, reliable identity, anomaly detection, and time rollback mechanisms.

How Close Is This?

Of course, everything could happen more slowly, not in 2-3 years, but in 5-10 years. Users might not want to delegate money, medicine, and legally significant actions to agents. Regulators will move slower than technology. Apple, Google, banks, and marketplaces might not want to cede the entry point to independent agents and will build their own closed ecosystems. Finally, agents themselves may remain unreliable enough for truly autonomous work for a long time.

And applications will not disappear everywhere. If the result is important to me — calling a taxi, paying a bill, buying a ticket — an agent is more convenient than an application. But in games, creativity, learning, and social scenarios, the interaction process itself is valuable.

Therefore, my prediction is not that Chrome, the App Store, or SaaS will disappear in three years.

My bet is simpler: we will stop performing a significant portion of digital tasks through interfaces ourselves.

We currently live in a world where AI is still an assistant. The next stage is Agent First: a person formulates the result, and the agent selects the tools and achieves it. AI doesn't just help — it solves tasks end-to-end. Moreover, it will be able to find and solve most tasks on its own even without user notification.

And beyond that, an Agent Native world is possible, where services are initially designed for interaction with agents, and the separate UI of services becomes one of the additional, optional surfaces.

Once, we ourselves visited dozens of websites in search of information. Then search engines became a single entry point to the internet. Today, increasingly, instead of a list of links, we simply ask an AI a question and get a ready answer. The next step seems natural: not only to stop searching for information manually, but also to stop manually opening applications, deciphering interfaces, and performing sequences of actions within them — this work will be taken over by a personal agent.

If this happens, the main asset of the next stage of the internet will not be another LLM or even an agent runtime. It will be the human's digital world model — and the trust to act based on it.
