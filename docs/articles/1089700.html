Back in early 2025, I started noticing how people, when using AI, would put their brains into passive mode.1 They'd ask the AI to perform a task (summarize text, write code, etc.), accepting the result at face value.2 At the time, this approach worked very poorly, and the results were often nonsensical.

But AI was developing, and I began to notice this behavior more and more often. Sometimes people would just ask the AI to write them code, which they would unconditionally accept as working.3 In other cases, a human was involved in the loop, monitoring the result and asking the AI to fix problems if they arose. Niklas Gruhn even coined a term for such people – meat proxy.4

Today, these meat proxies work better than in early 2025, and the software created this way is, in a sense, functional. It's hard to call it successful, of course, and it doesn't inspire a desire to use it. But I'm still impressed by how effective these proxies have become by September 2026. One can even imagine that in the foreseeable future, AI will develop to such an extent that with this development method, they will start creating software of medium quality. Or even better – perhaps AI will advance so much that it learns to create excellent software without human involvement at all.

Suppose that happens. Then what's the point of companies hiring meat proxies at all? They can just run AI in a loop and fire unnecessary employees. So, for the employees themselves, this clearly bodes no good.5

2. Luke Barton commented on this:

“I think the ability to work in this mode says more about the nature of the work being done than the workers themselves realize. Personally, I'm willing to give up solving a task independently and hand it over to AI only if it has no particular value and its failure won't affect anything.

When it comes to serious tasks, the probability of the model successfully solving them on the first try is much lower. In this case, I need to take on the role of tester, technical manager, and architect. Such work in a while loop (until it works) feels like a crunch. I have a persistent feeling that I've missed something, and that a bad prompt could lead to an architectural error that will require everything to be redone later.

I also notice that the accelerated pace of work forces me to raise my own bar for the volume of tasks performed. If before I could release an MVP and then gradually refine it, now I have agents that bring the product to completion and solve edge cases much faster. But without a proper prompt, they are of little use.

Perhaps this will lead people to unpleasant reflections, but I have a couple of questions for those meat proxies whose agents handle everything so easily: 'Don't you think you weren't trying very hard before?' and 'Why aren't you giving the agents more complex tasks?'

For example, in our work, we solved a task that you would consider suitable for full automation with AI – we were porting [redacted] to build with Bazel. And even with agents, it took several months to implement. It involves a huge number of subtle and hard-to-define requirements, the solution of which with agents requires constant supervision. The level at which you can simply ask them 'Port this to Bazel' and calmly go about your business has yet to be reached. Perhaps it will take months, years, or even an eternity. There are too many decision points and too many unknown unknowns in this process.

How often do you find yourself in a scenario where you encounter some code, the principle of which is extremely difficult to understand, but further actions depend on this understanding? There can be many variations. Perhaps it's related to development processes, or you doubt whether clients have started using this function. How will you solve the problem through AI in this case?

Or another example. Suppose you are reviewing your work with a stakeholder who suddenly says, 'Oh, why is this here? This is an unnecessary feature, we haven't used it for a long time.' And what decision was made here based on a false assumption about the necessity of this element?”

The moment when the need for decision-making becomes more apparent is when the agent encounters a task for which there was no information in the training set. In a mild form, this manifested when we compared how well agents can use different programming languages. They performed much worse with less common languages, on which they were trained significantly less than on popular ones. A more successful example would be trying to play a board game with AI (especially a modern one, not a classic like chess or Go). In general, if you take a game like "Lost Cities" or "Dominion," a state-of-the-art (SOTA) model with the necessary software support will perform worse than a person who has a general understanding of board games but has never played this particular one.

If you ask an agent about this game, it will turn out that it knows a lot about it and can make statements that might seem reasonable to an uninformed person. However, to any knowledgeable player, all these recommendations will seem like complete nonsense full of errors. I recently played Dominion with a beginner who thought ChatGPT would help him understand the game and its mechanics. Honestly, I was skeptical about this idea and assumed the opposite would happen (judging by my observations, that's what happened).

After playing a couple of games with the opponent, I looked at what ChatGPT was specifically advising him. Not all advice was wrong, but the incorrect part of it led the player into much worse situations than an average person who plays board games well and is familiar with general game mechanics would have gotten into. By the way, there is a lot of open information online. So even a person with no gaming experience at all, who decides to spend five hours thoroughly familiarizing themselves with the game or even just reading about it beforehand (30 minutes with permission to consult hints during the game), could easily enter the top 1%. This, of course, kills all the fun, and I wouldn't recommend doing that. But considering that agents can already perform searches, query APIs, and so on, it shows their current lag behind humans in tasks that go beyond the training set. As far as I know, the situation will change drastically in the next generation of models, but today this gap is still large.

Be that as it may, my point is that even when solving programming tasks, you often encounter non-standard issues that an AI agent will handle worse than a simply reasonable person. If you want to achieve high results today, you need to be able to notice such situations and deal with them yourself. ↩

When I try software from people who delegated the entire thought process to AI during its creation, I find serious problems in it. I've been told repeatedly that this approach works. However, I definitely cannot call the software from such developers compliant with the standard I spoke about here.

Let's take a funny example. One reputable programmer stated on Twitter that the issue of programming is closed – he tried implementing all sorts of projects with Claude, and the agent performed no worse than an expert. As another absurd example, I'll give a case where a person, following a standard user scenario (of a real commercial product), got into a deadlock. Technically, it was possible to get out of this deadlock – most programmers would probably figure it out. But an average user, for whom the software was intended, would likely get stuck and wouldn't be able to reach the functions they needed.

I also create a lot of software for myself, and its quality is also quite conditional – if you consider it from the perspective of a commercial product, I would say that it essentially doesn't work. Therefore, I don't think such software is necessarily bad. For example, I once talked about a regular expression engine that an agent wrote for me to speed up searching through ripgrep on a PC. In a similar way, I created a Rust interpreter to speed up agent iterations in some projects. I also mentioned that I like to run an agent in a loop when analyzing data – it produces incorrect results, and I tell it what and how to fix.

However, there is a huge difference between these approaches. It's one thing to create software for your narrow tasks, knowing perfectly well that it will only work with the right approach, or to generate deliberately raw results that you then manually refine yourself. And it's another thing to write a bunch of non-working junk and on that basis claim that programming has become trivial. Or, worse, to push subpar creations into real commercial products.

It seems to me that anyone who treats software the same way Thomas and Gary do has been feeling this way for a long time. For a while, I even wondered: maybe people who claim colossal productivity gains through AI manage to get much more benefit from it than all my acquaintances? But, as we've already discussed, as new evidence emerges, I become more and more convinced that people are simply deceiving themselves.

In the board game example, I like that you can clearly measure how good the resulting AI is. In some extreme case, you might encounter a situation like rock-paper-scissors, where you observe something like A > B > C > A. If the code is just typical AI nonsense, it becomes obvious on an objective level.

The same applies to commercial software. You can always either talk to the company's employees or look at the data yourself, which will show that conversion has dropped, customers are leaving, user dissatisfaction is off the charts, etc.

Here's another funny example, this time not about software. Once, a reader decided to fact-check one of my articles using ChatGPT and sent me the "exposing" results along with a sarcastic comment about errors. But the point is, I had already fact-checked this post through ChatGPT myself and corrected all the real errors. So, only the AI's own errors remained in the submitted list.

In general, I've noticed that in such tasks (and when analyzing any content, not just mine), the number of false positives is very high, meaning the AI's accuracy leaves much to be desired. At the same time, it misses errors quite often, meaning its recall is very mediocre. But if you engage your brain even a little in the process, it's still worth checking facts like this. After all, quickly skimming through a list with many false errors is much "cheaper" for me than asking another person to proofread the draft.

And one more idiotic example. Recently, tpatcek raised an interesting topic. He writes that you shouldn't fall for the flattery of AI, which praises your work. In that case, it was specifically about writing texts, but it's true for other activities as well.

If you share Gary's or Halvarflake's attitude, then it's self-evident. However, some people don't want to know this. I think you have to really try hard not to notice it, but I often see people proving the greatness of their work by the fact that AI praised it.

Perhaps a day will come when AI's words, stating that your work is brilliant, or that your arguments completely dismantle your opponent's points, will carry real weight. But today, we are still too far from that – at least in terms of accuracy, it's harder to say about time.

Therefore, if someone tries to prove the quality of their work or the weight of their arguments based on AI's praise, it is primarily a sign that the person has turned off their brain, and a strong signal that the work or arguments themselves are actually worthless. ↩

Another argument might sound something like this: "We'll all be out of a job soon, so why just give up?" I personally look at it from the opposite side. You can only reason this way if you are sure that this moment is literally upon us. If you are financially ready to retire, you can safely turn off your brain. Although, frankly, you could have always done this, and there have always been people who did a half-assed job. If you're not ready for retirement yet, it's more logical to do something that will bring you more money, and you're unlikely to achieve that with your head turned off.

If the future scenario is one where AI won't replace us, then there's no great need to chase earnings. But if you sincerely believe that total automation is just around the corner and you urgently need to earn money, then now is the time to push harder and do the exact opposite – squeeze the maximum out of your brain.

There is another perspective: "Why bother and do the job properly if you won't be paid more for it anyway?" I can't agree with that either. I myself, and my friends, have constantly received promotions and bonuses precisely for putting in the effort, finding problems, and solving them. The exception might be some toxic and destructive workplace where good work is not rewarded. But in such a case, people simply quit and go where they are valued.

Another argument is possible: "Due to the inertia of large companies, there will be a good opportunity to smoothly integrate into the role of a meat proxy after LLMs become good enough to replace programmers."

But if you look at things realistically and consider how enthusiastically companies are now laying everyone off, this argument seems to be the opposite of the truth. If your goal is to work as little as possible, then the best time for that has already passed. Haven't you discussed such topics with people working in large companies? If so, you've surely heard stories about employees who don't show up for work at all (and don't even work remotely), and it takes months or even years to fire them. In the last couple of years, I've heard such tales less often, but I know a firsthand case – one of my teammates did this. As far as I remember, it took the company six months to fire him after he decided to retire and realized he could get a few more salaries by simply stopping coming to the office. This is a pre-COVID story from a company where remote work wasn't even an option.

And a friend of mine told an even funnier story. He worked at another company, and it took the company a whole two years to fire his colleague, who pulled the same trick.

Moreover, for quite a long time, no one even started the firing process. Then followed a leisurely procedure of issuing increasingly strict warnings, after which the person was removed from staff. According to my friend, their manager later admitted that if that guy had wanted to cheat the system even more, i.e., simply started coming in and showing some activity, the countdown would have started anew.

If he had been a professional slacker, under the conditions at the time, he could have kept his job indefinitely. Personally, I don't understand how companies could be in such a state. Although now they are already actively moving away from it, using AI as an excellent excuse. Because of this, the current time, and likely the near future, will clearly be the worst period for those who wish to stay employed without making much effort and without bringing any benefit. Undoubtedly, there will be individual companies where such behavior can be overlooked. But if your goal is to do nothing and just get easy money, then you're a bit late. You could have successfully run this scheme for years when conditions were much milder than what awaits us in the near future (unless you shouldn't have gotten greedy and stopped coming to work altogether). ↩
