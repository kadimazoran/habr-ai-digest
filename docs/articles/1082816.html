+240% Commits, +30% Releases: Agents Hit the SDLC Wall

A recent NBER study estimates the cumulative effect of AI tools: up to 240% more commits, but only 30% more releases. Meanwhile, LinkedIn had to build a separate platform for AI code review, and developer surveys show a shift from writing code to verifying results. This article explores why generation speed is no longer the main bottleneck and what kind of engineering system is needed around agents: specifications, shared context, constraints, checks, and metrics all the way to release. In September, NBER published a paper on the impact of AI tools on development. The authors compared the public activity of over 500,000 developers on GitHub with telemetry from these tools. For users of autonomous agents, the estimated cumulative effect on commit count reached 240%. At the project level, it was 80%, and at the release level, 30%.

The gap is more significant than the headline number. An agent can write code quickly, but the change still needs to be reviewed, integrated into the system, and released. As AI coding agents become more widespread, the entire software development and delivery lifecycle (SDLC) becomes the bottleneck.

240% at the Bottom, 30% at the Top

The NBER paper covers several generations of AI tools. As the transition moved from autocompletion to interactive and autonomous agents, the estimated effect on commit count grew. Higher up the production chain, the increase quickly diminished.

These numbers should not be interpreted as a direct conversion of one task group to another. The authors did not separately measure releases from autonomous agents. They estimated the cumulative effect of different tool generations at various levels. The graph effectively illustrates the scale of the gap but not the causal link between a specific commit and a release.

The authors explain the tapering off with the weak link hypothesis. The speed of the entire chain is limited by the stage that is hardest to accelerate. In their model, the AI-to-human labor substitution rate is 0.23. A low value indicates that human and tool work are highly interdependent. The agent writes code faster, but the decisions before and after implementation remain with humans.

The study has its limitations. For starters, it's observational. AI users were compared to developers with similar past activity in the same calendar week of the previous year. Some telemetry is private, and public GitHub poorly represents internal company development. Two of the authors previously worked at Microsoft and are now consulting the company.

Despite all caveats, I appreciate the choice of the top-level metric. The authors looked at projects with releases, not just commit counts. A commit describes the value of development about as well as the number of parts on an assembly line describes car production. If assembly and quality control can't keep up, parts will increase, but finished cars will be few.

The Queue is Already Visible in Reviews

LinkedIn encountered a similar effect internally. Along with the volume of code from agents, the time to the first human review increased for the slowest ten percent of requests. The company built its own code review system. In a typical week, it handles over 79,000 reviews for approximately 40,000 merge requests across 7,500 repositories.

Such a system now needs to be maintained as an internal platform. It includes company and specific repository rules, monitoring, target processing times, gradual rollout of new models, and quality tests before updates. The usefulness of a comment is judged by whether the recommendation was incorporated into the final code. A 'like' on a comment is too cheap a metric for this.

This is the experience of one large company, and the article was written by the system's authors. It doesn't offer a universal solution. However, it clearly shows the new scope of work around agents. Simply connecting a model to a repository isn't enough. Rules, quality control, and outcome measurement after review are necessary.

Work Has Shifted from Writing to Reviewing

A longitudinal study of developers helps understand what humans are now doing. The authors conducted two surveys six months apart. In the first wave, 158 suitable participants remained; in the second, 101, with a comparable sample of 95 people.

82% of surveyed AI users reported spending less time writing code. A general shift from creation to verification was statistically significant. An increase in each specific type of verification work could not be confirmed. The authors termed this new set of activities "supervisory engineering." Humans direct the agent, evaluate its output, and fix errors.

The survey measures participants' perceptions, and the data were collected in 2024 and 2025. Tools have evolved since then. However, the result aligns with the main limitation identified in the NBER paper: code generation is accelerating faster than human review.

The DORA report in its 2025 study, based on responses from nearly five thousand specialists, showed a similar trend. A 25% increase in AI usage was associated with a 3.1% acceleration in code review. However, delivery throughput decreased by 1.5%, and stability by 7.2%.

DORA also used surveys and statistical modeling, so causality is not proven here. The authors hypothesize that AI increases the size of changes. Code appears faster, batches grow, large changes take longer to review and break more often. The old advice to release in small batches has thus become even more relevant.

There's also an inconvenient counterexample. In a randomized experiment by METR, 16 experienced open-source developers completed 246 real tasks. Using early 2025 AI tools, they worked 19% slower, although they themselves felt they had sped up. A later METR experiment showed a weak acceleration signal, but the researchers deemed the estimate unreliable due to participant selection and parallel work with agents.

The cautious conclusion is that acceleration depends on tasks, codebase, human experience, and the delivery process. A confident signal exists only at the problem class level: code generation is changing faster than other development stages.

The Agent Becomes Part of the Production System

A single developer can manually invoke an agent and review every change. A significant portion of control remains in their head. They remember architectural decisions, know about flaky tests, and spot suspicious code snippets.

With multiple autonomous agents, this approach ceases to scale. They open merge requests in parallel and make hundreds of small decisions, and a human can no longer review the entire flow with the same thoroughness. Implicit knowledge and personal discipline are no longer sufficient to maintain the system.

Atlassian describes a similar shift from the process management perspective. In a survey of over 1100 engineers and development managers, 94% of managers reported that their organizations use AI. 74% saw an acceleration in code generation, but 78% of teams continue to review the increased volume with standard peer reviews.

According to the same survey, 88% of managers believe a managed engineering system for AI is necessary. 19% have built one. Another 6% reported formal and widespread AI adoption as a standard in many parts of the SDLC. The criteria for the latter figure are stricter, so they cannot be directly compared.

Subsequently, Atlassian announced governed agent loops, shared code context, centralized standards, automated task assignment, change verification, and metrics. At the time of the announcement, some features were in open beta, and others were available by invitation.

The company sells this infrastructure, so its survey and announcement do not prove the solution's effectiveness. They merely confirm that the market is already investing in a separate layer for managing agent work.

What Should Surround the Agent

The technical wrapper for an agent is often reduced to a file with instructions. For a personal tool, this may suffice. A stream of autonomous changes requires a system composed of several parts.

Described Task

The agent needs expected behavior, constraints, error scenarios, and acceptance criteria. Without them, it will quickly write code for a misunderstood task.

Shared Context

Architectural decisions, product rules, and inter-repository dependencies must be accessible to both humans and agents. This context will need to be versioned, updated, and segmented by access levels. A neglected documentation folder won't make a model smarter.

Execution Boundary

The team decides in advance which repositories, data, and external systems the agent can modify independently. As autonomy increases, minimal privileges, an isolated environment, cost limits, and stop conditions are added.

Checks

Linters, static analysis tools, tests, security checks, and architectural constraints are best moved out of human memory. AI-assisted verification adds another signal. Proving the correctness of AI-generated code with another AI's verdict is still risky.

Decision History

It's necessary to preserve the task author, the context provided to the agent, the changes made, the results of checks, and the reason for stopping. After a failed release, without these records, one would have to dig through chats and logs.

Post-Release Feedback

Canary releases, rollbacks, incidents, and manual fixes should feed back into specifications, rules, and test suites. Otherwise, the system will learn to pass its own checks even when it's detrimental to the product.

Metrics for the New Bottleneck

Agent speed says little about delivery speed. It's more useful to measure losses between stages.

  1. Agent accepted the task.

  2. A merge request was created.

  3. Automated checks passed.

  4. The change was accepted after review.

  5. The change was included in a release.

  6. The release operated without rollback or subsequent fixes.

For each transition, three types of data are sufficient: the proportion of changes that passed, waiting time, and human hours spent on rework.

As we move from task to stable operation, some changes are filtered out, and waiting and human rework accumulate at the transitions.

These metrics are suitable for identifying bottlenecks, not for ranking developers. Instant merge requests that wait days for review indicate an overloaded review process. Fast reviews followed by a two-week release queue suggest fixing the release process, while frequent rollbacks mean speed was bought at the cost of risk.

For an initial experiment, one repeatable task class is sufficient. First, record the current path to release, limit change size, and add automated checks. Then, for several weeks, track released changes without subsequent rework. Autonomy can be increased when the system can stably handle the current flow.
