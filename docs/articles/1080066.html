The ML automation market in Russia is experiencing a rebirth. The first was classic ML: decision trees, CatBoost, XGBoost, scoring models. In that era, "ML for business" meant a neat pipeline on tabular data with a data scientist spending weeks tuning hyperparameters. The second rebirth occurred thanks to LLMs in agent mode, and these births are proving no easier than the first.

The fact is that we are lagging behind the world by an average of 1–1.5 years, and this lag is uneven: in some niches the gap is smaller, in others it is larger. The unevenness of the lag creates the strange picture that almost every integrator sees in the market now: the industry offers the client yesterday's architecture, and the client, having read recent reviews, is already asking for tomorrow's.

Typical Request vs. Typical Offer

A client comes with a request like: "We want an AI automation platform, build it for us, and then we'll launch models ourselves, replace them with newer ones, and so on." Essentially, the client is asking for ML automation with minimal effort, without constant dependence on a contractor.

And in response, the market almost always offers one of two things: either a boxed solution on top of a cloud model via subscription (most often wrapped in an interface for Claude or another external model via API), or a powerful server with one large model deployed locally.

These two options have different architectures at first glance, but in reality, they share the same logical error: in both cases, businesses are being sold a single universal "brain" that is supposed to equally handle all company tasks, from contact center calls to legal contract analysis.

Previously, a client representative would learn about new technologies from the same contractor who sold it to them; the information asymmetry was on the seller's side, and it was easy to sell the same monolith under different guises: write the core once, then only change the interface and branding. The classic software business model: write once, sell many times.

Now, the client representative opens a chatbot and finds out in five minutes what RAG is, how it differs from fine-tuning, and why a model needs to be retrained specifically for their data and processes. The asymmetry has disappeared. As a result, a phrase is heard in meetings that breaks the integrator's business model:

"We don't need one big model for all processes; we need each task to be solved excellently, and we want to launch models ourselves, replacing them with newer ones as they become available."

And a separate task, retrained for a specific company's data, is by definition not something that can be replicated in the next project without rework.

It's worth clarifying here: the demand for ready-made AI tools is not decreasing quantitatively. According to Gartner forecasts, the share of enterprise applications with built-in AI agents is expected to grow to 40% by the end of 2026, up from less than 5% a year earlier, and a significant portion of this growth comes through low-code/no-code tools simply because there is a shortage of engineers capable of building production-grade agent systems from scratch.

Low-code partially addresses the talent shortage in scenario assembly but is powerless where it hits the quality of the models themselves.

Here, it's important to distinguish between two different phenomena that cause confusion:

  • Orchestration Layer (integrations, webhooks, HTTP tools, routing calls between systems) is growing and not going away, because it solves the problem of system integration, not model quality.

  • The idea of a single large monolithic model as the source of intelligence for all tasks is dying, because the client now knows for sure: without specialization for a specific task and specific data, the result will be mediocre across the board.

If the core of the solution is calling a single cloud model via API for all cases, then for Russian businesses, this almost always runs into Federal Law 152 (On Personal Data), and several requirements of different natures at once.

First, localization (Part 5, Article 18): from July 1, 2025, after amendments by Federal Law 23, the initial collection and recording of personal data of Russians through foreign databases is directly prohibited.

Second, the basis for processing and a mandate agreement (Article 6): if your data is processed by a cloud service, SaaS, or model at your instruction, a mandate agreement must be executed with the processor, and without it, the mere transfer of data to the processor will be a violation, even before any leak.

Third, a separate and often overlooked requirement is to notify Roskomnadzor about cross-border transfer of personal data before it begins (Article 12), if the processor is physically located outside the Russian Federation. This procedure is not covered by localization or a mandate agreement.

Western cloud services, in most cases, do not allow for fulfilling either the second or third requirement in principle (hereinafter, a popular explanation of the law's logic, not legal advice).

Hence the exception, known to experienced integrators: if the system contains non-anonymized personal data, it is only possible to work within the Russian segment, using either a Russian cloud with a properly executed mandate agreement (Yandex Cloud, Sber AI, VK Cloud, etc.), or one's own hardware on the territory of the Russian Federation.

Open-source models deployed in a Russian cloud with properly formalized relations with the provider meet the legal requirements, while a direct call to a foreign cloud API for real personal data without a local perimeter is a stable source of legal risk.

Even if you don't have personal data, constant use of cloud models leads to commercial information leaks. Within a short period necessary for retraining, a frontier model will successfully recommend your approaches to a competitor who asks questions in the chat. And this is not an empty threat.

The second option for the proposed system looks like this: buy a powerful GPU server and deploy one model of 200–400B parameters locally as is, without retraining, assuming it will equally handle any company task. The problem is that general-purpose models are optimized to be good enough at everything, not excellent at one thing, and businesses almost always need the latter.

An academic cost-benefit analysis of deploying LLMs on one's own hardware shows that large open-weight models like Qwen3-235B require a cluster costing over $200,000, and although technically such models approach the quality of top closed-source analogs, this comes with significantly increased operational complexity, not a ready-made "out-of-the-box" result.

The operation of open models in an enterprise environment describes a typical evolutionary curve: the path goes from prompt engineering through RAG to parameter-efficient fine-tuning and then to full continued pre-training for the domain, meaning deploying a model "as is" is the initial, not the final, point.

A broader review of real production agent systems shows that where teams manage without retraining (70% of 20 documented cases), it's almost always cloud APIs of top foreign models, not self-hosted local giants of 200–400B. This means the mass successful "no retraining" scenario is someone else's cloud infrastructure, not one's own expensive cluster for the same undertrained generalist.

From Monolith to Microservices

A useful analogy is what has already happened with web development. Twenty years ago, a typical enterprise application was a monolith: one large codebase, one database, one point of failure; any change required rebuilding and redeploying the entire thing.

The industry moved to microservices not out of a love for complexity, but because different parts of the system have different requirements for load, access, updates, and specialization, and it's more convenient to keep them as separate services communicating via API than to try to cram everything into one codebase.

Exactly the same thing is happening with ML now. Instead of one monolithic model (cloud or local) that must equally handle all company tasks, businesses are moving towards an ensemble of narrowly specialized models and agents (ranging from 1B to 80B), each tailored to its specific task, data, and access requirements, all communicating with each other via API, orchestrated at the workflow layer.

Let's break down typical AI automation tasks and show that for each of them, the principle holds: a narrowly specialized component, retrained for specific data and a specific task type, outperforms a general-purpose model, even a very large one.

RAG

RAG for the legal department and internal corporate RAG are two different systems in terms of structure, content, and access.

The internal corporate knowledge base (regulations, HR policies, technical documentation) provides broad coverage, low accuracy requirements at the individual item level, and access for most employees.

RAG for the legal department provides narrow coverage (contracts, legal opinions, court practice), critical accuracy requirements at the level of a specific clause or contract article, and access must be strictly limited, as legal privilege and counterparty confidentiality do not allow these documents to be kept in a single general index with the corporate wiki.

Research confirms that for legal tasks, accuracy at the individual contract clause level becomes the bottleneck: a benchmark for evaluating LLMs in identifying legal risks at the contract article level shows that open models require targeted retraining precisely because accuracy significantly drops on long or rarely encountered clause formulations. This is a problem that simply doesn't exist in a general corporate knowledge base, as it doesn't have such a concentration of legally significant details per text item.

A separate study directly shows a practical trade-off: top cloud models are accurate in legal tasks, but when deployed at scale (processing a flow of contracts), their cost, latency, and data control requirements reduce practical benefits compared to a compact model retrained specifically for structured extraction from contracts. In other words, legal RAG/extraction requires a separate specialized component, not the same engine that powers search on a general RAG wiki.

In addition to the difference between legal and corporate RAG discussed above, there is a general picture for all RAG varieties. Work comparing fine-tuning and retrieval for knowledge integration shows that retraining effectively complements RAG, bringing almost as much accuracy as RAG itself.

This means RAG is a "reference book" for precise fact retrieval, while retraining is "learned knowledge" for reproducing the format, style, and specific structure of the answer.

Each specific RAG context (legal, accounting, HR, technical support) is a separate microservice with its own set of sources, access policy, and its own degree of retraining on top of retrieval, not a single common index for the entire company.

IDP

Document recognition in accounting and in the legal department are different tasks by nature. In accounting, the main flow consists of invoices, acts, and UTDs with tabular parts: this requires a model tailored for precise extraction of table rows (item, quantity, price, VAT) from documents of a sufficiently standard format. This is closer to the task of structured table extraction, where prompt engineering on a general model for clean documents or retrained LLM pipelines for low-quality scans and non-standard layouts perform well.

In the legal department, multi-page contracts of arbitrary structure are used, where the quality of recognition and analysis of each specific clause is important: has the document retained the legally significant wording, has a reservation on the fifteenth page been lost, has the date and party to the obligation been correctly extracted.

This is a fundamentally different quality metric, and it is confirmed by research: retraining a model on legal documents significantly reduces the rate of hallucinations, particularly in the extraction of named entities (compensation amounts, disability percentages, case parties) from legal texts.

Incoming Calls and Voice Agents

Here, the gap between "out-of-the-box" and "retrained" is perhaps the most measurable. In a recent paper on adapting NVIDIA Canary speech recognition models for contact center tasks, retraining on real phone recordings reduced the character error rate from 23.31% to 9.04% on noisy phone audio recordings, and for business-critical entities, names, and addresses: from 16.98% to 3.78%. For a voice bot, this is the difference between "understood the delivery address the first time" and "didn't understand and asked again for the third time, the client hung up.".

A similar story applies to the agent's communication style: retraining a model on simulated dialogues with a sample of just 100 examples consistently surpassed attempts to achieve the same through system prompts in terms of tonal naturalness. Prompt engineering in a general interface simply does not provide the same control over style as tuning the weights of a specific speech microservice.

Ticket Routing and Appeal Classification

In the LoRA Land study, 310 retrained models were tested on 31 tasks, showing superiority over GPT-4 in 25 out of 31 cases, with an average accuracy increase of 10 points. An independent study compared GPT-3.5/GPT-4 and Claude Opus in zero-shot mode with smaller retrained models on sentiment classification, approval/disapproval, emotions, and political stances, and retraining with application-specific data won in all cases.

A small retrained model almost always outperforms a huge general-purpose model on a specific classification task.

For routing tickets in a service desk or directing appeals in a chatbot, this means: if you have 40–200 ticket categories with their own specific terminology, a base "out-of-the-box" model will get confused, and prompt engineering cannot fully solve this problem.

Meetings and Minutes

Automatic summarization of dialogues and meetings is a particularly illustrative area precisely because basic "out-of-the-box" generation regularly lies about facts, even in top models.

A whole layer of research over the past two years has been dedicated to detecting and correcting such errors in dialogue and meeting summarization, from iterative refinement through model feedback to structured frameworks for evaluating hierarchical errors in dialogue summarization.

A meeting minutes service is also a separate microservice that requires its own fact verification layer on top of raw summarization, otherwise, some minutes will contain plausible-sounding but inaccurate decision formulations.

Document Recognition

Returning to the accounting example: typical, well-structured invoices can be handled with prompts on a general-purpose model. One recent study on extracting data from electricity bills showed that general-purpose models with a well-designed prompt can achieve high-accuracy field extraction without any retraining.

However, this applies to the simplest, most typical documents like invoices. As soon as it comes to large documents, non-standard layouts, or low scan quality, the recognition accuracy of general models without retraining drops.

Why Can't One Model Handle All These Tasks?

It's not about the greed of monolithic solution sellers, but about architecture. One model, whether a cloud subscription or a giant local one, operates at the level of a general API: you control the prompt, temperature, and list of tools.

You cannot simultaneously provide strict access control and high accuracy at the contract clause level for legal RAG, optimization for tabular parts for accounting recognition, optimization for low CER/WER on noisy telephony for a voice agent, and accuracy on one and a half hundred narrow categories for a ticket classifier through a single universal interface.

Each of these requirements pulls model optimization in its own direction, and the same model cannot be simultaneously optimal across all these axes, just as the same web microservice cannot be optimal for both payment processing and full-text search.

Universal frontier models are excellent for the MVP stage and complex logical tasks, as well as for writing truly complex code (e.g., drivers), but in the unit economics and quality optimization phase, specialized microservices become inevitable.

Where Is Everything Heading?

The ML automation market is undergoing the same evolution that web development went through two decades ago, from monoliths to microservices. The industry is now making the same mistake that early web developers made, trying to scale a monolith instead of breaking it into services: trying to sell the client one model (cloud or local) as a universal solution, lagging 1–1.5 years behind the realization of this fact.

The real architecture that the market is slowly but surely moving towards has two layers:

  • Orchestration Layer (integrations, webhooks, HTTP tools, routing calls between systems and between ML microservices themselves): here, low-code tools like n8n are genuinely good and not going away, because this is an integration task, not a model quality task.

  • Specialized ML Microservices Layer (ASR for noisy telephony, ticket classifier, RAG for legal department separate from corporate RAG, accounting document extractor separate from contract analyzer, meeting minutes summarizer with fact verification): each component is retrained for its narrow task and the specific company's data, communicates with others via API, and cannot be bought once and sold to a hundred other clients without modification, because each client's data and requirements are unique.

However, the approach with specialized models has a downside worth mentioning. It's the high MLOps tax. Integrators can no longer sell a boxed platform on a "write once and replicate a hundred times" scheme.

Each implementation turns into service consulting with data labeling, dataset assembly, and manual retraining. Licenses are not sold, but engineer hours are.

For the client, this is also a challenge: instead of one convenient button, they get a zoo of several ML microservices under the hood with their own hardware and maintenance requirements.

As a result, businesses face a fundamental fork in the road:

  • The Illusion of "Low-Code on Your Own": saves budget on engineers and allows for quick assembly, but hits the quality ceiling of universal "out-of-the-box" models.

  • Specialized ML Microservices Architecture: provides the necessary accuracy and security, but requires paying the "MLOps tax.".

If a company does not have its own staff of data scientists and MLOps engineers, the hope of "buying a low-code platform and handing it over to business analysts" will not work for high-accuracy tasks.

Low-code is well-suited for the orchestration layer, but maintaining quality, validating datasets, and monitoring the drift of specialized models will inevitably require a transition to long-term outsourcing from the integrator or maintenance by one's own ML team.

Conclusion

For the business model of integrators, what is happening in the market means the end of schemes like "wrote the core once and sold it a hundred times," but not the end of the ML automation market as such.

The unit of sale is simply changing: instead of a license for one universal model, it's the assembly and retraining of specific ML microservices for the client's specific data, on top of an orchestration wrapper that the client can and wants to operate themselves.

For clients, the option "let's take a low-code platform and launch various new models as is ourselves" is also losing its meaning, because low-code tools only solve the problem of system integration and orchestration, but cannot ensure the quality of the models themselves.

Any universal model launched "out-of-the-box" without targeted adaptation to the company's context (whether advanced Few-Shot/RAG pipelines, DSPy, or fine-tuning for specific data) will inevitably yield only mediocre results on narrow tasks.

In the end, clients are moving towards a two-level architecture: management of the orchestration layer (integrations, scenarios, webhooks) is transferred to internal company operations, while the development and fine-tuning of highly specialized ML microservices are carried out for specific data and business tasks (from legal RAG and accounting OCR to classifiers and voice agents).
