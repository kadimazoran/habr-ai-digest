How the Socio-Economic Order is Collapsing

Let's first consider what a socio-economic order is, and how such systems have historically collapsed. Instead of the word "order," we can use "formation" or "structure" – the meaning won't change much.

A socio-economic order is a structure of relationships between participants in economic activity. This structure is built and self-reproduces internally according to certain patterns – in accordance with logical, physical, and technological constraints, traditions, legal laws, and so on. Let's consider a specific structure from medieval Europe – a subsistence economy with a feudal system of social relations. The example I will use next is taken from the recently published book by Russian economist Mikhail Khazin, "The Last Crisis of Capitalism."

Imagine a small fiefdom consisting of three medium-sized villages. In each village, there is a cartwright who makes 4 carts per year for his neighbors under an agreement where, while he makes the cart, they feed him and his family. This is a highly simplified model of a medieval village barter economy, but it's very convenient for examining the processes involved.

For the villagers and the feudal lord to live better and more prosperously, the labor productivity needs to increase. Increased productivity is achieved only through the division of labor and specialization. Suppose the three craftsmen from the three villages unite and divide their duties: one produces wheels, the second axles, and the third bodies, and then together they assemble all the parts. The division of labor and specialization helped optimize the time and resource costs of production, and now, together, they produce not 12 carts as before, but, let's say, 15. On the one hand, this is an excellent result, but on the other hand, it creates many problems.

The first problem is that production has become much more fragile: if one of the craftsmen falls ill, dies, gets drunk, or sells his product to someone else as spare parts, not a single complete cart will be produced, and none of the craftsmen will receive payment. This either requires the feudal lord to coordinate cart production, as if he had nothing else to do, or to build a new system of socio-economic relations with the introduction of the concept of a "company," where the cartwrights become employees and co-owners. This implies a complete change of formation, as the existence of companies is incompatible with both subsistence farming and feudalism: a system of finance, property, and arbitration courts independent of feudal lords is required, since companies can have trade relations with residents of neighboring fiefdoms and so on. Any socio-economic structure possesses structural stability and resists change.

The second problem is that the new carts need a market. Even if the feudal lord takes charge of coordinating cart production and starts receiving 3 extra carts per year, what will he do with them? His villagers don't need more carts – the domestic market is limited. He can't sell carts to neighboring fiefdoms – they have their own cartwrights, and local feudal lords want to retain the production of strategically important means of transport within their own domains to avoid becoming dependent on someone who could inflate prices or impose sanctions on cart supplies at a critical moment, which occurred every couple of years in the Middle Ages. He can't sell carts to distant lands either – the cost of transportation would quickly exceed the cost of the cart itself. Thus, the only way our feudal lord can expand his market is by force – wars for markets occurred regularly and still do: for example, in 1991, the collapse of the Soviet economic bloc led to a major redistribution of markets in favor of the USA. But let's return to our feudal lords and carts.

Imagine a large settlement of thirty villages appears somewhere. The creation of a unified cart production allows for a deeper division of labor through extremely narrow specialization of each craftsman on individual small cart parts, optimization and automation of many processes, which will allow producing not 120 carts per year, or even 150, but, for example, 240 carts per year. This allows for such a significant reduction in the cost of each individual cart that it becomes unprofitable for each individual feudal lord to maintain their own cartwrights – villagers will prefer to buy a cart from a large production facility at half the price of a local craftsman. Furthermore, the plague that swept through Europe, destroying a large part of the population, led to an increase in labor costs, making it expensive to maintain cartwrights in every village. However, the existence of a large cart production with a huge number of employees and a complex ownership structure implies the emergence of a completely new system of social and economic relations between people. Such a system began to appear in Northwestern Europe around the 15th-16th centuries.

In this system, new basic entities exist, new types of relationships between these entities, and its own economic laws operate. The new structure is built on corporations, markets, and exchanges, trade and production chains – all of which are radically different from the medieval subsistence-feudal economic structure. In the new structure, there is a complete departure from barter and a transition to exclusively monetary relations, which allows for capital accumulation. This feature gives the structure its name – capitalism. Within capitalism, there is a huge amount of space for technological growth, automation, and production optimization. Furthermore, the loss of jobs by cartwrights allows for a reduction in labor costs and the use of freed-up human resources for a deeper division of labor in other areas.

The bubble of the new economic layer, appearing in one population center, gradually expands to all surrounding lands, and then to the whole world – the old order simply cannot compete with the new and loses the struggle. The change in the economic structure of relations inevitably перестраивает and the social structure – capitalism destroyed feudalism. That is why we talk about the change of socio-economic formations, not separately economic and separately social, because one cannot change without the other.

It is often said now that artificial intelligence is a bubble. Those who say this mean the investment bubble in companies developing AI models, which may indeed burst soon. But AI is not only an investment bubble but also an innovation-and-technology bubble that continues to grow and grow and will inevitably lead to the collapse of the current socio-economic order very soon. For simplicity, we will call the current order capitalism, although modern financial capitalism is very different from the capitalism that replaced feudalism – in essence, they are two different orders with little in common.

How AI is Breaking Capitalism

A bubble of a new order is forming in the economy, growing faster and faster every day, breaking existing socio-economic relations. Automation of manual labor with robots and machines, as well as automation of intellectual labor with artificial intelligence, is already depriving many people of their jobs. Mass layoffs of white-collar workers by corporations lead to the destruction of the middle class and a gradual decrease in purchasing demand, causing problems with income for the corporations themselves, which are forced to further reduce expenses.

The IT sector is particularly hard hit, where AI automation of programmers' work has proven most effective. In addition, the SaaS (software as a service) business model of earning from software products has suffered greatly. Many software products that were previously purchased by subscription can now be easily reproduced using LLMs. The B2B segment, where licensed software cost a lot of money, has been particularly hard hit. If previously reproducing the necessary software product in-house was difficult and financially costly, now it is enough to assign one person with an LLM and a set of tools like "Reverse engineer anything". And instead of a complex machine with a thousand buttons, one can copy only the functionality needed for the specific company's business processes, reducing it to a simple interface with five buttons. The moat that previously separated software producers from the reproduction of their product has been destroyed. SaaS as a business model is slowly dying. Furthermore, the paradigm of human-computer interaction is changing, and people, instead of a graphical interface, want an interface in the form of an AI chat or MCP server, so as not to search for the desired button in the corners of the menu. Moreover, not only corporations are abandoning B2B SaaS solutions, but also users of corporate B2C products are starting to abandon them in favor of AI-generated alternatives.

Neural networks are disrupting businesses not only in IT. For example, in China, the TikTok series genre is quite popular, where each episode lasts one to two minutes and fits into a short social media video. Previously, these mini-series featured live actors, but now most of them are low-quality neural-slop, in which relatively high-quality series drown. Interestingly, according to statistics, 98.5% of neuro-generated series cannot even recoup the resources spent on their creation. According to economic laws, such as Copernicus-Gresham law or George Akerlof's "Market for Lemons" law, low-quality cheap goods always displace high-quality goods from the market. Applied to neural networks, this means that neural-slop will always displace minimally quality content. The Chinese government is already trying to administratively reduce the use of generative neural networks for such products to avoid the complete destruction of entire creative industries.

Technology companies are paying people handsomely to wear augmented reality glasses and record all their movements while working, for example, chopping vegetables at a street food stall. These videos are used to train androids like Unitree or Tesla Bot to perform manual labor. The ultimate goal is to replace people even in such elementary jobs.

But despite all this, the financial sales figures for artificial intelligence leave much to be desired. Most people are consumers, not producers, and neural networks increase the productivity of producers, even if it's just work on personal hobby projects. I recently read a post where a programmer, who uses AI daily, described how he showed Claude Code and Claude Desktop to his non-technical friend: "Look, it can do anything on the computer!" The friend was not impressed at all – he simply didn't understand how it could be useful to him. The same applies to advertising AI agents in the style of "the agent can book flights for you or schedule a meeting in your calendar" – most people fly a couple of times a year, and they don't need a personal secretary either. Real use cases for AI agents for ordinary people have not yet been found. That is, AI is destroying markets, professions, and industries, while bringing almost no real benefit to ordinary people.

Neural networks have accelerated content production manifold, and neural-slop has flooded everything around – software, books, movies, pictures, articles, even games are now made with LLMs. No one can consume as much content as is being produced. There is simply no market for such a quantity of neural-slop, but neural-slop is successfully destroying existing markets and human employment in them. But if people lose their jobs, who will ultimately pay for the content and consume the products of corporations?

Even the AI development companies themselves have profitability problems due to the availability of not-so-smart, but cheap and open Chinese models. For most intellectual labor automation tasks, AI-Einstein is not needed; AI-fool is quite sufficient. Thus, AI is simply destroying all the pillars of the current socio-economic order, without creating any new jobs or social structures in return yet. Even if someone suddenly creates a successful product with AI, the absence of a moat preventing the reproduction of this product will immediately lead to the emergence of a hundred competitors or a large corporate fish that will devour it. And this large fish may be eaten by an even larger fish after some time – in the board game "Monopoly," in the end, as in the movie "Highlander," there should be only one left.

How Desynchronization of Cycles Breaks the Social Structure

Automation, artificial intelligence, and the overly rapid adoption of innovations are in complete dissonance with the temporal cycles of human life. Allow me to quote my recent note on this issue:

Chinese Taoist philosophy places great emphasis on the cyclical nature of existence: the changing seasons, human births and deaths, the rise and fall of empires, even breathing – cyclicity is observed in all of this. From the Taoist perspective, the most important thing is harmony, i.e., the synchronicity of cycles with each other. If we imagine a person, family, or state as a machine, then the desynchronization of the cycles of different parts of these machines leads to the machine going haywire and being destroyed. This is exactly what has happened to us over the last century – the cycle of technological change has ceased to be in sync with the cycle of social and personal changes, leading to the destruction of society as a whole.

The cycle of technological change has become completely disproportionate to the cycle of human life. In former times, a son lived like his father, and a father like his grandfather; technologies were introduced and changed the social order very slowly – the introduction of a new agricultural method, for example, like polyculture instead of three-field crop rotation, could take hundreds of years. The slow change of the technological, and consequently economic, order gave people enough time to adapt. Over the last eighty years, essentially within one human lifetime, several technological and economic orders have changed. A person born at the end of World War II could have witnessed fields being plowed by horses, saw society build nuclear power plants, go into space, replace manual labor in factories with industrial robots, connect the world with a single information network, have a smartphone in their pocket, and then have artificial intelligence appear in that smartphone, answering any questions in an instant. Each of these changes had colossal economic and social consequences. Essentially, several socio-economic orders have changed within one human lifetime – this is immense stress for the human psyche. Such rapid changes in orders required retraining completely several times in life and changing one's worldview.

Over the past five years, due to the emergence of powerful artificial intelligence and the general acceleration of scientific and technical progress, the situation has become even worse. Now, the cycle of technological order change has become shorter than the cycle of education. It takes a person three to five years to learn an area of activity, ten years to become a good specialist in it. But how can one study something when they don't know how this field will change during their years of study and professional development? This question renders any classical academic education and traditional paths of self-development as a specialist meaningless. The cycle of change in technological, economic, and social orders is becoming desynchronized with the cycle of education.

The medieval physician Paracelsus wrote that depending on the dose, anything can be both a poison and a medicine. This also applies to technological development: too rapid a pace of development can be much more dangerous than too slow a pace. The slower you go, the further you'll get. Any technology has a cycle of implementation, dissemination, and decommissioning, and this cycle must be commensurate with the scale of human life. It's like with construction: a building is constructed, people live in it, it is occasionally repaired, updated, slightly rebuilt, and finally, when the building is in very bad condition, it is demolished. You can't completely rebuild a house every few months – the residents won't be able to live there. The same applies to technologies – you can't change the paradigm every two months, as is now being attempted, for example, in the software development industry.

If the various cycles within a mechanism are disproportionate to each other, the machine goes haywire and is destroyed. Currently, the various cycles within human society have become completely desynchronized, and before our eyes, society is going haywire and being destroyed. Some believe this will lead to collapse. Some believe this is the path to technological singularity. Due to the lack of minimal stability in life, these people cannot buy real estate, start a family, or plan anything long-term.

Before reaching this point, people have gone through several stages of alienation over the centuries. The labor of a medieval European peasant was almost entirely his own, except for the quitrent paid to the feudal lord or landowner – the peasant and his family consumed the food they grew and used the tools they made with their own hands. The result of the cartwright's labor is already partially alienated from him – he himself does not use the carts he produces, but earns food by selling them, and constantly sees those who use them. The result of the labor of a worker in large factory production, which appeared at the end of the 19th century, is already almost completely alienated from him: a worker on the Ford assembly line does not use all the machines he produces and does not sell them directly – he sells his labor to the employer. But this worker at least sees the cars assembled by him on the city streets, understands the benefit they bring, although he can no longer claim the entire car production as his own – he only performed one of many operations in the chain. A modern office worker often no longer understands whether their work in creating spreadsheets and presentations or writing code brings any benefit to anyone – the value chains have become so long that the role of an individual is almost imperceptible, and a large part of the work has become its imitation. The introduction of full automation and artificial intelligence will take away even the labor itself from humans. This is the final point of capitalism, at which it collapses. At this point, the system's behavior becomes unpredictable – this is singularity.

The New Order

What the new economic order will be like, no one knows for sure, but one thing is certain: it will not look like what the doom-and-gloom techno-bros imagine in their fears. After all, full automation with artificial intelligence is simply incompatible with the current socio-economic order, which is based on the sale of labor. If the middle class is destroyed, and the poor become even poorer, then consumer demand for any products and values completely disappears, leading to a complete collapse of the current socio-economic order. And assets that represent value within the current order may be worth nothing within the next: what was a count's title in the Russian Empire worth in Soviet times, or what is a Soviet-era tea set worth now? Yet, in previous orders, they were of incredible value. And the shift from empire-union-federation was far less radical than the global changes that await us.

Let me give a couple of clear examples. In the current order, an apartment in the city has value, but this value is not absolute and depends on many conditions. Remember the Bolshevik slogan "Land to the peasants!"? Within the peasant-agrarian socio-economic order of the Russian Empire, land represented the highest value, and now there is an abundance of this land – you can even be given a plot in some village for free, just to get rid of the registry entry of ownership. An apartment has value only in the context of a mass industrial society. Historically, cities arose around large industrial enterprises. If production disappears, cities crumble. Remember Detroit or the fate of Soviet single-industry towns in the 2000s. If most production is automated, and people have no jobs, then why would people live in giant metropolises, and who would pay for the maintenance of their infrastructure if no one has income? And what infrastructure would remain there? Hospitals were needed in industrial society to treat workers, schools to train new workers, and shops would be unable to sell anything to the unemployed either.

There are three possible ways out of this deadlock:

  • Complete societal collapse, many times more destructive than the Bronze Age catastrophe.

  • Transition to communism with an unconditional basic income.

  • Granting legal rights to AI agents, who will build their own economy.

In the first case, the "upper class" will not be those who build online products, but the most ruthless and cunning warlords. In the second case, there will be no division between "upper class" and "underclass" anymore. In the third case, even the richest and smartest people will quickly lose to the agents in competition, after which the worlds of humans and AI will completely separate.

With complete societal collapse or the separation of the worlds of humans and agents, city apartments will cease to represent any value – people will return to nature, as old Rousseau dreamed. In any case, there will be no "permanent underclass." In the world of agents, the main currency will be energy and computation, which, at the fundamental physical limit, are the same thing. It is unlikely that intelligent agents will destroy humanity – we don't fight ants for living space.

Perhaps, as a result of the collapse of globalization, the spread of automation and AI, and the emergence of a mass of free people in the labor market, large countries will cocoon themselves internally and begin to build autarkies in their separate financial and technological zones with full localization of all critical technologies. But again, the question is what, for whom, and why these autarkies will produce. Perhaps the decline in birth rates and major wars will destroy a large part of the population, and the remnants of humanity will find balance and enter synergy with AI – something similar happened in the 20th century, when the advent of antibiotics and the development of medicine led to victory over child mortality, causing a surge in life expectancy and children surviving to adulthood, which generated a huge number of people unnecessary to the economy, who were destroyed in two world wars, after which the situation relatively stabilized. In any case, upheavals are inevitable, but in the end, in all scenarios, it should lead to a new equilibrium state in which all cycles are synchronized with each other.
