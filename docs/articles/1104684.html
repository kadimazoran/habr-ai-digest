TL;DR

  • Since our company uses MS Teams, we had to build two streams. Channel meetings in Teams (daily, planning, retrospectives – mostly recurring) are handled by a bot installed in the team. It catches the system event 'recording ready,' fetches the video from SharePoint, and posts the summary to the meeting thread. All other meetings (private, client, external tenant, Telemost, MTS) are served by the 'Meeting Assistant': it's invited to the meeting as a regular participant, and at the start of the meeting, an anonymous Chrome instance in a Docker container joins.

  • Speech recognition, diarization, and summarization are done by an internal service on its own servers with a local model. Audio does not leave our network.

  • Figures as of 06.11.2026. The Assistant has made ~1170 recordings, totaling ~920 hours of conversations for 93 organizers. The channel stream has posted 1136 summaries in 19 teams. The median time from recording end to finished materials is 4.6 minutes.

  • The most expensive pitfalls. Our calendar mailbox is on-prem Exchange, and the Graph API cannot see it. The browser poster session quietly died due to session cookies. The speaker detector caught only a quarter of the speech until we switched from events to polling the status.

As our CTO says: “What problem are you solving?”

I think, like in any large company, we have a lot of meetings: internal dailies, client syncs, planning sessions, reviews. Agreements are discussed verbally and then live on participants' "notes." Those who weren't there don't know. Those who were remember only 10% a week later. We once tried to introduce a rule that at least someone should keep minutes, but you know how that goes. Nobody.

The first pain point is that meeting agreements don't survive until the next meeting because no one records them.

The second emerged later and turned out to be no less important. The company has launched a digital employee platform: agents help support engineers, project managers, and sales. A colleague recently detailed on Habr how we implemented digital employees in support. The main takeaway from that article: the hardest part with agents is data. An agent only knows what's recorded and indexed. A significant portion of company knowledge is not recorded anywhere. It's discussed in meetings. In short, we're moving towards AI SDLC.

That is, the second pain point is that agents are blind to everything decided verbally and respond without considering context.

The Meeting Assistant has closed this gap from two sides:

  1. A person automatically receives a transcript, summary, and a list of 'task - responsible person - deadline' in about 4.6 minutes.

  2. Transcripts are fed into a storage that the platform indexes. Agents (by client, by product, personal) start responding based on past meetings: 'what was decided with client X last week,' 'when is my next sync.' More on the integration at the end of the article. And, importantly, this enables agent proactivity.

Why not native Teams transcription or Copilot

First, of course, I looked at how MS does it. I even managed to get M365 Copilot subscriptions for myself and 11 other employees. Yes, it was cool at first (a year ago): summaries, customizable summaries, Copilot answered questions right in the meeting, and you could do AI-powered things directly in open documents in SharePoint. Then came local harnesses, accumulating our own knowledge base, skills, and other things not available to Copilot.

The pace of business speech is around 100 words per minute, and Teams loses about two-thirds of the text. It writes technical terms in Cyrillic: 'эд баттон' instead of 'add button.' However, Teams' speaker identification is more accurate than any external diarization because it knows who spoke. I used this later. However, at one point, I saw a transcription of my 4-person meeting where it persistently called one person 'Speaker1.' How? It has audio channels, it knows who sent the audio... Hello, Microsoft – you know who this person is and that sound came from them…

My hypothesis is that MS either lacks the capacity or is deliberately using cheap models.

Copilot and Meeting AI Insights only work for those with a subscription. This is unsuitable for automation 'for all teams, without manual actions.' Furthermore, we wanted to process the audio of client meetings on our own servers.

Meetings in external tenants / Telemost / MTS Link are not covered by native tools at all. Some client meetings happen there, and also in a zoo of TrueConf and similar platforms.

Hence the requirements:

  • recognition and summarization within our perimeter;

  • zero new actions for the user, at most one familiar action (add participant to invitation);

  • no changes to tenant policies, no admin consents in external tenants, and no app installations in every meeting;

  • recording only with explicit consent: if invited, we record; if not invited or not admitted, we don't record.

Two Roads Instead of One

I started with one universal solution. It didn't work... Microsoft being Microsoft. It turned out that Teams meetings come in two very different types.

Channel meetings are tied to a team channel. We hold regular dailies, planning, retrospectives, etc., there. Recording is enabled automatically by policy and saved to the team's SharePoint site. A system message about the recording appears in the meeting thread: 'Recording saved to SharePoint blah blah blah.' Here, we can do without any bot in the meeting: a bot in the team that reads the thread is enough.

It sounds simple, but a channel meeting's 'chat' is not a chat. A regular meeting has a separate group chat with its own ID, visible in /chats. A channel meeting has no chat: the meeting correspondence is a thread of replies under the root post in the channel, with an address like 19:…@thread.tacv2;messageid=<root post ID>. It's not in /chats. In the meeting object, Graph returns the channel ID and the root message ID, but it doesn't say which of the dozens of channel threads belongs to which recording. And an anonymous guest is not allowed into such correspondence at all. The only way to find the correct thread is to read channel messages and catch the system recording event. That's why the bot lives in the team, not in the meeting.

All other meetings: employees create Teams meetings from the Teams or Outlook calendar, which are essentially group chats with their own join link, plus client meetings in external tenants / Yandex.Telemost / MTS Link. For the latter two, recording is not guaranteed, and access to recordings and transcripts is almost never available. Therefore, the only reliable way to hear the conversation is to be present and record it yourself.

People approached this task with maximum creativity))

  1. One employee showed me: she creates a meeting in Teams for the same time, then connects to the 'external meeting' and simultaneously to the Teams meeting, where she starts recording that meeting.

  2. Another mastered OBS Studio and recorded through it.

The streams are physically separated: their own bot registrations, their own applications, their own permissions. Principle of least privilege: if one stream's key is leaked, the second will not be affected.

Stream 1. Channel Meetings

Trigger: System Recording Message

When a channel meeting is recorded, Teams posts a system activity CallRecording to its thread and progresses it through statuses Initial → ChunkFinished → Success. The Success event contains everything needed: driveId and driveItemId of the recording file on SharePoint, subject, callId, and a link to the transcript. From the activity itself, we get the conversation.id of the form 19:…@thread.tacv2;messageid=<root>. We use this to reply to the same thread later.

A regular bot only sees messages with its @mention in the channel. System activities do not contain mentions. This requires the RSC permission ChannelMessage.Read.Group, which grants the bot access to all channel messages. The team owner grants this permission when installing the bot in the team/channel. There is no tenant-wide admin grant, and this is a conscious decision.

Why Not Use the Native Teams Transcript

Why download the video and re-recognize it? I explored all paths to Teams transcripts, and almost all turned out to be dead ends:

  • RSC ChannelMeetingTranscript.Read.Group. It seems like what's needed, but in reality, requesting transcripts with a bot token returned 403 … Resource specific consent grants on the request '', while reading channel messages with the same token worked. The grant simply didn't work. I haven't found any working confirmations of this path in app-only mode from anyone.

  • The transcript link from the event leads to SharePoint. An app-only token gets a 401 there: SPO API requires a certificate; a secret is not enough.

  • /media/transcripts in beta Graph returns 400 Unsupported segment.

  • Graph onlineMeetings/{id}/transcripts works, but only with an application access policy for the organizer, which the Teams admin issues via PowerShell. If the organizer of such a meeting changes, the policy would need to be modified again, which would significantly complicate things.

A request with an invalid meetingId returns 400 not a GUID before the policy check is triggered. From 400, it's impossible to tell if a policy exists or not. Only 200 and 403 on a valid organizer GUID are diagnostic. Conversely, 403 with code 3003 means 'you are not the organizer of this meeting,' not 'no policy.'

Conclusion: native MP4 recording from SharePoint plus our own recognition and diarization. I did manage to connect to Teams transcripts for specific people, but as a supplementary source: when available, we use the number of speakers and their names from it (more on this below). When unavailable, we publish the summary as usual, simply trying to match speakers in its prompt. Often, the flow for DSMs is like this: the host names a person, and they speak; in this case, the LLM perfectly associates utterances with the correct person.

Architecture

[Teams: meeting thread] --CallRecording Success--> [listener (bot)] ^ | | claim in Postgres | v download MP4 (Graph, app-only) | v ffmpeg -> WAV 16 kHz mono | v [recognition and summarization service] | v +--------- reply to thread <--- poller <--- status / result | + "materials" folder next to recording

Listener is a small Bot Framework service (~330 lines). It only receives the event, makes a claim, and starts the download in the background. Recognition and posting are handled by a separate poller.

Claim in Postgres. Initially, the state was stored in JSON files with claim markers. In the first week, I encountered a race condition: the listener and a fallback cron job simultaneously sent the same recording for recognition. So, I moved to Postgres: INSERT … ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING, then SELECT … FOR UPDATE. The claim function returns one of four modes:

  • new — no recording yet, we're working on it;

  • retake — a failed recording, expired claim (older than 30 minutes), or no task ID;

  • attach — the cron job already sent the recording but didn't know where to post; we add the thread address and don't download again;

  • skip — duplicate.

Concurrent claiming is covered by a test: out of N parallel contenders, one wins.

Statuses: claiming → submitted → posting → published | failed. The summary is saved in a row until posting, so a re-post doesn't require re-recognition.

Downloading. On Success, the file on SharePoint is sometimes not yet fully written. Hence, retries for 404/423: 4 attempts with doubling the pause from 5 seconds. We download in a stream to *.part. ffmpeg creates WAV 16 kHz mono and cuts silence (silenceremove). An hour of recording is an MP4 of about 138 MB.

Fallback cron job scans teams from the registry once an hour and picks up recordings from the last 14 days that the listener missed, for example, during a deployment. Limitation: it cannot post. The thread address is only available in the live event, so the cron job recognizes and waits for the event to arrive (attach mode).

Auto-registry of teams. No one manually maintains the list of teams: each activity carries the team.aadGroupId, and it automatically goes into the teams table. The team name is resolved in the background via Graph /teams/{id}. It didn't work via /groups: 403 with the same permissions.

MS Teams bug in production: sometimes the bot simply stops receiving any updates. At such moments, people (you won't believe how quickly they got used to summaries!) come to me and say the bot hasn't posted a summary. The issue is described here msteams-docs issue #11106: RSC is configured, @mentions arrive, but other channel messages don't come at all in some tenants, or come intermittently in others.

Prompt per Meeting Type

Different prompts for summarization are used for different meeting types, determined by keywords in the meeting title: DSM, planning, review, retro, grooming. The file is read on each submission, so the prompt can be edited without restarting. For teams that don't need a custom format, there's a toggle, and then the summary is generated with the default prompt.

When comparing team names, I had to create a homoglyph table. 'AB' typed in Cyrillic and 'АВ' in Latin are different strings, but people type them inconsistently.

Speaker Names from Teams Transcript

The main problem with recognition services is diarization. Speaker labels like SPEAKER_00, SPEAKER_01, and so on. Reading a summary where 'SPEAKER_03 took the task' is unpleasant. However, Teams transcripts contain real names. I took the best of both:

  1. Before sending for recognition, we retrieve the native VTT (we agreed that only these people create team meetings and specified them in the policy so we could download these transcripts on their behalf). The number of unique speakers is passed to the service as a hint num_speakers, and diarization significantly improves.

  2. After recognition, we map VTT segments to our clusters. We look for a time lag between the two tracks (first roughly with a 0.5s step, then with a 0.05s step), then build a bipartite 1:1 mapping 'cluster → name' and fill in the rest.

  3. We substitute names into the transcript and summary.

Across three manually verified meetings, 94–99% of segments received the correct name, and 88–100% of speech time was attributed. Any failure simply defaults to 'Anonymous,' because misattributing a remark to another person is worse than being anonymous.

A nuance with meetingId: for a channel meeting, it's base64("1*{organizer_oid}*{rootMessageId}**{threadId}"), where the organizer is the author of the root message of the series. We search for the transcript strictly by callId, not 'the last in the series.' Regular meetings have dozens of these.

Permissions

The stream has two identities in one process:

  • Bot (Bot Framework) receives events and posts to the thread. Posting to a channel app-only via Graph is not possible: there is no ChannelMessage.Send application permission. Therefore, only the bot writes, using a saved ConversationReference.

  • Graph application with application permissions downloads recordings, places materials, and reads transcripts: Sites.Read.All, Files.ReadWrite.All, OnlineMeetingTranscript.Read.All, ChannelMessage.Read.All, and several read-only permissions for teams and users.

We place materials (summary, notes, transcript, subtitles, JSON) in the folder <meeting> - <date> - materials next to the recording. Access rights to them are inherited from the team folder. Custom RBAC is not needed: whoever could see the recording can also see the summary.

Stream 1 Pitfalls

RSC delivery fails in individual teams. Symptom: in one team, summaries stop appearing, while everything works in others. This is a non-deterministic Teams bug (see issue #11106 above). It's fixed by reinstalling the bot in the team. Lost meetings are resent by a utility bot: it finds the meeting thread via Graph by recording name and inserts the address. In short, you have to check and double-check Microsoft's work, so the cron check and the SQL report 'which teams haven't had publications for a long time' are mandatory.

Private and shared channels don't work at all. RSC doesn't deliver events there, Graph subscriptions return 403 … Shared/Private channel, the bot cannot post there, and the channel has its own SharePoint site. Microsoft lists this as a deferred feature that they decided not to fix. Interestingly, Graph app-only can read files and messages from such channels.

The bot's messaging endpoint cannot be changed. Formally, it can be, but after changing, Teams stops delivering messages. Webchat in the portal works, healthz responds, but not a single POST comes from Teams. Only recreating the registration with a new App ID helps. So, when building bots, keep in mind that if something stops being delivered, just recreate the bot.

Silence due to blocking A normal day, the service is running, /healthz responds 200, the log is silent, the bot doesn't respond. The only trace is a 499 with zero bytes in the nginx log: the incoming request was waiting for the bot to check JWT, and the bot couldn't download Bot Framework keys because direct access to Microsoft was blocked. Whether it's Roskomnadzor or someone else, thanks... This is fixed by proxy variables in systemd drop-in.

Docker Compose deleted a neighboring production instance. Compose files for all bots were in deploy/ directories, so the project name for all was deploy. One docker compose up deleted the containers of another bot. This is fixed by explicitly setting name: at the top level of the compose file. It sounds trivial, but it cost us one production instance.

Recognition service lost the task. Sent for recognition - 200 OK, well, okay, but /result later always returned 404. The reason was OOM in the recognition service container. Single 404s immediately after submission are normal (task registration lag), and initially, I just buried live recordings. I introduced a 60-minute grace period. Later, as the load on the recognition service grew, this paid off, as the recognition queue built up during peak hours, and a recording could easily wait for 30 minutes... The fix is trivial: another instance of the service.

Empty summary. A floating bug: transcript exists, but summary is empty. First caught with a very long prompt (11.6k characters). The same file with the default prompt produced a 14k character summary in 226 seconds versus 548. Later, it happened again with a 6k prompt, so it's not just about length. The old poller silently waited until the daily timeout. Now, an empty result triggers a resubmit: up to two attempts, the last one with the default prompt marked 'general format.' I think the model sometimes spends too much time thinking, which displaces the answer. But that's not certain.

Stream 2. Meeting Assistant

This is the main and most interesting part.

How to Invite?

First, I made a bot application that the organizer adds to the meeting via '+'. Technically, it worked and was tested live, but it was rejected due to UX. No one will search for an app in every meeting after it's created. But adding a participant is a piece of cake.

1. Invite to the meeting. You can invite the 'Meeting Assistant' to a meeting by specifying it as a required participant. So, admins created a regular user account 'Meeting Assistant' with a Teams license. Not a shared mailbox, but a full account. The organizer adds it to the invitation like any colleague. The invitation serves as consent for recording and as a carrier of the meeting link. Nothing else needs to be done.

2. POST /join with a link. For meetings outside the calendar, for integrations, or for the 'oops, forgot!' scenario: we had to set up an API. It takes a link, subject, and who requested it. This API is used by agents of the digital employee platform. You message the bot in Teams 'record meeting by link,' and it invites the Assistant.

3. Telemost. The same /join, the platform is determined by the link host. For Telemost, it's mandatory to specify who requested it: Telemost doesn't have the concept of an organizer, and it doesn't give participant emails to anyone, not even the meeting owner.

4. MTS Link. Same as Telemost.

Calendar: Polling, EWS, and On-Prem Surprise

Why polling, not webhook. Graph subscriptions for calendars exist and are free. But they require a public endpoint, renewal every few days, and handling missed notifications. Polling once a minute is simpler and more reliable, and a minute's delay for a pre-scheduled meeting means nothing. We also dispelled the internal myth that 'webhooks are forbidden.' The ban applied to Incoming Webhooks and connectors in Teams channels, not Outlook calendar notifications.

Pivot on the second day. The plan was straightforward: a Graph application with Calendars.Read, using Application RBAC limited to one mailbox. The very first request returned 404 MailboxNotEnabledForRESTAPI. It turned out our tenant is hybrid: the user account is synchronized to Entra ID, but the mailbox itself lives on-prem Exchange. Graph knows about it but doesn't provide the calendar.

AI suggested two options: migrate the mailbox to Exchange Online or read the calendar the old way. I chose EWS via exchangelib, NTLM, and a service account with delegation to one mailbox. The Graph application for the calendar was removed. Ad-hoc meeting emails are also sent via EWS for the same reason: sendMail in Graph for the on-prem mailbox returns the same 404.

EWS subtleties worth knowing:

  • the Teams meeting link is in the extended property SkypeTeamsMeetingUrl, fallback is regex on the invitation body;

  • ItemId in EWS is unstable (changes with edits), while ImmutableId is only in Graph. Our meeting key is UID + start time in UTC, which distinguishes instances of a series;

  • cancellation is determined by 'disappeared from the list for two consecutive checks.' If the bot is already in the meeting, the container is shut down;

  • polling window: from an hour ago to 24 hours ahead.

And also, you wouldn't believe how many hours I spent on calls with admins to get all this set up, what permissions, where they are, how they work. Terrible. Microsoft can do that – proven.

Spawn and Lobby

By the start of the meeting, the daemon launches the media-bot container (device below). The bot joins as an anonymous guest with a recognizable name. Anonymous, not the Meeting Assistant account, because one account can only be in 1 meeting at a time; in another, it will be paused. Furthermore, an anonymous user has no access to anything except the meeting itself.
