In the task of training a machine translation model, there's a pleasant moment: you find several million sentence pairs and for a second, you feel like the hardest part is over. Then you open a random line. On the left is a sentence, on the right is a table fragment. The next pair has the same topic, but not the meaning. A bit further, someone carefully put HTML remnants into the text. The dataset arrived with the furniture of previous tenants.

For low-resource languages, this is compounded by the limited availability of parallel data. Therefore, it's especially useful here to understand what exactly the model receives as input and what architecture we are trying to train on it.

This is Anya Lebedeva, a developer in the AI VK experimental technologies department, and today I will talk about kazRush — a system for translating from Russian to Kazakh and back. We collected and cleaned a corpus from open sources, trained models from scratch, and selected a model configuration with approximately 197 million parameters. Along the way, it turned out that stricter cleaning sometimes worsens the result, a larger vocabulary is not necessarily more useful, and increasing the model to 738 million parameters does not solve the problem by itself. In general, several quite reasonable ideas had to be sent back to the door, but the final result justified the effort. The Russian-Kazakh model took first place in its track at LoResMT 2026 — a workshop at the EACL conference.

Why this language pair needs separate work

Russian and Kazakh are used in education, media, government services, and everyday communication. There are enough practical scenarios for translation. The situation with data is more complicated: the presence of millions of native speakers does not mean the availability of a comparable number of high-quality "sentence-translation" pairs.

Kazakh has a feature that must be considered during training: it is an agglutinative language. Affixes are sequentially attached to the root, forming many word forms. For the model, this means more variants that need to be linked together and learned to translate. When there are few examples, some of these variants are encountered rarely.

Large multilingual models provide a good starting point, but we were interested in what quality could be achieved with a specialized system if bilingual data were carefully prepared and the architecture tailored to it.

The main applied direction was translation from Russian to Kazakh. The reverse model was trained simultaneously: by this point, the corpus and pipeline were already ready. The tokenizer and architectural principles are the same for both directions, but the weights are separate.

When merging datasets, we kept a source label for each entry. This small decision later proved very convenient. If a peculiarity repeats in thousands of lines, it's useful to find out which corpus brought it.

Cleaning was divided into general cleaning, applied to all data, and specific rules for particular corpora.

How to remove garbage and stop in time

Rule 1: Don't neglect the boring part

First, let's go through trivial data cleaning methods. They might seem obvious, but it's important to mention that we didn't neglect them, as they form the basis of high-quality preprocessing.

  1. Removed exact duplicates

  2. Removed HTML tags, other markup, and garbage characters

  3. Applied Unicode normalization NFKC

  4. Removed duplicate spaces, separators, and other extraneous punctuation

  5. Each sentence in a pair was independently checked by the language identification model fastText. Pairs were kept where the predicted languages matched the expected translation direction. For example, for the "Russian — Kazakh" direction, we ensured that the source sentence was detected as Russian text and the target sentence as Kazakh text.

  6. As another check, we used LaBSE: we obtained multilingual embeddings for the source and target sentences, calculated their dot product, and removed pairs with a value below a manually set threshold of 0.4. It would have been logical to adapt the threshold for each dataset, but selecting it started to take a lot of time and was still unstable, so we settled on a single value, losing some good pairs and keeping some bad ones.

Let's check the numbers

Now, let's move on to the non-trivial part. After standard preprocessing, we still had a significant number of mismatched pairs. We needed to devise rules to find and remove such instances. One idea was to check numbers. We extracted sequences of numbers from both sides and compared them as sets. Comparing ordered lists was pointless — due to grammatical peculiarities, numbers in the source and translation might appear in different orders, but this approach accounts for both composition and frequency of repetition without requiring the same order.

When a mismatch was detected, the sentence pair was sent for manual review. Automatic deletion would have been too harsh: numbers can be correctly represented in different ways, for example, as digits in the source and words in the translation. If such a case was identified during manual review, the numbers were converted to a single format. If the review showed that texts with mismatched numbers were not translations of each other, the pair was excluded.

Even the first letter can be a separate task

After the first iteration of training, we discovered that regardless of whether the source sentence started with a lowercase or uppercase letter, the translation always began with an uppercase letter. This is incorrect, as users don't always translate entire sentences, and in general, for translation, it's important to preserve the format of the original text, whatever the reasons.

This behavior turned out to be a consequence of the data used for training: the corpus contained 237,859 Russian sentences starting with a lowercase letter, and for half of them, the Kazakh translation began with an uppercase letter.

We manually checked a hundred random examples. In most cases, the difference appeared to be an artifact of corpus preparation; only a small portion required preserving the original case. Therefore, we added a rule: if the source sentence begins with a lowercase letter, the first character of the translation is also converted to lowercase, leaving the rest of the text unchanged.

Another creative heuristic against garbage

We wanted to filter out lines that contained hundreds of exclamation marks or scattered dashes instead of text. To do this, we calculated the proportion of letters and digits in each line and divided it by the line length raised to the power of 0.1:

score = (number of letters and digits / total number of characters) / line length^0.1

The intuition is that the more punctuation and other non-alphanumeric characters in a line, the less likely it is to be a real sentence. Dividing by the length to the power of 0.1 further reduces the score for long lines, as we operate at the sentence level, and a very, very long line is suspicious. A pair was kept if the score for each of the two texts was not lower than 0.4.

Alignment score from readily available means

We investigated the hypothesis that if the source and translation differ significantly in length by characters, it's likely not a "text-translation" pair. After studying reference translations from the FLORES+ dataset and manually reviewing other data, we set the acceptable length ratio between 0.5 and 2.0. That is, if one sentence in a "source — target" pair is more than twice as long as the other, the pair was deleted.

Exact duplicates were removed. With almost identical ones, it was more interesting

At the beginning of the pipeline, we had already removed exact duplicates from the entire collection. After that, we tried to find near-duplicates using MinHash LSH — an approach for finding similar records. The expectation was clear: fewer repetitions mean more diverse training. However, in our experiments, the quality of the final model deteriorated after such processing.

A possible explanation is that similar sentences act as natural augmentation in conditions of limited data. The model sees variations in constructions and receives additional training examples. This is a hypothesis, but the practical conclusion from the experiment is quite specific: removing near-duplicates was not included in the final recipe.

Why one set of filters wasn't enough

After general cleaning, we reviewed the corpora separately. Subtitles had their own artifacts and garbage characters that required special regular expressions. Another source contained artificial numbers at the beginning of sentences. Some long pairs had content at the beginning and end that differed; the alignment was only partial.

When we found a recurring problem, we updated the processing and ran it again. The source label allowed us to limit the special rule to the data for which it was needed.

As a result, the corpus composition changed:

  • KazParC was kept almost entirely after basic checks. Manual review showed high quality for both original texts and synthetic parts.

  • From OPUS, we kept cleaned OpenSubtitles, QED, TED Talks, TED2020, Tatoeba-2022, and Tatoeba-2023.

  • TIL data was included after more thorough cleaning.

  • MultiCCAligned and WikiMatrix were excluded due to quality and alignment issues.

After all stages, 9,054,960 sentence pairs remained.

Tokenizer: 8,000 units for 2 languages

A tokenizer breaks text into small parts — tokens — that the model works with. For kazRush, a common SentencePiece tokenizer was trained on the Russian and Kazakh parts of the cleaned corpus. The vocabulary size was 8,000 subword units.

We also tested a version with 16,000 units. It slightly worsened the final quality, so we stuck with the previous version. The resulting SentencePiece was converted into a T5 tokenizer from Hugging Face and used in all experiments.

Architecture: where to spend 197 million parameters

We used a Transformer with an encoder and decoder based on T5. The encoder processes the source sentence considering the context, and the decoder builds the translation step by step, accessing the source text representations through cross-attention. We trained the translation model on parallel examples and chose an architecture directly suited for this task. Attempting to create a strong translator based on a decoder-only model from scratch would require considering a different balance of data, model size, and computation.

All models were trained from scratch, with random initialization. Pre-trained T5 weights were not used.

First, we compared the standard configurations of T5-Small, T5-Base, and T5-Large. Then, we assembled our own: we kept relatively small hidden representation and attention sizes, similar to T5-Small, but increased the depth of the encoder and decoder, as well as the size of the feed-forward blocks.

The assumption was that with our data volume, increasing the depth and capacity of feed-forward blocks might be more beneficial than significantly expanding the hidden state and attention dimensions. Why might this configuration be advantageous? Additional layers allow for sequential refinement of text representations: each subsequent layer works with the result of the previous one. Increasing the intermediate size of feed-forward blocks allows for computing more features for each token, which, in turn, allows for considering more diverse combinations of features — for example, lexical, grammatical, and contextual — when updating a token's representation. At the same time, we maintain compact hidden state and attention dimensions, limiting the number of parameters to be trained on the available parallel data. This way, we direct the model's additional capacity into the depth of processing and feature transformation. In our ablation experiments, this parameter distribution proved more successful than standard T5 configurations, including the significantly larger T5-Large.

How we trained and selected the model

The maximum sequence length was limited to 128 tokens. We used a batch size of 128 with gradient accumulation and mixed precision BF16. The learning rate was 0.003, warmup was 1,000 steps, and label smoothing was 0.1.

Training continued for up to 300,000 optimization steps. The checkpoint was selected based on validation loss.

For comparisons, translations were generated identically: beam search with three beams, no sampling, and with early stopping.

What the experiments showed

We used the FLORES+ dev dataset for validation and compared results on the FLORES+ devtest dataset. We looked at three metrics:

  • BLEU evaluates word sequence matches with the reference translation and penalizes overly short responses.

  • chrF compares character n-grams. This is useful for rich morphology — matching parts of word forms is also considered.

  • COMET is a neural network-based evaluation that uses the source text, the model's translation, and the reference.

For all three metrics, a higher value is better.

What we learned from this work

The most useful solutions turned out to be quite specific. Preserve the origin of each pair. Examine data manually. Check numbers, case, language, and semantic consistency in separate stages. Measure the effect of cleaning on the final model — including when new filtering appears to be an obvious improvement.

A similar approach was used with the architecture: we compared several configurations and found a suitable parameter distribution for our task. The 197-million-parameter model proved stronger than the larger T5-Large in our internal experiments, and at the competition, it confidently outperformed the compared multilingual systems.
