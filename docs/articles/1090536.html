I am not a programmer. I am one of those who love technology and interesting solutions and sometimes build something with my own hands to test an idea. This article is about how a small home experiment with sound led me to a question that, in my opinion, no one is really asking right now: Where should an AI assistant live?

It all started with one of my projects, also about sound and human movement: a phone in your pocket picks up footsteps, and in your headphones, each step sounds like walking on snow, a forest path, or under stone arches. Spatial sound already works live there.

During this work, I found a way to move sound around your head: place it to the left, right, in front, or far away. Like in ASMR, but I choose the location myself, right with my finger on the screen. After playing around, I noticed something that I hadn't encountered as a ready-made tool for the listener. That's what I want to talk about.

Sound That Captures All Attention

When I thought about how music and film production works, I saw a common pattern. Almost everything is designed to fully capture the listener's attention. And preferably, for the listener to be sitting still.

Sit down — listen to the album. Sit down — watch the movie. Put on headphones — and the sound fills your entire head, from ear to ear. This is great when you want to immerse yourself. But it works poorly when you need to think about something else simultaneously: write, calculate, work with your hands.

What Already Exists in the World

Then I went to search and found out that there is nothing fundamentally new in the technology itself. Controlling where sound comes from has been possible for a long time: stereo, binaural recordings, ASMR, "3D audio" in movies and games. Even a regular browser can do this with built-in tools, without any special programs.

But everywhere, it's the same picture: the soundscape is pre-constructed by the author. The sound engineer for the film decided that the helicopter would fly from left to right. The author of the ASMR video decided that the whisper would be near the right ear. And the listener is left with only one thing — to accept the ready-made decision and preferably listen while sitting still.

In headphones with "spatial sound" from major companies, it's also like this: you can turn the effect on or off. You cannot choose *where* exactly the music will sound.

To be fair, the very idea of music that doesn't demand attention is not new either. Brian Eno invented ambient music back in the 70s — music that you can listen to, or not listen to, as part of the environment. And the effect of background music on work has long been studied. But even there, the listener doesn't choose the sound's location in space.

What I Found: Sound Can Be Moved Out of the Center of Attention

This is why I'm writing all this. When I started moving the sound point around, I noticed: moving the sound out of the center of your head changes the very way you perceive it.

As long as the music sounds "inside," from ear to ear, it captures all attention. If you move it to the side and a little further away, it stops being the main focus. It starts to work as background and leaves your head free for other tasks.

It's like a radio on a windowsill. It plays somewhere to the side, and you sit at your computer and work. You can hear it, but it doesn't bother you. Right now, as I write this text, music is hanging to my front left, far away — and I'm calmly working on the text.

And one difference from a real radio, which I liked even more: this "speaker" moves with me. I stand up, walk around the room — the sound stays in the same place relative to me, at the same volume. The brain quickly gets used to considering its own head as a "room," and the point near the right ear is perceived exactly there.

Another observation. If you let the sound fly around your head — in a circle, in a spiral, slowly or quickly — over time, you start to better distinguish between "front" and "back." This becomes a kind of training for hearing and attention.

Wisp Player: What Came of It

From these experiments, a project emerged that I named Wisp Player — "an echo on your shoulder." The first association when the sound started playing on my shoulder was a pirate with a parrot. The parrot remained a character, and I named it Wisp, or it could also stand for Whispering Immersive Sound Partner.

It's a player that works right in the phone's browser. No installation is needed. What it can do:

  • Radar. You are in the center. Drag the marker (the parrot) with your finger — and the sound moves there: to your shoulder, in front, behind you, up to 3 meters away.

  • Distance. The further the point, the quieter and more muffled the sound, and if desired, a little "space" is added, like in a room.

  • Orbit. The sound itself flies around your head in a chosen shape: circle, oval, figure-eight, spiral, comet, pendulum, free wander. Speed and size are adjustable.

  • Audiobooks. You can open files or an entire folder. The player remembers where you stopped in each chapter.

  • Internet radio. You can add a station via a direct stream link.

  • Conveniences: speed 1–1.5×, sleep timer, radar lock so nothing shifts in your pocket, and a "3D off" button to compare with regular stereo.

  • Skins, like in the good old Winamp. They change the entire look of the player, not just the colors. A skin is a file; you can save it, modify it, and share it with a friend.

How It Works

Everything is built on Web Audio — a sound "constructor" built into any modern browser. No separate server is needed: the player is a single HTML page, and your files never leave your device and play directly on your phone.
