"Life is a disymmetric phenomenon"
V.I. Vernadsky "On Rightness and Leftness" (1940)

Nature fundamentally does not wish to be symmetrical. The first to notice this was not a physicist or cosmologist, but a geochemist from Petersburg—and he noticed it decades before science could prove it.

In 1940, Vladimir Vernadsky wrote a short formula in his work "On Rightness and Leftness": "life is a disymmetric phenomenon." Behind this phrase lies not a poetic metaphor, but a firm assertion. For Vernadsky, dissymmetry means not just the absence of mirror equality, but a directed bias: in living matter, right and left are never balanced. Inert nature contains chiral molecules in equal proportions. The living biosphere does not.

Back in 1940, molecular biology did not yet exist, and elementary particle physics was just approaching its major surprises. Vernadsky approached it from geology and living matter, but hit the very core of modern science. Today, symmetry breaking—from neutrinos to galaxies—is considered one of the main mechanisms generating complexity.

Until the mid-20th century, physicists believed in P-symmetry almost religiously: the laws of nature should not change upon mirror reflection. This seemed not a hypothesis, but a condition of rationality. In 1956, Tsung-Dao Lee and Chen Ning Yang doubted: what if the weak interaction does not conserve parity? A year later, Chien-Shiung Wu conducted an experiment with the beta decay of polarized cobalt-60—and the electrons flew anisotropically. Left and right are distinguishable at the level of fundamental laws. The 1957 Nobel Prize came quickly. And Wolfgang Pauli, who did not believe in such a turn until the very end, uttered a phrase that became an epitaph for the old worldview: "God does not play dice."

And the world was shaken—neutrinos have no mirror twin! All observed neutrinos are left-handed, antineutrinos are right-handed. This is not a sampling error, but a built-in property of the Standard Model. Later, it turned out that combined CP symmetry is also violated—in the decays of neutral kaons and B-mesons. Symmetry turned out to be not the foundation of the universe, but merely a special case of it.

When physics raised its gaze from microparticles to the cosmos—asymmetry dealt another blow. For a billion photons of relic radiation, there is approximately one baryon. There are practically no antibaryons. If the laws of nature were strictly symmetrical, matter and antimatter would have completely annihilated each other, leaving the universe without galaxies, stars, and us. Our existence is direct proof that nature is asymmetric.

In 1967, Andrei Sakharov formulated three conditions under which this bias could have arisen: violation of baryon number, violation of C and CP symmetries, and deviation from thermal equilibrium in the early universe. These conditions remain the cornerstone of cosmology, although the specific mechanism—leptogenesis or electroweak baryogenesis—has not yet been chosen. Asymmetry here is not a metaphor, but a fundamental cosmological constant, expressed by a precise parameter—the ratio of baryons to entropy.

When science descended to the molecular level, the incompatibility of chemical compounds with their mirror images was discovered. Almost all biomolecules turned out to be chiral and incompatible with their mirror forms, like a left and right hand. Louis Pasteur had already noticed this homochirality: in 1848, he separated tartaric acid crystals into two mirror forms and stated that "the universe is a disymmetric whole." Living matter, unlike inanimate nature, possesses chiral purity—with rare exceptions, all proteins consist of left-handed amino acids, and the deoxyribose and ribose sugar residues in DNA and RNA molecules in all organisms have right-handed chirality. The reasons why life chose specifically D-sugars and L-amino acids during evolution remain one of the main mysteries of the origin of life, but this rule is strictly followed throughout the Earth's biosphere.

Francis Crick called the origin of homochirality "the unsolved problem of biogenesis": the random choice of one hand seems completely impossible without a physical mechanism that breaks symmetry. But the mechanism of this evolutionary origin of chiral purity of proteins and nucleic acids is still unclear to us. Modern hypotheses fall into three lines. Either circularly polarized ultraviolet radiation in interstellar space selectively destroys one enantiomer. Or parity violation in electromagnetic interactions—Z-boson exchange—creates a negligible energy difference between L and D forms, which prolonged evolution can amplify. Or autocatalytic reactions like the Soai reaction amplify a small initial asymmetry to complete homochirality.

Vernadsky, in his thoughts, went even further and linked the dissymmetry of life to the special properties of the biosphere's space-time. Today, this is read more as a heuristic metaphor than a physical assertion. But the metaphor works: it drives researchers to seek bridges between biology and fundamental physics.

We are accustomed to considering symmetry a sign of harmony and perfection. But science says the opposite. It is the bias—the creative principle that generates diversity and complexity. Ilya Prigogine wrote that "order and disorder are not opposites, but mutually generate each other"—and fluctuations that break symmetry are fixed in macroscopic structures. Asymmetry is measurable: it is recorded in the helicity of neutrinos, in the baryon excess, in the homochirality of proteins. It is not "difference in general," but a property of structures that affects their dynamics and stability.

Can we go further, if living systems in this logic are amplifiers of chiral asymmetry that maintain bias against the tendency towards racemization?

Let's gather all the facts. Symmetry is not a universal principle that fully defines reality. Symmetries define invariant frameworks, and symmetry breaking determines which state is realized. And if they are realized in all living things, then they are realized not only in physics, chemistry, and biology, but also extend to our very knowledge of the world and our behavior in it, and are reflected in epistemology, sociology, and consequently even economics.

Vernadsky's thought pushes us to an inevitable conclusion—in fact, our very knowledge of reality and our judgments about reality at a very fundamental level exhibit properties of asymmetry, which is directly related to the search for truth and the topic of Yudkowsky's article.

The world is held not by a perfect mirror, but by its crack. Vernadsky saw this crack in living matter seventy years before physics and cosmology confirmed it with their methods. And if Vernadsky is right, then this same crack allows us to distinguish truth from falsehood, and the hallucinations of artificial intelligence from the reliable knowledge of real living human specialists with genuine, not rote-learned, knowledge and experience.

This is a logical continuation of Vernadsky's idea—asymmetry works not only in the structures of the world, but also in the mechanism of selecting true judgments about it. Unlike mutually contradictory subjective opinions about reality, ready to distort it in any direction, reality, although not always clear where it leads, is globally coherent and systematically consistent, which can be technically detected through mathematical asymmetry of correlations in accordance with the coordination theorem:

"In an open system where agents publish claims about the state of the world, and reputation is inherited and updated based on correlation with other agents' claims, the strategy 'strive to be consistent with the maximum number of independent primary evidence' is an evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS), dominating any strategy of pure fiction, provided that reality introduces asymmetry into the probabilities of correlations."

Even a very large number of confirming facts for a particular statement obtained through inductive generalization makes it only highly probable, but not absolutely certain. At the same time, one reliably proven refuting fact is enough to discard such an inductive generalization as invalid. Karl Popper formulated this unequal "strength" and role in verifying the meaningfulness and truth of scientific theories, which is characteristic of confirming and refuting factors, as the principle of falsifiability and called it "cognitive asymmetry."

Based on this "asymmetry," the inevitability of replacing the "principle of verification (i.e., positive verification, in other words, confirmation), proclaimed by logical positivists, with the principle of 'falsification' (i.e., equally real and measurable refutation) became obvious. It means that the verification of the meaningfulness of arguments, and then the truth of knowledge or hypotheses, should be carried out not by searching for facts that confirm them, but primarily (or even exclusively) by searching for facts that refute them.

This approach is fundamental in the scientific world, but for some unknown reason, it has not been implemented in information technology anywhere to date. And it is very strange that Yudkowsky seems to have forgotten about it and did not mention it at all in his essay.

Popper arrived at this criterion while reflecting on the problem of induction. No matter how many white swans we see, it will not prove that all swans are white; but one black swan definitively refutes this statement. The asymmetry between confirmation and refutation here is fundamental—confirming cases are always open to future exceptions, whereas a reliably proven refuting case definitively closes the issue. Hence, Popper's famous scheme of the growth of knowledge: problem → tentative hypothesis → attempt at refutation → new problem. Science moves not by accumulating confirmations, but by the survival of hypotheses that could not be refuted, despite serious attempts.

This is not mathematical logic, but game theory. It is not semantics or linguistics, which have failed their mission of understanding meaning, nor the "opinion of the majority," whose verification is manipulated by scammers and populists using biased administrators, hired experts, special content curators, corporate oracles, state-issued certificates, hallucinating and biased artificial intelligence algorithms, clickbait likes/dislikes, falsifiable votes, or tokens that can bribe any user.

It is precisely this stance that makes Popper's criterion so valuable today. In an era when confirmations are cheap and generated by algorithms faster than a person can read them, the discipline of refutation becomes not a philosophical luxury, but a condition for maintaining connection with reality. The criterion of falsifiability is not a guarantee of truth, but a guarantee that we will not accept as truth what cannot even be imagined as false.

Survival under the pressure of refutations, not the number of coincidences, is the measure of epistemic robustness, and therefore the algorithmic implementation of Popper's falsifiability criterion, thanks to the coordination theorem, is an epistemic machine that mathematically guarantees the priority of reality in the long game.
