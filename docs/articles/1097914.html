News Feed

Journalistic text auto-generation was invented long before neural networks. In 2006, Thomson Reuters started with simple natural language generation algorithms to write news about business quarterly reports.

With the development of these algorithms, they learned to automate news about sports, traffic jams, warnings about dangerous natural phenomena (there was a story about a post 3 minutes after tremors) and comments on stock quotes to varying degrees. It was possible to algorithmize writing a note based on any structured information or obvious trigger. Interestingly, at that stage, the share of automatic content did not interest many people.

Of course, in the era of neural networks, there is much more generated news. By the end of summer 2025, 9% of articles and news in American newspapers were fully or partially generated by AI. Most often, technology news and weather reports were written this way. Moreover, in these articles, fictional statements were encountered 8.2 times more often than in human texts.

The second interesting conclusion from the same study is that in opinion pieces written by prominent public figures, generated content was encountered 6.4 times more often than in regular articles of the same publications.

In Russia, from March to May 2025, among 19 leading news publications, either 3 or 4 were found to contain generated content (more than the threshold chosen by the researchers). The discrepancy in the figures is not accidental – the work indicates 18% of publications, but this does not yield a whole number from 19 platforms.

More recent data is available from the Netherlands. In September 2026, researchers checked 252 opinion pieces in major daily newspapers and found that 49 of them were fully generated, and 57 were partially generated.

Amusingly, a prepared reader sometimes rates AI news more highly. I emphasize preparation for a reason – the experiment was conducted at a Chinese university among journalism students, and their assessments may differ from those of ordinary people. Students preferred AI news about nature and society: thanks to the relevance of the headline, adequacy of topic coverage, readability, as well as punctuation and sentence structure (these are all criteria from the study). AI has partly learned to imitate humans well. However, human texts were rated higher for perceived factual accuracy and use of sources.

These assessments reflect the essence of an author's work. Journalism has never been just about producing texts. Letters and words are just the visible part. Humans still have an advantage in the invisible part: they act in the real world, find sources, negotiate with speakers, and obtain new evidence. So, we are still left with investigations, working with confidential data and sources, emotional reports from the scene requiring contact with participants, verifying controversial facts and describing points of view, as well as tracing cause-and-effect relationships.

Books

There are more myths surrounding the abundance of generated books and the commercial effect of their release than in the field of journalism. The favorite fairy tale of infobusinessmen is that a novice decided to generate books and wrote a bestseller. Entrepreneur Tommi Pedruzzi publicly boasted that he earned $3 million from generated books. But it turned out that he received income mainly not from books, but from courses on "How to make money on AI books.".

No data was found on Russian self-publishing – only reports from services that neural sludge does not dominate (it would be strange for them to claim otherwise).

However, for the English-language market, there are more detailed results. A group of researchers analyzed 14,419 books published on Amazon KDP (a self-publishing service) from 2023 to 2026 and compared them with sales statistics. It turned out that 20% of the analyzed volume consists of books where more than a quarter was written by AI. Nearly 1,000 books were generated by 90% or more. However, the sales share of this content is small.

The study's authors found that the best-selling books among those (at least partially) generated by AI borrow more characteristic language constructions from existing human-created literature. Here's a business idea: want to make money? Write a second or even a third volume of "Dead Souls," or even a fifth volume of "War and Peace," imitating the author.

In some categories, the share of generated books is much higher. In the topic of herbal remedies and folk medicine – the Herbal Remedies category on Amazon – by 2025, 82% of them were already generated (publicly available fragments of books published from January 1 to September 30, 2025, were analyzed). The category bestseller is written by a non-existent author who heads a fictional organization. At the same time, the book refers to herbalists-experts with no digital footprint, as well as to articles in a never-published magazine.

Self-help is another "popular" topic for AI. Originality.ai analyzed 844 books in the "Success" subcategory on Amazon (part of the "Self-Help" genre), published from August 31 to November 28, 2025. 77% of them were highly likely written by AI. 12% of this volume was published by only 4% of authors – these are the ones who have thoroughly integrated neural networks into their work.

AI allows for faster writing, even if the text is not fully generated. The monthly output of books from 2022 to 2025 tripled. Book market revenues are not growing as fast – this reduces the average revenue per book in most genres, and consequently, reduces the income of human authors. AI is displacing people from the market – even though readers quickly identify generation.

It seems that traditional publishing with its editing is transforming from a barrier and a toll collector from poor authors into a defender of the reader (and the good name of the human author) from the flood of neural sludge. This will likely be the survival strategy for everyone: authors will have to cooperate with publishers so that, leveraging their reputation, they grant them access to an audience tired of the flood of generations.

Photos

Photography is a bit different from text. Stock photos have long filled corporate websites, pages of various courses, and news publications. By 2020, the stock photo market volume had grown to billions of dollars, while the stock text market had always been practically zero.

Neural networks have started to take a bite out of the revenue of photo stocks. According to analysts, this is the only consistently declining segment of commercial photography. Wedding, reportage, or fashion photography has been affected much less.

Due to how easy it is to get the desired illustration from ChatGPT, the volume of generated content is enormous. In June 2025, researchers from AI Forensics studied the output of TikTok and Instagram for 13 hashtags in Spain, Germany, and Poland. For each tag, they manually checked the top 30 search results. On average, in TikTok, 25% contained AI-generated images, and in Instagram, 4.9%. Moreover, over 80% of the detected synthetic images looked photorealistic.

It's not a given that all these pictures would have been taken by a photographer if neural networks weren't available. Sometimes the content creator didn't intend to go to a photographer – they needed an illustration to attract attention to other content (an article/presentation/banner). Such pictures could be obtained for free.

However, overall, the same thing is happening in the stock photo market as in book publishing. Neural sludge increases the volume of sold works, and although it attracts less attention, photographers and illustrators have to compete with it.

Furthermore, generated photographs allow for manipulation of history. If it will be immediately clear that Tolstoy did not write the fifth volume of "War and Peace," then the electronic watch added to a portrait of Marie Curie might be overlooked. It's as if AI erases the line between a photograph as evidence of an event and an image that merely illustrates a text.

The authors of the SIMODS Report 2026 (on disinformation) analyzed misleading content on major platforms. The share of disinformation publications with AI images or videos was about 6% on TikTok and about 2.3% on YouTube. However, the assessment is indirect (the share of disinformation on TikTok was estimated at 25%, and in 24% of this amount – publications with generated images).

Music

As with journalistic texts, algorithms created music long before neural networks. It was enough to take pre-recorded sounds and patterns and combine them randomly or according to some rule. But with the advent of generative AI, it became much easier to create complete tracks, leading to the same content overproduction as in other areas.

There are no statistics for the entire internet, of course. One can judge the share of neural sludge only by data from streaming services. Deezer reports that in June 2026, AI music accounted for over half of new uploads on some days – about 90,000 tracks per day. There seem to be fewer neural tracks on Russian services – possibly because not everyone who wants to has accessed the leading generative models. Reporting on the start of AI content labeling, "Yandex.Music" states 4.4% of the catalog and 27% of new uploads.

As in other areas, AI musicians adhere to the strategy of "shooting at a flock of sparrows with a ten-barrel gun," creating a mass of roughly identical tracks and hoping one of them will hit. In some segments, the strategy works – deepfakes or successful remixes unexpectedly go viral. An old example is the track "Heart on My Sleeve" by Ghostwriter.

However, commercial success is unlikely here. The copyright situation in the music industry is quite strict – the author of that composition did not actually receive any real money. Playbacks were frozen due to complaints from Universal Music Group (UMG). How profitable other examples are for their creators is unknown.

On the other hand, it is already clear that precisely because of strict copyright laws and the fact that generated music is in a gray area, AI tracks are widely used as background music for non-musical videos. But this often doesn't bother the user – what difference does it make where the background music comes from when discussing the latest tech models or nuances of UAZ car repair? It would seem to be a blow to the stock music market. But it's not a given that all these bloggers would have bought compositions (instead of leaving the sound of a garage as background).

Overall, the problem in the music industry is the same as in the book industry. At first glance, the situation is regulated naturally: generated content is listened to less than human content. But still, neural tracks dilute the overall volume of attention, reducing the earnings of human authors. To maintain income, musicians have to invest almost more effort in marketing than in creating a track.

The Battle for Attention

In all the described segments, the supply of content is rapidly increasing. But a book that is published but no one reads, like a track that is uploaded but no one plays, only increases download statistics. This content may not change audience habits at all. Some Vladimir Ivanovich (coincidence with real people is accidental) continues to read his favorite Conan Doyle and listen to Mozart, no matter how much neural sludge is uploaded to the network.

The nuance is that Conan Doyle and Mozart have already solved their promotion issues. But it is much harder for a new author to enter the market. Competition for attention has intensified.

There is another side to the story. Models are trained on human works, for which authors do not always receive compensation. Thus, disputes about copyright arise not only because of another deepfake or a too-recognizable copy of someone else's style, but also around the training of models themselves. How correct is it to use copyrighted work to produce materials that compete with the originals?
