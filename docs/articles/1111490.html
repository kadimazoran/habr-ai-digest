Globally, up to 3.5 million scientific papers are published annually, registered in international databases like Scopus. This excludes national journals not indexed internationally but searchable. China alone contributes about 1 million articles yearly. In 2010, the global output was around 2 million articles per year. Over the last 15 years, scientific content production has increased by over 65%.

But this is across all sciences, you might say. Let's take neurobiology of the brain as an example, including research on brain fMRI, brain-computer interfaces, cognitive brain functions, and others. Up to 100,000 articles are published annually on this topic. To read them all, you would need to read 274 articles every day without days off. If you spend at least 30 minutes on each article, you would need 137 hours in a day. It is not surprising that the proportion of scientific articles that remain uncited in the humanities and social sciences reaches 80%.

Over the past 20 years, the number of scientists in the world has increased 1.5 times, from 6 to 9 million. And fivefold over 50 years. Back in the 70s, there were only 1.5 million scientists worldwide. This explosion is due to technological development becoming a method of competition between states in the global market. Technological dominance creates prerequisites for state dominance, as we see in the example of AI development.

Recently, the growth in the number of articles has accelerated due to the emergence of chatbots, which an increasing number of scientists use to write articles. Such a volume of articles cannot be peer-reviewed, so peer review is also carried out using large language models in an increasing number of journals. But LLMs tend to approve articles written by themselves or similar models. As a result, an LLM filter emerges – those who did not write an article using an LLM have a lower chance of passing peer review. And then new LLMs learn from articles approved by their predecessors and published. Thus, we fall into an LLM information bubble, where it writes and approves its own content,

With the physical growth in the number of articles, scientific progress is stagnating on what has already been achieved and simply rephrased. Reading articles increasingly evokes a sense of déjà vu. In such conditions, the emergence of a new theory is practically impossible. There is no mind that could sift through this stream of simulacra. Therefore, we see fewer and fewer original new theories that revolutionize science.
