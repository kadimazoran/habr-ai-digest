Why Cubes at All

Modern video models, like Seedance 2.5 and MiniMax Hailuo, produce images beyond praise. But their directing skills are poor. You write a prompt:

"The camera slowly rises from under the ground, focuses on an eagle, and dives after it towards a field."

– the model will try its best, but execute it in its own way. There will be an eagle, there will be a field, but that specific fly-through might be missing. And you're left to re-generate. The fifth take, the tenth – it's like explaining directions, and they keep taking wrong turns because "it's faster that way.".

In filmmaking, this was solved long before neural networks – with previz. Before shooting, the scene is blocked out with gray primitives: where the camera is, where it's going, where the actors are. Simple placeholders are used to agree on the mise-en-scène (arrangement of actors/objects/decorations) without getting into details.

For generative video, this technique is needed even more.

How It Works

Multimodal models, such as Seedance 2.5 and MiniMax Hailuo H3, accept video references. If explained that the reference only sets the camera, timing, and placement, while the appearance is taken from text and images, the model replicates the camerawork almost one-to-one.

The scenario is simple: add the "3D Scene" node to the canvas, describe what should happen in the chat (you can manually adjust the result if the assistant misunderstood), press render, and connect the outputs to the video model – video as a reference, prompt as an instruction.

You can add reference images of characters. The model will take their appearance from these, and the movement from the previz.

It might seem that 3D is something very complex, but it's not the case here. The scene is assembled by an assistant based on your text, and you only need to move something manually if the model misunderstood slightly.

What's Inside the Node

The scene is stored like a regular document: duration, background, objects, cameras, lights, cut list. Objects consist of four primitives: cube, sphere, pyramid, and group. The group is invisible; it's a pivot: its children have local coordinates, so the tractor made of a chassis, cabin, and wheels moves as one unit, and the eagle doesn't lose its wing during a turn.

Animation involves time-based keyframes for position, rotation, and scale; for cameras, also for point of view and field of view. It's edited with a mouse: gizmos, an object tree with an inspector, a timeline, undo/redo.

A crucial architectural point: the state of the scene at any given time is calculated by the same function, which is used by the viewport preview, gizmos, and offline rendering to file. What is visible during playback is guaranteed to match the render result (because it cannot be otherwise in principle).

We decided to render on the client side, not the server – the scene is lightweight, and the server is busy with generation. Frames are drawn by a WebGL renderer deterministically.

The fast path is WebCodecs: a frame from the canvas is converted into a VideoFrame and sent to the hardware H.264 encoder, ffmpeg.wasm then just packages the stream into mp4. Eight seconds in 720p are encoded in seconds. The fallback path for browsers without WebCodecs uses PNG frames in chunks via ffmpeg.wasm.

A couple of pitfalls:

  • raw RGBA is not suitable (30 seconds at 30 fps is already 3.3 GB of memory);

  • ffmpeg.wasm has one instance per tab; the scene renderer and video editor share it via a common lock;

  • the WebGL context limit in browsers is around sixteen – after rendering, the context must be explicitly "lost," otherwise the viewport stops drawing.

The Main Battle: How to Make the Model Speak Concisely

If you had asked me at the beginning of the project what was harder – 3D graphics or communicating with LLMs, I would have bet on graphics. I was wrong.

The first version of the assistant worked straightforwardly: we put the JSON schema and the current scene into the prompt, asking it to return the entire new document. It turned out to be slow: for the task "a person enters a room and lies on a bed," the response was about 9500 tokens and a minute and a half, and "move the bed to the left" cost as much as generating the scene from scratch. It was also unreliable: the model regularly broke long JSONs with hundreds of numbers – losing a comma, renaming a key. Plus, without hierarchy, the head detached from the body during rotation, the door rotated through the wall… you get the idea.

We set up a test stand with test tasks, automatic evaluation using geometric heuristics, and token counting – and started changing the format.

Step 1. Text instead of JSON. One line per entity, animation keys as indented strings, defaults are not written:

scene dur=8 bg=#1b1d24
box floor "Пол" #8a8a8a p=0,-0.05,0 s=6,0.1,6
group car "Машина" #b03030 p=-5.3,0,0
box car_body "Кузов" #b03030 parent=car p=0,0.6,0 s=4,0.8,1.8
  @0 p=-5.3,0,0
  @6 p=5,0,0 ease=easeInOut
cam cam_1 "Общая" p=6,3,8 t=0,1,0 fov=50
cut 0 cam_1

The response became two to three times shorter, and the parser forgives what JSON wouldn't. Plus, such a response can be applied line by line during streaming – the scene is assembled as it's typed.

Step 2. Patches instead of a document. The model responds only with changes: a line with a known identifier replaces an entity, with a new one – adds it, a separate command deletes it. "Move the bed" turned into a single line instead of rewriting the entire document.

Step 3. Macros. The most interesting solution. The model struggles with geometry calculations but understands what needs to be done excellently. We extracted typical constructions into macros that regular code expands: a room with a door, a person with a pivot at their feet, "walk along points," "lie on the bed," "camera with a clear line of sight." The model writes one line, and it doesn't calculate furniture traversal or camera angles.

Result: the response shrunk to 300 tokens – 30 times less than the original JSON, 12 times cheaper, 13 times faster. Quality according to the stand's heuristics increased from "sometimes" to 10 out of 10. A rare case where optimization is simultaneously faster, cheaper, and higher quality.

Step 4. Lint and one retry. The remaining errors are caught by geometric checks after applying the response: an object under the floor, a camera through a wall. The remarks are sent back to the model in one short request, and one retry is almost always enough.

Another nuance: reasoning models think carefully before responding, but for assembly from ready-made macros, long deliberations are not needed. A low level of thinking effort further reduces consumption several times without loss of quality.

This economy determines the price: a scene via the assistant costs from 10 to 100 ₽. Moreover, rendering the 3D template to mp4 is free – it happens on your computer.

The Prompt for the Video Model is Included

Previz looks like colored cubes on a plain background. Give it to the model without explanation – it will do video-to-video: "improve" the cubes and return the same cubes, just prettier.

Therefore, the node has a second, text output, which contains instructions on how the video should actually be interpreted:

  • ready instruction – take only the camera, shots, timing, and object placement from the video;

  • consider each primitive a placeholder for a real object;

  • do not consider the primitive's color as the object's color.

The wording was chosen based on Seedance 2.5 and Hailuo H3 (both have a convention for explicitly assigning the role of the reference video).

What's interesting is that long lists of prohibitions worked worse than a short positive instruction: "take camerawork from the reference, objects and atmosphere from the description" (models, like people, seem to react better to positive formulations).

Ultimately, the user adds their scene description and character references to the instruction.

Small Things That Turned Out to Be Important

Several technical solutions that the user barely notices, but without which working with the node would be significantly less convenient.

Gradual Scene Update.

The assistant's response is applied immediately as it streams. If the generation ends with an error, changes can be fully rolled back; a successful edit is added to the general undo, like a regular editor action.

Correct Pivot for Groups.

When grouping objects, the editor places the rotation point at the center of the group and converts child elements to local coordinates. Thanks to this, the position of objects in the world is preserved, and the gizmo is directly near them, not somewhere at the beginning of the scene.

Cache Control.

Since the workflow can reuse results from already executed nodes, outdated result indicators have been specifically defined for the 3D scene. Otherwise, after changes, the node might continue to serve an old render.

Shared Code for Client and Server.

The parser, macros, linter, validator, and video model prompt are extracted into a single package. This prevents situations where the browser interprets the scene one way, and the server checks it according to different rules.

What This Changes

The 3D Scene node has appeared as another node alongside image and video generation, LLM nodes, frame extraction, and a video editor with a timeline – capabilities close to CapCut. Previously, the scene was assembled in one service, references were prepared in another, the video was generated in a third, editing in a fourth, and files constantly moved between tools like trains on a schedule. Now, it's all on one screen, and every step can be redone without exporting files!

Higgsfield and we came to the same conclusion almost simultaneously: the next step in controlling video models is not in prompts, but in geometry. A model replicates what it's shown more accurately than what it's described. Logically: it's easier to show even a person than to explain – remember any attempt to dictate directions over the phone when there's a map available.
