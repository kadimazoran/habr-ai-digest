SOC analysts—whether they work in a large team or a small one—regularly tell me that they see all the alerts from correlation rules but don't have time to investigate them. And this doesn't mean they aren't working well. A SOC in a large organization registers over 10,000 alerts per day, and it's physically impossible to manually gather context, consult a knowledge base, and make a decision for each one. In such conditions, almost two-thirds of alerts remain uninvestigated, mostly those assigned low significance levels. This allows an attacker to "blend into the background." When we at Positive Technologies simulated insider behavior in a corporate infrastructure, a traditional system built on signatures and correlation rules generated only three alerts with medium and low significance levels.

To avoid missing important events and correctly assign risk levels in similar situations, MaxPatrol SIEM has a built-in behavioral analysis module, MaxPatrol BAD. However, the mere fact that an event wasn't lost in the stream doesn't remove the analyst's need to understand it. It is at this point—between "the system found something" and "the specialist understood what it is"—that lies the gap we decided to close with AI.

When implementing AI in processes, a significant portion of the market relies on connecting ready-made integrations from third-party vendors. This approach has its advantages, primarily the ability to bypass the need for in-house expertise. For tasks where AI does not handle sensitive data, this approach is justified.

We consciously chose a different path. Primarily due to the nature of the data that SIEM processes. Essentially, it's a complete picture of what's happening in the client's infrastructure: accounts, processes, network activity, incidents. Sending this to a third party is a decision we didn't consider for ourselves: for a company that creates security tools, client trust in where their data is physically located is not an option, but the foundation of the product.

Therefore, we went to a deeper level: an LLM deployed within our environment and our own hardware in our data centers. At the same time, we approach model selection responsibly. We regularly test current open-source models specifically on our tasks—analyzing processes, executed commands, correlations—and select the one that shows the best results for them. Then, we equip it with Positive Technologies' expertise: about what legitimate and illegitimate activity looks like, about the specifics of MaxPatrol SIEM. This, in my opinion, is our real differentiator: not just our own infrastructure, but also experience at the intersection of AI and information security.

The approach we chose determined the solution's architecture: MaxPatrol SIEM operates on-premise at the client's location, while the AI assistant itself is in Positive Technologies' secure cloud, with which MaxPatrol SIEM exchanges data via a controlled channel.

Practical Examples

The easiest way to demonstrate PT Naira's work is through specific examples—the very ones we tested with real analysts.

The interface remains familiar. You open MaxPatrol SIEM, the event list, the event card—everything as usual. The difference is that the event card now has an assistant menu: you can select a pre-made command ("explain event," "describe process") or simply type a question in the free chat.

Let's start with the routine that consumes the most time—obfuscated scripts. A classic scenario: something like hex encoding on the outside, base64 on the inside. Any specialist can deobfuscate this themselves, but it takes minutes for each script. An analyst might encounter many such alerts in a day, and minutes add up to hours. Here's an example of an obfuscated malicious script:

eval $(printf '
power' 
'shell') 
-Command "$([Text.Encoding]::UTF8.GetString([Convert]::FromBase64String('R2V0LVByb2Nlc3MgTFNBU1MgfCBPdXQtTnVsbA==')))"

The assistant performs deobfuscation and explains what's happening in the command line in seconds—in our case, it turned out to be an attempt to access LSASS via PowerShell, a fairly clear indicator of credential theft.

But if you ask to explain not the process itself, but the context around it—the launch path, the parent process chain—the picture changes: launch from a temporary directory, double shell nesting. A legitimate utility turns out to be a Living off the Land tool that an attacker uses to bypass defenses.

The same applies to correlations that an analyst encounters for the first time. For example, an alert with an unfamiliar name—the assistant explains what technique it is, what attacker actions are behind it, what the next step might be—and, most importantly, how to distinguish a real attack from a false positive.

Now let's return to the insider case from the first section. To remind you: MaxPatrol BAD issued an alert with a risk assessment of "100" and a dozen triggered behavioral models. It's possible to analyze such a card manually—but these are the same minutes per event I mentioned earlier.

This is where the synergy of tools comes into play: MaxPatrol BAD finds anomalies that signature rules miss, and PT Naira breaks them down into an understandable story: it explains the process launch chain, provides a suspicion score for each step, and, if the activity truly resembles an attack, offers recommendations on what to do next.

Another aspect is connecting new event sources. When raw JSON events arrive in SIEM, they need to be parsed into fields—a normalization rule needs to be written. Resources for this are always limited: it's impossible to cover every source with rules manually. The assistant first explains what happened in the event and what the key entities are—this helps decide whether to collect such a source in SIEM at all.

If yes, context is automatically pulled from the event, and the analyst only needs to fill in a few fields—vendor, title, category. Then the system generates a rule itself, and after a few minutes, you have a working draft that needs minor manual refinement. What used to take days, and sometimes months, now takes tens of minutes.

And one more detail that turned out to be unexpectedly useful in practice is the query history. An analyst can return to what they asked yesterday or run multiple normalization tasks in parallel and attend to other matters while they are running in the background.

When we launched testing, our expectations were quite modest. In reality, PT Naira allowed us to halve the investigation time and reduce the time to write normalization rules by an order of magnitude. The latter metric surprised even us.

It might seem that a normalization rule is a technical trifle: connect a source, parse JSON into fields. But if you've ever done it in practice, you know it's not a trifle. It takes time, expertise, and colleagues who understand a specific event format—and they are almost always busy with something else. It is this dependency on others' time and attention that disappears when the assistant prepares a draft rule in minutes, rather than waiting weeks in a queue for someone else's expertise.

During the preliminary testing, over 250 analysts asked PT Naira more than 3500 queries. This confirmed that the assistant is actively used, but we wanted to understand user impressions in more detail. During interviews with test participants, we heard that PT Naira has found its place in the daily work of analysts. People didn't just note that the assistant helps with script analysis, but described their feelings: "no need to switch between tabs," "completing a rule is easier than writing from scratch," "it's immediately clear what requires more attention."

PT Naira does not take away the analyst's job and responsibility. The final decision—whether to investigate an incident further or close it as a false positive, to accept a normalization rule or refine it—still rests with the human. And, in my opinion, this is the correct place for AI in SOC: not instead of the analyst, but between them and the volume of routine tasks that previously took time away from decision-making.

What You Need to Make It Work for You

PT Naira is available in MaxPatrol SIEM starting with version 27.6. Therefore, the first step is to update to this version or higher. Next, you need access to update.ptsecurity.ru—this is how data is exchanged between your on-premise environment and the cloud part of the assistant, which I wrote about in the second section.

Second, a license. You need to obtain and update it within the product.

Third, download and install the customizable event card template. It is through this that the assistant menu appears in the interface and access to pre-made commands ("explain event," "describe process," etc.) becomes available, as discussed in the third section.

It's worth noting separately that we deliberately did not tie the assistant to the main release cycle of MaxPatrol SIEM. Updates to the card and assistant logic can be released more frequently, and new features and improvements in response quality don't need to wait for the next product release. For teams that carefully plan infrastructure upgrades, this separation significantly simplifies their lives.

Conclusion

The gap between system detection and analyst understanding cannot be bridged by increasing SOC staff or adding another layer of correlation rules. Staff doesn't grow proportionally to the number of alerts, and rules, by definition, only see what they can already describe.

What has truly changed over these months is not that the analyst is working less. What has changed is what their time is spent on. Searching for information in knowledge bases, manual script deobfuscation, drafting normalization rules from scratch—this is routine that used to consume hours for each investigation. Now it takes seconds or minutes, and hours are freed up for what the analyst is actually needed for: to determine if it's truly an attack and decide what to do next.

It's important to realistically assess PT Naira's capabilities. The assistant does not investigate incidents or write final rules—it handles the first, most labor-intensive layer of work and provides the human with prepared context. The decision—to close an alert as false positive or escalate, to accept a normalization rule or refine it—still rests with the analyst. And, frankly, I don't see anything here that needs changing: AI's place here is not instead of expertise, but alongside it, where previously there was only volume.
