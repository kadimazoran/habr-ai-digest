On my datasets, this corrected every fifth error (WER dropped from 27.0% to 21.7%).

The experiments resulted in a free Mac application that records meetings and transcribes audio and video to text locally. I'll explain how I chose the models, what I measured, and which obvious solutions didn't work. The article includes a prompt for an agent to build it yourself.

Why I Was Looking Only for Local Transcription

The standard solution: search for "meeting transcription" and choose a service. There's no shortage of options. The problem is understanding how they differ.

I immediately limited my search to local applications. My meetings discuss budgets, HR decisions, contract terms, and internal company issues. Not every C-level meeting can be sent to an external service.

I know many people send such recordings to Claude or ChatGPT. But I'm not comfortable sending both the conversations themselves and the participants' voices there. Therefore, the requirement was simple: the audio and transcription must remain only on my laptop.

It seems like all applications are structured the same way: they take an open-source model, add an interface, and offer a subscription. There are a few additional features: sound recording, speaker diarization, handling long files. I'll get to that.

The list of familiar models within different programs indeed repeats. MacWhisper, Buzz, and SuperWhisper use Whisper. Handy supports not only Whisper but also Parakeet.

In fact, I was going to just buy SuperWhisper Pro. I remember the price was around $85 for a year, but the payment didn't go through. Perhaps if the purchase had succeeded, I would have stopped there. As it is, I had to figure things out.

I started with Whisper, which is the base. It produced understandable text but made noticeable errors in my Russian-language meetings and was slow. For some tasks, this quality is sufficient. I wanted better.

After reading Habr and Deep Research with Claude, Gemini, and ChatGPT, the main candidates for me were Whisper in its large-v2, large-v3, and turbo versions, Parakeet TDT v3, and GigaAM-v3. Later, for the sake of experiment purity, I added Breeze-ASR, Qwen3-ASR, and other models.

Connecting the model itself turned out to be the easiest part. Even local agents can create a prototype that takes a file and returns text. But a working prototype doesn't answer the main question: which model to choose and why?

I had to answer that myself, and it took significantly more time than the code.

First, I Chose Models by Eye

I ran recordings of my meetings and read the transcripts. I had to code a diff tool to compare texts more easily. My initial impressions were:

  • GigaAM hears normal Russian speech well. I like the punctuation. But it started having problems with technical terms, product names, and IT slang. The text looked coherent, but it wasn't always possible to grasp the essence of the conversation.

  • Parakeet heard technical terms and English slang significantly better. The final text looked worse; the model is multilingual and not as specialized for Russian as GigaAM, but you could almost always restore the meaning from its text.

Both models worked quickly.

  • Whisper seemed very attentive to some details. Sometimes it preserved what Parakeet and GigaAM missed. But it worked slower, and in some places, it added phrases that weren't in the recording. Its quality varied greatly. The result didn't seem stable, but it was sometimes impressive.

These were impressions from analyzing my transcripts (an archive of 36 hours of meetings).

At that point, I chose Parakeet. For my recordings, it seemed like a reasonable compromise between speed, accuracy, and preserving technical terms.

For a while, this suited me. Then I wanted to improve the quality, and it became clear: without measurements, I would just be choosing the text I liked more. Metrics were needed!

How I Calculated Errors and Speed

The main metric, WER, calculates substitutions, deletions, and insertions relative to the reference. The lower the value, the better. Before comparison, I converted the text to lowercase, replaced 'ё' with 'е', and removed punctuation. "Клод" instead of Claude was considered an error. Punctuation was not evaluated at all by this check.

Audio was fed in chunks of up to 20 seconds, segmented by pauses. For Whisper, I used Silero VAD to separate speech from silence. Measurements were taken on an Intel Core i9 without GPU acceleration.

Why WER Alone Isn't Enough

After the measurements, it became clearer why the table and text impressions sometimes diverged.

WER doesn't know which word is important for the conversation. Replacing a system name and replacing the word "well" both count as one error. But for a human, the consequences are different. Without the name or term, it's sometimes impossible to understand the essence of the conversation.

There's also the opposite situation. The model wrote "эластик" instead of Elastic. For a strict comparison, this is an error. For a reader who knows the product, the meaning might be preserved.

I wanted to separate spelling errors from cases where the model genuinely distorted the word. Therefore, I added my own heuristic and called it Sound-based WER (WERx).

It works with text, not by listening to audio. Latin characters are converted to a Cyrillic phonetic key, and soft signs, spaces, and hyphens are removed. For example, Cloud-MD and "клауд мд" get the same key. Then, the similarity of keys or their consonants is compared. However, two ordinary Russian words must match exactly: "пропадал" and "пропадает" do not become the same word.

This is not a universal phonetic metric nor an evaluation of meaning preservation. It's needed for the question: how many errors are related to similar sounds being recorded differently?

Thus, I developed two different requirements. I need a model that handles normal Russian conversation well. And I need a way to preserve the terms (anglicisms/slang) on which this model makes mistakes.

A Cascade of Two Models: GigaAM and Parakeet

I liked GigaAM's Russian text and how Parakeet handled terms (+ it's 4 times faster). I wanted to combine their results.

I took GigaAM's transcript as the base. Parakeet recognized the same audio independently. Then I aligned the two texts, found discrepancies, and checked for possible substitutions.

I took the variant from Parakeet if it contained Latin characters and was sufficiently similar in sound to the corresponding GigaAM fragment. The rest of the text remained unchanged.

For example, this allowed replacing "апкид" with AppKit, "Sweeft" with Swift, "Промиус" with Prometheus. But errors also occurred: "диф" could turn into div, and "клот" into Cloud instead of Claude. Similar sound doesn't always mean a correct substitution.

The main parameter became the similarity threshold. With a low threshold, the cascade more readily accepted corrections; with a high threshold, it skipped more candidates.

On dense IT text, the cascade yielded to Parakeet alone. However, compared to GigaAM, it reduced WER by 11 percentage points and increased the proportion of accurately spelled terms from 19% to 44%.

This came at the cost of a second recognition pass. The cascade achieved an RTF of about 0.3 on my Intel. For the recognition itself, this is about 18 minutes per hour of audio.

Why GigaAM Became the Base, Not Parakeet

Before the cascade, I used Parakeet. So, I also tested the reverse approach: keeping its text and correcting uncertain words using GigaAM. This scheme did not outperform GigaAM alone.

And an additional factor: the main part of the conversation consists of ordinary Russian speech. On this, GigaAM showed better results on my data. It should be trusted with the base, and the second model should be tasked with correcting inaccuracies.

This doesn't mean GigaAM should be the base for all transcriptions. For recordings with a dense stream of English terms, Parakeet alone was better than this cascade.

What Didn't Work

A Term Dictionary Corrected Familiar Errors and Created New Ones

The first idea was obvious: create a substitution dictionary. The model writes "клот," I replace it with Claude. It writes "кафка," I replace it with Kafka.

A dictionary compiled from short IT recordings improved the result on those same recordings by 6 to 13 percentage points. But on a long recording that wasn't used to create the dictionary, the gain was only 0.3 to 0.5 points. Correcting already known errors turned out to be easier than new ones.

Then I took a large dictionary of 8,130 terms and ran it through the archive. "Нас" turned into NAS 1,246 times, "нет" into .NET 864 times. On IT texts, the dictionary removed 94 errors, while on meetings, it added 24.

A filter based on word frequencies on Habr helped: don't make a substitution if the Cyrillic form is used no less often than the Latin one. After cleaning, the number of matches in the archive decreased from 3,175 to 449. This means the problem wasn't with the dictionary itself, but with unconditional substitutions without considering ambiguity.

Overlapping Audio Fragments Didn't Improve Whole Recordings

Models received audio in chunks of up to 20 seconds. Errors were more frequent at the boundaries of chunks than in the middle. It seemed logical to add a two-second overlap so the model would re-hear the beginning and end.

On 74 short fragments, WER decreased by 0.4 percentage points. On whole recordings, it increased from 30.8% to 31.3%. The improvement on the short test didn't hold up on the long one.

It later turned out that words near a normal pause, where there was no cut, were also recognized worse. I attributed the problem to segmentation, although a significant part of it was related to phrase boundaries.

Prompts Improved One Type of Speech and Ruined Another

I tried prompting Whisper on how to transcribe English terms. For large-v3 on IT test, WER decreased from 59.8% to 58.1%. However, on meetings, it increased from 27.1% to 66.7%.

A prompt cannot be evaluated solely on the examples for which it was added. In my task, the model had to handle both terminology and the rest of the conversation.

A Smarter Metric Didn't Improve the Cascade

I trained a metric to better distinguish between similar variants from GigaAM and Parakeet. Its own AUC score increased from 0.926 to 0.941. The cascade's WER did not improve.

In this setup, even an ideal choice among the considered substitutions could only yield about 0.3 percentage points on meetings. Useful and harmful substitutions were often equally similar in sound. Similarity alone was not enough to determine the correct answer.

I also tried considering Parakeet's confidence. On meetings, this reduced WER by 0.2 points; on IT speech, it added the same amount. For my pipeline variant, there was no significant gain.

How I Verified Corrections on Real Meetings

A single final percentage is not enough. I needed to see which words were changing and why. After that, everything had to be checked on an archive of 230,000 words. This took about 250 hours of background processing.

I made the folder with transcripts a git repository. Each processing step was saved as a separate commit. In the diff, I could see the substitution along with adjacent lines.

This is how errors were discovered that are easily missed by average WER. VPN turned into SPN. "Квик" became Quick. "НПР1" was shortened to NPR and lost the digit.

For a work conversation, such changes can be more significant than several corrected ordinary words. Especially when it comes to system names, identifiers, and numbers.

Thus, "Recorder" Emerged from Model Experiments

Recorder uses the same pair of models: GigaAM provides the base, and Parakeet corrects terms. The text appears immediately after GigaAM's first pass, and corrections follow in the background.

Recorder records application audio and microphone separately. For system audio, it uses ScreenCaptureKit. No virtual audio driver or bots in meetings. It works for calls in Zoom, Yandex Telemost, Google Meet, Teams, and other applications.

Two tracks were useful for separating participants. One for the microphone, the other for the speakers. Speaker identification is done by tracks, so the system doesn't have to decipher everyone in a single mix from the start.

So, How to Transcribe Audio and Video to Text for Free and Without SMS?

Download Recorder if you have a Mac from the application page. The application accepts mp3, m4a, wav, mp4, and mov. No recording length limits. You can transcribe an already saved call or convert speech from video to text without uploading the file to an external service.

The current scenario is designed for Russian speech with anglicisms. It's not very suitable for fully English meetings.

What Was Achieved and What Remains for the Next Article

I started by trying to preserve the details of work meetings. In the end, I spent most of my time verifying errors of different models, tuning parameters, and annotating data.

A brief summary: GigaAM handles ordinary Russian conversation better, while Parakeet helps with terms. Their cascade wasn't the winner on every test. It turned out to be a good compromise for meetings where ordinary speech, technical discussions, and anglicisms are mixed.
