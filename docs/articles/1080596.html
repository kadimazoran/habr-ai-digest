1. How We Trained the Model

In short, the training looked like this: we took an SFT checkpoint, grew six separate models from it (one per domain), and trained each with online RL and its own reward; then we combined the six models back into one through on-policy distillation.

Further details, step by step: why online RL is needed at all, how the experts and rewards are structured, what happened within each domain, and how everything was glued together.

1.1 Why Online RL is Needed at All

At the SFT stage, we show the model ready-made solutions and ask it to reproduce them. It learns what a correct answer looks like, but not how to arrive at it. Give it a task that wasn't in the training set, and the difference is immediately apparent: the model starts confidently, repeats a familiar reasoning structure, and fails on the third step, not noticing that it's contradicting itself.

Online RL works differently. We give the model a task, it solves it itself, we evaluate the result, and adjust the weights towards the attempts that worked. We repeat this thousands of times. The word "online" means that the model generates the training data itself as it goes, rather than taking it from a pre-collected dataset. Gradually, techniques that increase the chance of reaching the answer are reinforced: re-checking intermediate results, going back a step, approaching from a different angle. This is what is called reasoning.

1.2 Six Experts and One Reward Structure

1.2.1 Why Not One Model?

Initially, we tried to train a single model for everything, and we hit two limitations: domains compete for the same weights — progress in code regressed quality in dialogues — and domains require different evaluation methods. An algebra problem has a correct answer that can be verified, but advice on how to write an email does not have such an answer.

Therefore, from one SFT checkpoint, we trained six separate experts. Each learns independently, and the reward can be tuned more precisely for it than in a single large training process.

1.2.2 Algorithm: CISPO

We trained using the CISPO algorithm from the MiniMax-M1 paper. It is a close relative of GRPO, and the essence of both is the same: for each task, the model writes several answers; answers better than the group average are reinforced, worse than average are suppressed. The difference is in one detail, but it turned out to be important for reasoning.

Any RL algorithm limits the step size to prevent the model from diverging after a single update. GRPO does this crudely: if a token becomes too probable or too rare after an update, training on it is simply turned off. The problem is that for a reasoning model, the most important tokens are precisely the rare ones. "However," "let's check," "stop, there's an error here," anything that makes the model doubt and go back. The SFT model rarely writes such words, and when RL starts to increase their probability, they are the first to fall outside the boundary and stop being learned. The model we are teaching to doubt does not receive a signal precisely where it doubts.

CISPO limits the step differently: it doesn't turn off tokens but dampens the contribution of those that have gone too far. Training remains stable, and the signal reaches all tokens, including rare branching points. The result is simple: CISPO converges faster than GRPO. The authors of MiniMax showed this on AIME 2024, and we observed the same thing with our experts.

1.2.3 Schedule: Tasks Become More Difficult Along with the Model

Not all tasks are equally useful for training. If the model solves a task in all attempts, then the rewards in the group are the same, the average equals each answer, and the gradient is zero. The same happens if it doesn't solve the task in any attempt. A useful signal comes only from the zone where the model succeeds sometimes: some answers in the group are correct, some are not, and there is something to compare. The maximum signal is at a success rate of about half.

The useful signal is maximal on tasks that the model solves in about half of the cases. The 75% threshold already filters out mastered tasks

The problem is that this zone moves. A task that the model solved in one out of eight attempts at the beginning of training, after a thousand steps, is solved in eight out of eight and stops teaching anything. Therefore, our schedule is built around difficulty: training begins with tasks that the model can already almost solve, and as the pass rate increases, the batch composition shifts towards more difficult ones.

This is done simply. Before each training session, we run the current checkpoint through the entire pool of tasks and measure how often it solves them. Everything that is solved more than 75% of the time is discarded from training: the model already knows how to do it, and it's pointless to spend rollouts on such tasks. The remainder becomes the training set. The next expert or the next stage starts with the same procedure, and the 75% threshold cuts off a new portion of tasks that the model has managed to master each time.

A side effect that we didn't plan for but appreciated: this schedule saves GPU. Tasks with zero gradient do not take up space in the batch, rollouts are not generated for them, and thus we save 50% of inference computations.

1.2.4 Reward

There is no single reward; each expert has its own, but the structure is common to all, we call it a cascade: the answer goes through several evaluation levels in sequence.

Gates (gated rewards). Binary checks that the answer must pass before it is evaluated on its merits: the answer is complete and in the correct format, written in the correct language without garbage or mixed alphabets, and does not violate strict instructions. If it fails the gates, the reward is zero, no matter how intelligent the reasoning within. The set of gates varies for experts: the compilability rule for code makes no sense in mathematics.

Gates appeared not from theory. In our experience, without them, the model starts speaking in various languages within a single sentence. In addition, in the paper on MAI, we noticed that such language shifts contribute to training instability.

Sum (additive rewards). Answers that pass the gates are evaluated on several axes with weights: correctness, completeness, adherence to instructions. Here, the evaluation already has gradations, and the model distinguishes between a mediocre answer and a good one.

1.2.5 Length Penalty

If the length of reasoning is not controlled, the model quickly notices that writing more is more beneficial: it can double-check everything three times, hedge its bets, add "however, perhaps..." As a result, it spends several minutes thinking about a question that a human would answer immediately.

1.3 Code: Two Experts and an Agent Rollout Factory

Working with code breaks down into two modes, and they are engineered very differently. In the first, the model receives a task and writes or edits a block of code entirely in one response. In the second mode, it works with a repository: navigates files, executes commands, reads output, and takes the next step. We trained two separate experts for these modes.

1.3.1 Code Expert: Algorithms, Edits, Tests

The first expert was trained on classic coding tasks: competitive programming, algorithm implementation, editing and refactoring existing code, generating tests. They all have one thing in common: the answer can be verified by running it. The entire task and all the code are included in a single request, with no interaction with the environment.

Currently, most tasks are in Python. We plan to expand the list of languages in the next release.

Let's discuss one mistake. In the early iterations, we gave a reward for each passed test, not for the task as a whole: passed seven out of ten tests, get 0.7. The idea seemed reasonable, a partial signal should help in complex tasks where the model is not yet capable of a full solution. But it didn't help as expected. In olympiad problems, examples from the problem statement almost always end up in the tests. The model noticed this and, for the most complex tasks, stopped solving them altogether: it would write if for the input data from the example, print the expected answer, and take its share of the reward. We had to abandon partial rewards for tests: either all tests passed, or none.

1.3.2 Code Agent Expert: SWE-bench-like Tasks

The second expert is the most complex engineering-wise of all six. It was trained to be an agent: it receives a real repository and a problem description as input, and a working patch as output. Along the way, the model reads files, executes commands, observes the output, and decides what to do next.

To do all this, the model works through a harness—a wrapper program around the model: it provides tools (terminal, file reading and editing, test execution), runs the loop "model says, harness executes, returns result," and ensures the agent doesn't get stuck in an infinite loop. There are many such harnesses: Claude Code, Codex, OpenHands, mini-SWE-agent, and dozens of others. They are written in different languages, have different architectures, and are updated weekly.

1.3.3 The Fork: Rewrite the Harness or Train Through It

There are two ways to train a model to work with a harness.

  1. Rewrite the harness within the training loop. Take one or two of the most popular ones, reproduce their logic in your stack, and train the model in this copy. This allows you to start quickly, but in the long run, it's difficult: each harness lives its own life, the copy falls behind the original within a month, and for each new harness, you have to rewrite everything from scratch.

  2. Don't touch the harness at all and train the model through it. To do this, a proxy is placed between the harness and the model, which records every request and response. The harness works as usual and doesn't know it's participating in training, and the recorded requests and responses are used to build a trajectory for training. The model is trained in the same environment where it will be used later, not in a simplified copy.

We wanted to train the model on as many harnesses as possible from the start, so the first approach didn't suit us. We went with the second and based it on Polar, an open-source framework from NVIDIA, which works exactly like this: a proxy before the model, a separate server for rollouts, a pipeline that spins up containers for the next tasks while the model is still thinking about the current ones. We forked it for our stack and deployed it entirely locally, including container virtualization.

1.3.4 The Cost

Now, honestly, about the drawbacks. Setting up Polar took us two months, and it was fully operational a week before the release training. Therefore, in this release, the Code Agent was trained on a single wrapper—mini-SWE-agent. The entire infrastructure for dozens of wrappers is already in place; we will load it in the next release.

1.4 Agents: Function Calling, Memory, Search

A separate expert was trained to call functions and conduct agent scenarios. Target benchmarks: Function Calling V4 (function calling, memory, search) and τ²-bench (multi-turn dialogues of an agent with a user).

1.4.1 Dialogue Agents

τ²-bench evaluates whether the model completes the task in a conversation with a simulated user. In the airlines and retail domains, the agent accesses the database itself, checks bookings and orders, and suggests valid options. In telecom, it doesn't have direct access to the device, so it guides the user through diagnostic steps using the tools available to the user. In real dialogues, both patterns occur together.

The main difficulty turned out to be in the data, not the algorithm. There are many SFT examples for such tasks available in the public domain, but almost none for RL: a database with consistent data, scenarios on top of it, and a verifiable target state are needed to play out the dialogue online and provide a reward. Therefore, we built the environments ourselves, using two pipelines.

Environments from existing projects. We take a real open-source framework with documentation as a basis, for example, a project from GitHub: an electronic library, a learning management system, and similar. The LLM reconstructs API methods, entity descriptions, and the database schema from the code and documentation, and also writes text templates for user-model prompts. Then everything is procedural. From the API methods, we build a dependency graph: which method consumes the result of which. We sample a path in this graph, and it becomes the skeleton of the task: for the path, we generate circumstances in the database, its desired final state with a verification method, and a prompt for the user-model. Several such tasks are combined into one session, resulting in a scenario with a large number of events. This resulted in five environments and eight thousand tasks. The databases here are large and deep, so one environment covers many different scenarios.

Turnkey environments. The second pipeline is fully agent-based and uses no ready-made components. First, a pool of everyday situations is created: food delivery, hotel reception desk, and so on. For each situation, agents design a database and API endpoints for it, populate it with entities, define the agent's policy, generate user scenarios, and finally, generate and filter ground truth so that the final database state exists and is unique. There are two differences from the first pipeline: agents invent the documentation themselves, rather than using existing documentation, and tasks are generated directly each time, without pre-generated templates. This resulted in 50 environments with 80 tasks each, totaling four thousand samples. The databases here are smaller and the scenarios simpler, but everything is entirely in Russian.

1.4.2 Diversity in Cold Start is More Important Than Accuracy

This is the main conclusion from the agent expert. The effectiveness of RL strongly depends on the SFT cold start. If, at the SFT stage, the model has not seen enough diverse responses to the user (clarifying questions, alternatives, explanations for refusal), then in RL, it lacks exploration to learn this. Such a policy gets stuck in repetitive replies and does not complete the scenario, and rewards for completed episodes come too rarely for training to progress.

The quality of the cold start is conveniently measured by pass@k on the starting checkpoint: this metric shows what proportion of tasks the model can solve in at least one of k samples, i.e., how well its diversity covers the scenario space. It is this coverage that RL then uses: if the correct trajectory is not found in any sample, then no reward comes, and there is nothing to learn. In our experiments with smaller models (10B), a cold start without conversational agent scenarios yielded a pass@32 of around 0.4 on τ²-type environments. After adding such scenarios to SFT, the pass@32 of the same starting checkpoint increased to 0.8-0.9, and RL on it proceeded noticeably faster.

1.4.3 Memory: Good Reading is Impossible Without Good Writing

There is almost no open data for personalized memory: no RL environments at all, and SFT datasets are either too few or lack reasoning before calling memory tools. We prepared both SFT and RL data ourselves.

Memory has two distinct abilities, and Function Calling V4 tests both. Reading is working with an external storage: finding information using tools, collecting it from multiple sources, summarizing. Writing is a separate skill: assessing the importance and uniqueness of a fact relative to what is already stored, avoiding clutter with duplicates. In RL, the phases were trained separately: for reading, we prepared environments with user information and question-answer pairs based on them, with a reward for answer correctness; for writing, we labeled whether a fact should be saved after a specific utterance, with a reward for consistency with the labeling.

It is perhaps even easier for the model to master writing than reading. But errors accumulate here: the error of reading is added to the error of writing. Therefore, preparing high-quality data for writing is indeed difficult and important: long multi-turn scenarios are needed where all user facts are consistent with each other, and the decision to save each one is unambiguous.

An example of a debatable case: a user writes "I might switch to vegetarianism." Should this be recorded as a fact "the user is a vegetarian"? This is more of an intention than a fact, and it might conflict with already stored information ("loves steaks"), and annotators have to decide whether to update the old record, mark it as outdated, or not save it at all.

1.4.4 Agent Search

For search, we prepared our own set of questions in the spirit of BrowseComp. The answer to such a question cannot be found with a single query: the model breaks it down into sub-questions, answers them sequentially, correlates the found facts, and does not take the first search result at face value.

The set is built automatically. First, the agent collects initial question-answer pairs from Wikipedia and internet materials: it navigates between related articles, accumulates confirmed facts, and formulates a question with a reference answer based on them. Then, the system iteratively complicates the question: it adds intermediate links and replaces direct mentions of entities with indirect hints. In the final stage, independent search agents check three things: whether the answer is confirmed by available sources, how difficult it is to reach it in a limited number of steps, and whether there are other suitable answers. Only correct, unambiguous, and sufficiently complex questions make it into the training set.

Of the remaining, another 70% do not pass the RL pass rate selection: approximately half of them are solved either in all eight samples or in none, providing no learning signal. For what remains, answer uniqueness is checked, which removes another ~7%. As a result, only about one in five generated questions reaches the training set.

1.5 Dialogue and Instructions

Everything that governs the model's everyday behavior—tone, explanations, coherent texts, formats, long context—does not have an answer that can be checked against a standard or run. This is the most inconvenient domain for RL, and within it, we trained two separate experts because their rewards are fundamentally different.

Dialogue Expert is responsible for normal conversation with the user: answering to the point, explaining, writing text. There is no correct answer here, only "better or worse," so the reward is built on comparison.

Instructions Expert is responsible for controllability: constraints in the prompt, formats, structured output, long context. Here, the answer is checked as follows: either the constraint is met, or it is not.

1.5.1 Reward

We use several rewards to train the conversational expert, but the main one is responsible for the quality of normal dialogue. We use SBS comparison: for a fixed set of prompts, we take the answer of our model and the answer of a strong model (GPT-5.2 Sol, Gemini 3.7 Flash, etc.), and MiniMax M2.7 acts as a judge, choosing the best one based on adherence to the prompt, completeness, and quality of phrasing. The reward is built from this pairwise comparison. This approach significantly improved metrics, but after analyzing one of the runs, we noticed that the average answer length almost tripled: the judge systematically preferred longer answers, considering them more detailed and higher quality.

To eliminate this behavior, we added a length penalty that depends on the prompt type and the average length of strong model answers. After this, the average length increased by only 8%, and SBS metrics continued to improve steadily and reached the same high level without inflating answers.

1.5.2 Curriculum

In the instruction expert, instead of uniformly mixing all types of tasks, we shifted the beginning of training towards instruction following and structure. The model became more controllable faster: it adheres to constraints, follows formats, and executes instructions more accurately. This also affected other domains where the correct answer can easily be lost due to format violations.

1.5.3 Data

The main work then involved the composition of data and proportions. For some tasks, such as complex planning, high-quality natural data was insufficient, and we filled the gaps with synthetic data.

We also reworked structured output: improved examples, system prompts, and schemas for JSON, and added other formats so that the model perceives the response structure as part of the instruction.

For long context, simply increasing the window is not enough: the model must find and connect information within a large text. We improved the infrastructure for training on long sequences and added data on information retrieval, working with articles, and correlating distant fragments.

1.5.4 What Went Wrong?

For example, when working with JSON, we relied partly on JSONSchemaBench, but in early runs, the metric remained near zero, although the answers looked correct manually. The reason turned out to be that the model was accustomed to explaining its actions: before the JSON, it would write something like, "Certainly, here is the result," and after, it would add a comment. This looked normal to a human, but a strict parser considered the entire response incorrect.

In RL, we added a separate penalty for any text outside the required JSON structure. After this, the model quickly stopped adding introductory and explanatory remarks where a strict answer was needed, and the metric went up. This turned out to be a simple example of a conflict between two useful skills: the habit of explaining improves normal dialogue but can completely break the result in structured output.

1.6 How to Combine Six Models into One

After RL, we have six experts, but we need one model. We combined them using on-policy distillation (OPD). The student generates an answer itself, and the teacher—the expert for the domain to which the task belongs—evaluates each token: "Here I would say this," "And here, differently." The signal comes along the student's trajectory, based on its own errors, as in RL, but the feedback is much denser: not one number for the entire answer, but an evaluation at each step.

2. Training Infrastructure

Before discussing training efficiency, it's worth understanding what is needed for training to be stable and predictable at all.

2.1 Engine Consistency

Almost all research on this topic agrees on one point: train and inference engines must be well-aligned, otherwise, unnecessary noise enters the training process. Rollouts are generated by one engine, gradients are calculated by another, and if they diverge in the probabilities of the same tokens, then the training, which we consider on-policy, is actually off-policy.

The problem is particularly acute for MoE models. The MoE router operates non-deterministically: on the same data, train and inference select different experts, and the divergence between engines is noticeably greater than in dense models.

The rollout routing replay (R3) technique proved particularly useful. The idea is simple: during generation, we record which experts the router selected during inference, and during training, we reuse exactly that selection. The main source of desynchronization simply disappears. In addition, we calculate some operations with higher precision, fp32 — calculation of logits, normalization, storage and accumulation of mamba slots, and so on.

2.2 Asynchronous Pipeline

After solving the problem of stable training, it's time to focus on its speed. RL efficiency requires a multifaceted approach: both the training engine, the inference engine, and the orchestration of all components between them need to be optimized.

There are many approaches to optimizing RL pipelines. We based our work on the asynchronous training pipeline from verl and made it scalable for large models, achieving an implementation more than 2.5 times faster than the original. The original implementation scales poorly: it has many bottlenecks, starting with processing and transferring examples between the teacher and the rollouter.

The main advantage comes from separating the teacher and the rollouter onto different GPU resource pools. The components stop waiting for each other, and each can be configured and scaled independently.

2.3 Weight Synchronization

A separate stage that becomes a bottleneck when scaling training to large models is weight synchronization. After each training step, new weights must be delivered to the inference engine, and for a model of this size, this is already costly. There are many implementations that speed up this stage, and they are similar in structure; we settled on the implementation from the miles repository — it is efficient, yet flexible and adds almost no overhead. We adapted it for verl and reduced the synchronization time from several minutes to 8 seconds per training step.

2.4 Inference Acceleration

Generating rollouts is the most expensive part of RL training, so accelerating inference provides the biggest efficiency gain. The model itself helps here: it natively supports MTP (multi-token prediction), meaning it predicts multiple tokens per step, and generation speeds up without a separate draft model. In total, this provides about a 30% acceleration in training.

2.5 FP8

An obvious point of efficiency growth is the transition to FP8. But in RL, it has a peculiarity that follows from what was described at the beginning of this section: engine consistency is key to successful training. Therefore, FP8 cannot be enabled only for inference. It must be implemented on the teacher's side and the rollouter's side, and their coordinated operation must be built separately, otherwise, the difference in precision between the engines itself becomes a source of desynchronization.

The result is worth the effort. Transitioning to FP8 accelerates training by 1.3-2 times and is now our standard for all training. We do not see any quality degradation: results are comparable to training in bf16.

It is worth noting that GigaChat 3.5 430B Reasoning is a model trained natively in FP8 at all stages.

Instead of a Conclusion

We promised to explain why this took over nine months. The algorithm is the smallest part of the work: CISPO, the pass rate schedule, and the reward cascade can be reproduced in weeks. The time was spent on environments with verifiable rewards and on the infrastructure where online RL on MoE of this size converges at all.

What we wish we had known at the beginning:

  • Rewards will be exploited. Partial reward for tests, a judge without a length penalty, absence of language gates — each time the model found a loophole before we did. Design the reward by asking "how can this be most easily cheated?"

  • Cold start sets the RL ceiling. If the correct trajectory is not found in any of the k samples, there is nothing to learn. SFT diversity is more important than its accuracy.

  • Engine desynchronization is hidden off-policy. For MoE, without R3, other optimizations were meaningless.

Weights and launch code are available on huggingface, and you can also try it at giga.chat. We await feedback and examples where the model behaves unexpectedly — in the comments or on our channel.
