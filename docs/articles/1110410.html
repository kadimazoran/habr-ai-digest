Include HR, managers, of course, the developers themselves, while also considering any company, even if it has only one first-year developer who occasionally paints buttons on a single CMS website.

3. What does it mean to replace an employee with AI?
This is the point I consider most important in this article. Let's take simple examples:


Developer A, with five years of experience, has completely stopped writing code manually and now simply assigns tasks to agents. Is this considered replacing a specialist with AI? I think not, because the developer has simply changed the level of abstraction they work with to a higher one, and now they are exclusively engaged in, for example, conceptualizing and developing architecture for new features and projects.

Developer B, with six years of experience, was laid off because Developer A increased their efficiency by 6 times, and the team no longer needs a second senior developer. Is this considered replacing a specialist with AI? This question is more complex, and here we can recall dialectics. On the one hand, the reason for this layoff was AI; that is, if there were no AI, Developer A could not physically increase their efficiency by a factor of, say, 5-10 times, and Developer B would continue to work in the same place. On the other hand, AI did not fire anyone (yes, it sounds silly), management fired the person because even if Developer B also increased their productivity by 5-10 times, there simply isn't enough volume of tasks to require such effort. Or, it is simply more profitable for management to maintain the same (if not greater) volume of work, so they can fire one person, save money, and the other has already increased efficiency and will continue to work for the same salary (perhaps slightly more, but this is rare).

Developer C, with three years of experience, works as a freelancer. They create simple Telegram bots and landing pages for small businesses. Sometimes they engage in more serious development. However, over the year, the number of orders has decreased threefold. It has simply become possible for the small business owner themselves, or one of the initiative employees (or someone who can't say no), to subscribe for $10 to opencode go, which will then code that Telegram bot or landing page in 1-2 days. Is this considered replacing Developer C? In this case, I think the task itself disappears, or rather, the entry barrier for performing this task is lowered. Or one could even flip it and say that the average skill level of workers has increased due to more effective access to information and a higher level of abstraction (please keep this thought in the back of your mind, because when we move on to finding historical parallels for greater clarity, it will be used quite often).

What do we get in the end? Even from these 3 examples (I think we can come up with more), we see at least 3 different phenomena.
1. Change in the level of abstraction (not a replacement at all)
2. Layoffs due to increased efficiency/desire to optimize costs for profit maximization. (Replacement, but indirect)
3. Disappearance of the task itself, as an order requiring a narrow specialist. (Almost a replacement)

I believe it is necessary to clearly explain that these are 3 completely different phenomena and they cannot be lumped together.

4. The issue of measurement.
Returning to the examples described above, I would like to discuss an idea that might have occurred to an experienced Reader, and from it, derive an important question for discussion. For example, Developer C (who developed simple Telegram bots and landing pages) got a job at a factory (because one has to eat) and one evening, just to reminisce, decided to open a freelance platform. What will they see there?

On the one hand, anyone can code a Telegram bot with a $10 subscription and 2-3 hours of free time. Does this mean all orders should disappear? Above, I wrote that this is "the disappearance of the task itself, as an order requiring a NARROW SPECIALIST." And this is quite important, because here we are stepping onto slippery ground of paradoxes, and one of the most important, which I honestly do not fully recognize as a paradox, is Jevons' Paradox. There was even an article about it on Habr (https://habr.com/ru/articles/883174/).

Essentially, the idea is simple. The cost of development decreases -> more companies can order development -> more orders. This might not increase profit, but the idea is that development will become conveyor-belt-like, and one developer will supposedly churn out 20 bots a day using AI.

On the one hand, everything seems to fit together, everyone is happy, but it's worth remembering the concentration of capital. Capital has only one goal: continuous growth. Growth in the realities of full globalization can be either through optimization (which is what we discussed above), or through the absorption of competitors (monopolization), or through the development of new sales markets (there aren't many of those left). Therefore, we must remember that the vast majority of small and medium-sized businesses, as well as private clients, are simply getting poorer year by year due to the concentration of capital in the hands of large monopolies and transnational corporations, which do not need to place orders, they have their own staff. This means they will have less funds for orders. And again, there is no clear answer as to whether this will increase or decrease the number of orders. There are too many factors.

Now that the problem has been roughly outlined, we can approach the question. How do we measure that AI has replaced someone? By the number of layoffs? But there's also freelancing, which also needs to be considered. I propose to measure what AI replaces programmers by the number of paid hours demanded. Unfortunately, I do not have the ability to collect the amount of information required to analyze the entire market, and not enough data is published, so I decided that the calculation formulas would simply be meaningless due to the lack of numbers to plug into them. I propose to simply agree that when talking about a decrease in demand, we will look at three things: the number of vacancies, the number of freelance orders, and payment.

5. Level of Abstraction
I believe this is one of the key concepts that will be mentioned in this series of articles, so I will allow myself to spend a little more of your time, dear Reader, and explain what I mean by it.

The level of abstraction is easiest to explain using the example of our favorite development. Let's recall how this direction has evolved:
In the olden days, people interacted with computing technology at the lowest level (punch cards or machine code of 0s and 1s)
-> assembler (operation codes are left behind, the human works with commands)
-> high-level languages (most of the work with the processor and memory has moved down, the human works with expressions, functions, types, and classes)
-> frameworks (typical infrastructure is left behind, the human works with routes, ORM, and is closer to architecture than to code)
-> AI (writing and editing code has moved down, though not completely, and the human describes the task in natural language). 


The further technical progress advances, the less workers need to deal with what has already been solved for them, and the more abstracted they become from low-level work. Each stage of abstraction does not eliminate the profession itself, but changes what the worker does, while lowering the qualification required to enter the profession (this will be explained in more detail below when we move on to historical examples) + it increases the number of tasks.

A higher level of abstraction makes people who know the previous level more in demand (at the established abstraction, not the new one, because when a new abstraction appears, workers who can only use it do not appear out of nowhere, and the market needs to stabilize).

The level of abstraction of AI agents differs from previous levels. The level of high-level languages and frameworks is predictable, while AI is a probabilistic tool, so it requires more control.

Historical Parallels


It seems we have defined the concepts (for those who disagree, please proceed to the comments and express your position clearly and argumentatively; I will listen to everyone to the extent of the weight of their arguments), so we can finally move on to the discussion, where I would like to start by giving examples that will allow us to examine what happened in other industries where changes have already stabilized. Moreover, we remember the wisdom from school: "History develops in a spiral," so there is hope that we can find some answers on its previous turn.

Power Looms
What happened in the weaving industry after the automation of labor in the 19th century? Because demand was much higher than supply (or rather, it was ready to grow as soon as supply increased), automation allowed for a significant increase in the number of jobs. How were jobs increased? It was economically viable that if you hire a worker for a power loom, spend time on training (if required), and include salaries, then such a worker will pay off their labor, even if the price of the product is reduced. Thus, demand can be increased (price reduced -> demand increased), and the owner of the means of production will extract super-profits, which means they need to hire, as demand can absorb everything. Later, however, demand became saturated, and the number of jobs began to decrease.

Why didn't this happen with us? It's simple, we started moving to a new level of abstraction when the market was oversaturated (February 2022); everyone rushed into IT, companies hired enough to meet demand, the market began to decline, the number of vacancies decreased, and then AI arrived. And the market, where it was already unclear if so many developers were needed, collapsed. Although it is already starting to rebound slightly compared to the second half of 2025 (I used FRED data for the US, so it's worth considering that things are a bit different in our region). Although I would rather say recovering. Nevertheless, AI was only one of the factors in this already declining market.

Telephone Operators
We can recall another story where automation led to the complete disappearance of a market, and this is what many fear. Telephone operators completely disappeared due to automatic dialing. Not instantly, of course, but over several generations, they completely disappeared.

Why can't AI do this? This is exactly what I described earlier – AI differs from other levels of abstraction in that it is probabilistic and requires human supervision, preferably by a person with knowledge of at least the previous level of abstraction. The work of telephone operators was replaced by a fully automated, non-probabilistic method. Developers are not threatened by such a complete disappearance.


Spreadsheets and Accounting
After the advent of spreadsheets, the number of clerks decreased by 400,000 over 35 years, while the number of accountants increased by 600,000. This is US data from bls.gov. The trend continues, with the number of clerks decreasing and accountants increasing. Accounting has become cheaper, and companies have increased demand for accountants.

It is quite possible that this scenario will coincide with the scenario in our field, because if development becomes simply 10 times faster, and consequently cheaper, won't the demand for it increase?

In conclusion, I want to say that historical examples help to exclude several outcomes with high probability.
1. The example with power looms gives us an understanding that without elastic demand, we should not expect an increase in the IT hiring market.
2. The example with telephone operators shows that the complete disappearance of a profession is almost impossible in a situation where there is no complete deterministic automation.
3. The example with accounting leads us to look again at Jevons' Paradox in IT. However, there is no guarantee of growth, even if the price of development plummets even lower.
