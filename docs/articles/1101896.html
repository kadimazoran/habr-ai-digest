At four in the morning on August 1, 2012, a planned update was completed on the servers of Knight Capital, then the largest broker on the US stock market. And just 45 minutes after the exchange opened, the firm lost $440 million and ceased to exist as an independent business forever.

The reason was not a virus or a hacker attack by competitors. Engineers were updating the SMARS order routing code in a great hurry to meet the launch of the New York Stock Exchange's new trading program. They used an old settings array where, on one of the eight servers, the flag for a dead Power Peg function, written nine years ago and long unused, was accidentally left active. But when the stream of market data began, the faulty server started continuously buying stocks at inflated prices and selling them at a loss, sending four million orders per minute to the exchange.

Of course, Knight Capital engineers were not fools; they knew that the old legacy code needed to be completely cleaned up and that deploying to eight servers manually without orchestration was not the wisest decision. But on Wall Street, a day's delay in release threatened the loss of major clients. And as a result, haste forced them to sacrifice security.

Intuition in such cases pushes us to look for some hidden puppet masters and villains behind it. We tend to think that secret orders, corrupt elites, or a shadow world government are behind global catastrophes. But reality is a bit different.

In boardrooms, government commissions, and engineering headquarters, you'll typically find educated, rational people. They don't want ecological collapse, nuclear war, or an AI uprising. But each of them, day after day, makes a locally correct, logical choice that, with mathematical inevitability, pushes the entire system into an abyss.

The name for this phenomenon is Moloch.

1. Who is Moloch?

In ancient times, Moloch was the name of a Phoenician and Canaanite deity whose cult demanded child sacrifices in exchange for victory in war or protection from plague. If a city was besieged by enemies, rulers would throw children into the idol's red-hot bronze womb, hoping to buy the favor of the elements.

In the 20th century, this image was reinterpreted by beat poet Allen Ginsberg in his 1955 poem “Howl.” He called Moloch the soulless conveyor belt of industrial capitalism, grinding down culture, spirit, and people's health for the sake of an abstract figure of economic efficiency.

The concept experienced a second birth in July 2014, when California psychiatrist and rationalist Scott Alexander published the essay “Meditations on Moloch,” transforming Ginsberg's poetic image into a tool for rigorous systems thinking.

Moloch is the personification of coordination failure in multipolar non-zero-sum non-cooperative games.

The essence of Moloch is easiest to understand through a classic thought experiment formulated in the 1950s by mathematicians Merrill Flood and Melvin Dresher of the RAND Corporation. Albert Tucker later named it the Prisoner's Dilemma. Two suspects are isolated from each other and offered a deal: betray their accomplice or remain silent.

    Разделение конкуренции
    The natural question that any reasonable person will surely ask in this situation is: "Won't we kill progress if we try to completely neutralize the effect of selection? Didn't fierce competition give us fast processors, fiber-optic networks, and neural networks?"
    And the answer here is not as simple as it seems at first glance. It lies in the fundamental mathematical difference between positive-sum and negative-sum games.
    Joseph Schumpeter described the concept of "creative destruction": competition is beneficial when the contest is in the realm of creating new consumer value. The invention of the transistor, compiler optimization, or the synthesis of a new antibiotic enriches the entire global system. Here, the local victory of one participant increases the overall pie of civilization (in the social welfare model ):
    Moloch's trap arises where competition degenerates into pure positional struggle with a zero or negative sum ():
    • When millions of teraflops are burned not on training useful models, but on creating bots for generating spam and neuro-slop.
    • When the best minds spend years of their lives reducing option latency by three nanoseconds without creating a single useful product for the real economy.
    • When laboratories cut AI safety protocols solely out of fear that a competitor will announce a model two weeks earlier.
    The task of fighting Moloch is not to artificially stifle competition and force stagnation. The task is to build rigid algorithmic and institutional banks. Because a river without banks turns into a rotten swamp that floods cities. But the same river, confined by granite embankments, turns hydroelectric turbines, supplying light and energy to millions of homes. Mechanism theory, cryptographic proofs, and Ostrom's polycentric rules are the granite embankments for humanity's creative energy.
    Engineers Against Moloch
    The world is not collapsing due to a conspiracy of secret societies or the fatal degradation of human nature. All the global crises we see—from the depletion of common resources to the AI arms race—are born from the mathematics of non-cooperative Nash equilibrium in systems with incomplete information. This is because we have built a civilization of planetary scale on tribal communication protocols not designed for exponential technologies.
    But in this struggle, we have a colossal advantage over any biological selection algorithm. A bacterium in a Petri dish cannot comprehend the laws of population dynamics that cause a colony to consume the last agar before extinction. A bacterium has no interface for reflection.
    But humans possess subjectivity. We can rise above the plane of the payoff matrix, see the destructive equilibrium from the outside, and apply the mathematical apparatus of Computer Science to rewrite the rules of the environment.
    And the solution here is not in some naive altruism; it requires an exclusively professional and pragmatic approach:
    • Stop looking for malicious intent where blind incentives of local benefit are at play.
    • Implement protocols with proven incentive compatibility, where honesty and cooperation are mathematically more profitable than deception.
    • Use cryptographic commitment schemes, trusted enclaves, and zero-knowledge proofs for mutual verification without loss of privacy.
    • Extend the shadow of the future within our own teams and architectures, designing open, long-lived standards instead of disposable hyped add-ons.
    Moloch rules the world only as long as agents are locked in isolated solitary cells and paralyzed by fear of the opponent's next move. But as soon as normal communication is established, transparent protocols, verifiable code, and rigorous mechanism theory are introduced, this entire clay colossus crumbles to dust.
