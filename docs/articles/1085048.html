Why You Can't Just Upload Fitness Tracker History to an LLM and Ask It to Assess Your Health

Researchers decided to test a seemingly obvious question: if you give a large language model real measurement history from a fitness tracker—heart rate, sleep, steps over six months—can it understand this data as confidently as it discusses health in general? The result was unexpected. Models performed much worse at simple calculations on the numbers themselves than at clinical interpretations of the same data. GPT-4o correctly identified trends and anomalies in only a quarter of cases, but reasoned about health risks based on the same data twice as confidently.

Logic suggests the opposite: clinical interpretation is a high-level skill requiring knowledge and caution, while calculating an average or finding a spike in a series of numbers is a simpler, almost technical task. It turns out that for language models, things are arranged differently, and the gap between these two skills is a systemic problem that the authors of the WearableQA benchmark identified across 14 models simultaneously. Let's explore what this means for those planning to entrust AI with the analysis of their own health data.

Why You Can't Feed Graphs from Your Watch to the Model

The idea of giving LLMs data from wearable devices is not new—sleep and fitness assistants based on large language models are appearing one after another. The problem is how they have been tested so far. Existing benchmarks for reasoning over time series primarily rely on synthetic or simulated signals, not data from real people. The difference is fundamental. A synthetic series is clean and predictable, like a textbook problem with rounded numbers. But readings from a tracker on a living person involve sensor noise, missed days, individual body peculiarities, and dozens of minor details that are simply not available in a lab setting.

The authors of WearableQA took a different approach. They used data from 200 real people with daily measurements, some spanning nearly a year and a half. To each profile, they added blood test results (insulin, glycated hemoglobin, and about fifteen other indicators) and basic personal information: age, sex, weight, origin. This resulted in 4084 questions. Moreover, these were the kinds of questions an AI assistant in a health app would realistically encounter if it ever gained access to your personal data. The questions were structured as multiple-choice tests with 10 options.

How to Verify an Answer When It Concerns Live Physiology

For a question like "Is this person's resting heart rate increasing?" an authoritative answer is needed. And it can't be pulled out of thin air: it must be based on either medical literature or a statistically confirmed pattern.

The researchers chose both sources. Some questions were based on data from peer-reviewed articles—the authors selected 11 key publications (including from Nature Medicine and PNAS), verified identifiers by DOI, PMID, and PMCID, read each one thoroughly, and required that the identified pattern be confirmed by at least two independent studies with the same effect direction.

Another part of the questions originated directly from the data: the team looked for patterns in the 200 user profiles—trends, spikes, correlations between different signals. But finding a correlation is not enough; it also needs to be verified. Therefore, each such pattern was passed through three filters.

First, the strength of the association: a correlation coefficient of at least 0.5, with the sign matching in both halves of the time window. Second, robustness. The pattern had to withstand data resampling (at least 80% of 30 repetitions) and not fall apart if any single day was removed from the sample. And finally, authenticity: associations were checked against randomly shuffled pairs of users—this way, they filtered out what was actually statistical noise rather than real physiology.

Only patterns that passed all three checks became questions. Incorrect answer options were also handled separately: if a model could guess the correct answer simply by looking at the wording, without delving into the data, the question was rephrased.

Two Types of Thinking That the Benchmark Separates

All questions are divided into two independent groups. The first group tests a type of reasoning. Some questions require direct calculation from raw measurements: finding a trend, a spike, recovery time after exertion, the strongest link between two signals. Others require interpretation: assessing risk, making a prediction, offering a recommendation based on clinical knowledge, not just raw numbers.

The second group looks at complexity by the number of signals. Some questions concern a single metric, for example, only heart rate. Others require combining several signals—for instance, looking for a link between physical activity and resting heart rate.

Together, these axes yield 16 types of questions and, more importantly, allow us to pinpoint where exactly the model makes mistakes: whether it fails at the calculation itself, at the medical interpretation, or at combining multiple data sources into a single picture.

Getting to the Main Point

The researchers ran 14 models through the benchmark: from GPT-4o and Gemini 2.5 Pro to compact Llama and Gemma. Accuracy ranged from 19.6% to 72.9%, with random guessing at 10%. This in itself is not surprising: larger and newer models are usually more accurate. The surprise lies elsewhere.

For almost all models, accuracy on clinical interpretation was higher than on calculations from raw data, even though intuition suggests the opposite. For GPT-4o, the difference was 25.3% versus 53.5%; for the compact Gemma-4-26B, it was 33.8% versus 59.8%; for Mistral-Small, 20% versus 47.9%. Only one model, Gemini 3.1 Pro, showed the reverse: it handled calculations even more confidently than interpretation—75.4% versus 67.8%.

It seems the issue isn't that clinical interpretation is an easier task. The key point is where the answer comes from. When assessing health risks, a model can simply rely on general knowledge about physiology learned from its training data. Much like a student who has memorized a textbook answers a test question without even opening the problem statement. However, to find a trend or a spike in a specific series of numbers for a particular person, general knowledge is insufficient. One must actually look at the data and perform calculations.

The benchmark authors tested this hypothesis with a separate experiment. They compared pairs of signals of two types. Some were obviously related, almost by definition: for example, the number of steps and calories burned—that's self-explanatory. Other pairs revealed themselves only through observation of a specific individual, such as resting heart rate and stress levels. The strength of the association was deliberately equalized for a fair comparison. And the difference in accuracy was striking: for Gemini 2.5 Pro, "obvious" pairs gave an advantage of almost 52 percentage points, and for GPT-5.4, nearly 48. Models indeed recognize familiar patterns more often than they calculate new ones from an individual's data.

Blind Spot: When Multiple Signals Need to Be Combined

The second part of the experiment, concerning inter-signal reasoning, proved equally revealing. For most closed models, accuracy on questions with a single signal is noticeably higher than on those requiring the combination of multiple signals. For GPT-5.4, the gap was 59.1% versus 45.7%; for Gemini 2.5 Pro, it was 58.1% versus 45.3%.

One type of question stands out—predicting one signal based on its relationship with another (cross-signal prediction). Here, the results are almost catastrophic even for strong models: from 8.3% for the compact Llama-3.1-8B to 40.5% for Claude Opus 4.6—even though random guessing yields 10%. This means the best model on this specific task barely exceeds the random chance level by four times, whereas on other tasks, the gap with guessing reaches seven to eight times.

Graphs Don't Help, Code Does

The impact of data presentation format on the results was also examined separately. The authors compared text-based representations—line-by-line records, column tables, Markdown, CSV—and visual ones: separate graphs for each signal and grouped graphs.

The difference between text formats was minimal, within one to two percentage points. However, the results with graphs were interesting. Accuracy did not increase but decreased: from 51.2% with plain text to 36.6% when each signal was given its own graph, and to 34.1% when graphs were grouped. Combining text and images did not improve the situation either.

However, when models were given access to a Python interpreter and allowed to write code for calculations themselves, a sharp jump occurred: accuracy increased from 51.2% to 71.3%, a gain of almost 20 percentage points. It seems the issue is not that models are bad at "reading" time series themselves. It's just that mental arithmetic with a huge array of numbers is difficult for them, but if they are allowed to calculate directly, the gap between theory and practice largely disappears.

There's also an almost anecdotal moment in the study. When models were forbidden from reasoning step-by-step and asked to immediately state the answer letter, smaller models began to noticeably gravitate towards certain options. This was most evident with Llama-3.2-3B: in direct answer mode, it chose the same option 51.5% of the time, regardless of the question. When the model was allowed to reason aloud, this bias almost disappeared: the proportion of a single option dropped to 13.5%. Larger proprietary models showed a milder bias, but it was still noticeable—for example, Claude Opus 4.6 preferred earlier letters of the alphabet.

Instead of a Conclusion

The results of WearableQA paint a rather unpleasant picture for those who already use or are planning to use AI health assistants based on tracker data. A model might confidently and coherently discuss health risks, but this does not necessarily mean it is actually analyzing your personal measurement series. It could simply be relying on general knowledge about physiology, regardless of what is actually happening in your specific numbers. And it's almost impossible to detect this substitution: both types of answers look identical.

The practical takeaway for those developing such products is also quite specific—judging by the experiment with Python access, the best way to make a model actually calculate, rather than guess based on general knowledge, is to give it a calculation tool, not just a more detailed textual description of the data.

If you knew for sure that the AI assistant in your tracker relied more on general physiological knowledge than on actually analyzing your specific numbers, would you trust its advice as much as before?
