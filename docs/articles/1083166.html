Once upon a time, the ability to whistle E7 was a quite practical skill. The telephone network did not expect a whistling register in the TF channel and accepted the bluebox as a brother capable of speaking its language. Signaling was removed, the skill remained. Practical use - memes, t-shirts, eZine. One could whistle even more elaborately, but the network no longer hears.

Years of expertise do not grant the right to the next budget.

You are doing well. Product line, installed base, partner channel, annual conference. Some people have learned to agree on which features will be included in the next release. Almost without a fight. Others have agreed to deliver features. Almost on time. A third group has almost agreed on "purchases." Almost.

Budgets are allocated, budgets are planned, headcount is in order, only sunshine ahead? Nope.

Have you never encountered a situation where your client says, "I stuffed your report into a mob..." or "My accountant 'navaybil' it..."? No? Then you don't communicate with clients.

The main question of this article is: what should an IT vendor invest in a world where a universal agent can already perform the main work?

In "The Creator's Manifesto," I whistled into a blue whistle. Now I propose to break down the magical quadrature perpendicularly along the Tragic Circle.

Let's sketch broadly.

An agent can write your product.

An agent can replace your product.

These are two different chords cutting the pie.

In the first case, the reproduction of what you sell becomes cheaper. In the second, the client gets the desired result without your box.

In the first case, someone must want to, set up the agent, burn tokens, and turn code into a product. This requires some effort and someone who strongly desires it.

In the second case, the tragedy is greater. The code can be complex, beautiful, fast, adorned with patents. But if the result is achieved in another way, the aforementioned remains a matter of your legitimate pride and something to show your mom. An invoice can be issued, but no one will pay.

The statement "our product cannot be written in an evening" can be completely true and completely useless. The client does not need to reproduce twenty years of development, compatibility with all platforms, and settings for other clients. It is enough for them to meet their own needs. In "Three Elephants," this was called JITI: asked, received, discarded.

No one is obliged to repeat your mistakes to stop paying you.

Your next competitor doesn't even know that a product exists in your "quadrant." Nor do they know about the existence of product categories. They have the client's task and the ability to solve it. Or they already have a tool that can solve THIS TASK TOO. Hello, AppSec/SAST: an agent that writes code also checks it for vulnerabilities, and does it better than 30 years of technological development.

Aykyn's Sieve

What to do? Now is a very opportune time to sift. You have years of experience, millions of lines of code. Let's go.

I won't elaborate on which sieve cuts along which chord. I don't like three-dimensional tables.

First, a bit of math.

Product = Promise − Code + Post-sale

If you can't grasp this, it's time to change professions.

The promise and post-sale are individual, so the sieve must be applied to a specific purchase: what client, for what result, under what conditions are you paid. Two companies buy the same product for different reasons. One needs a table on demand. The other needs agreed-upon figures, change history, and the ability to explain in three years where everything came from. These purchases have different resilience.

Zero Sieve: Is the work still there?

Part of the software serves human weaknesses. Tasks need to be transferred, statuses collected, explanations given about who didn't transfer what to whom. If the business changes, some of this activity may disappear along with the need for its automation.

Agents also need state, dependencies, acceptance, and error handling. But they are not obliged to reproduce the entire ritual of a human office. Selling them automation for a corporate stand-up is like giving a demon a corporate calendar with kittens via cron.

Look at what exactly you are automating. Perhaps a useful result. Perhaps a way to transfer information between people who will no longer be in this process. The delivery train will have to explain not only adherence to the schedule but also the presence of rails and passengers at the final station.

First Sieve: Can an agent perform this work without your product?

Give it the task, access to the data, and ordinary tools. Compare the result. Account for errors, rework, human verification, and operating costs. Don't pit a multi-year product against an empty chat that is forbidden everything useful for the sake of experimental purity. Give it everything.

And don't confuse "the agent performs the work" with "the model thinks about every byte every time." If deterministic calculation is needed, the agent can write or call ordinary code. If a database is needed, it can use a database. To issue an invoice, it is not necessary to ask the neural network every time how much two plus two is.

Determinism, speed, and economies of scale remain important. However, the need to calculate quickly and correctly does not imply the need to buy your specific calculator. Computers are generally good with numbers.

If a universal agent already provides an acceptable result under the client's conditions, find out why they continue to pay you. The answer "haven't tried yet" is a weak basis for an investment program. The fact that this function is indispensable does not yet mean that you are indispensable.

Second Sieve: What does the agent still have to get from outside?

Observations about the real world. Accumulated history. Access to infrastructure. The right to perform an action. Execution of an operation at the required speed. A contract under which someone is actually responsible. It makes sense to linger here.

A product is a promise. Code and post-sale are ways to fulfill it.

The client buys the ability to get results tomorrow, after a neighboring system update, and when everything suddenly stops working. Someone must maintain the data, handle incidents, issue fixes, and wake up an engineer at three in the morning. Preferably before the client wakes up.

The agent bears no obligations. The agent provider may well take them on. Therefore, "we have an SLA" is a good start to the conversation. The next question is, what do you provide the promise with, and why is it difficult for another provider to do it no worse?

The word "platform" or "ecosystem" is not considered an answer.

Third Sieve: Does "years of experience" need to be bought from you specifically?

Now you can open the can. It's useful to give the current version to the agents to tear apart: let them find what's really worth keeping. Engines, adapters, verification shells, accumulated exceptions, knowledge of where it breaks. And separately - pieces of code that for twenty years explained to a person which button to press next.

This is Legacy. Legacy is not gold. It's spent nuclear fuel, raw material that hasn't been processed yet. What's there, uranium-238 or samarium, will be revealed by sifting.

The composition must be analyzed, not the age of the storage.

One component will retain independent value. Another will become a tool for the agent. A third will have to be replaced because its sole purpose is to circumvent the inconveniences of the first two.

Separately check your favorite "unique competencies." Show the difference in results.

Give one task to a universal agent. Then add your knowledge and tools. What has changed? Has it become more accurate, cheaper, faster? Have errors that the client actually notices disappeared? Has it become possible to do something that was impossible without you? Let's call this a subtraction test.

If only the amount of context has changed, congratulations, it's samarium.

The archive of tickets and requirements may turn out to be just an archive of tickets. The groomer left, the backlog remained.

A feedback loop is valuable when it helps distinguish successful solutions from unsuccessful ones and improve subsequent ones. But "the expert accepted the recommendation" is not yet the truth. The probability of human error in a routine operation is about 10⁻³, in a complex one - 10⁻². By functional safety standards, this is SIL 1, on a good day SIL 2. Up to six sigma is three orders of magnitude. A closed ticket does not necessarily mean a solved problem. It's quite possible the client was simply tired of the correspondence.

Break down the product into components, but remember how they are connected. A free interface can lead clients to a paid engine. A low-profit service can provide observations without which the engine will lose to competitors. A product committee that cuts everything based on P&L can kill a cash cow by simply stopping watering the meadow where it grazes. Without any AI.

Fourth Sieve: Who chooses you now and for what?

The product may remain useful, but it will no longer be bought separately. It will be included in someone else's package, moved under someone else's contract. It will remain in the stack and disappear from a separate budget line.

If the product becomes a tool, it needs clear operations, documentation, access, and predictable errors. MCP or another convenient connection method. It should be easier for the agent to work with you than to figure out how to bypass you.

MCP is a socket. Demand is not included.

There remain authorized vendors, already paid packages, contract terms, data parsers, and the person who approves the purchase. The agent can choose within this set. They may not choose at all because the choice was made for them. Or they can choose. And someone can look at the billing.

The order of API calls rarely dictates the order of profit distribution. But check who can do without whom. An orchestrator with access to ten identical tools will negotiate differently than an orchestrator dependent on a single source of necessary data. In "The Manifesto," this was called the Central Control Point, in "Three Elephants" - the entry holder. Rent goes there. If you are not at the node, you are a feature of someone else's CCP.

Now for the second half of the question - what do they pay for.

There are fewer people, more work, and your subscription is still per person. You congratulated the client on automation and got a reduction in the next year's bill. IDC predicts that by 2028, pure per-seat will become obsolete, and 70% of vendors will restructure prices around consumption, results, and other value units. Before copying another pricing grid, figure out what work disappears for you thanks to agents, and what starts to be performed more often.

The client needs results. The CFO needs a clear bill.

You can go further and sell the entire job completion. But count the people who are still doing it for the agent. A service business can be great. Only it needs to be counted as a service: linear scaling, cash gaps, and service margin, not software.

There may be fewer users. Your work may increase. Check what exactly you are charging for.

Fifth Sieve: When will your answer expire?

Especially the answer "the universal model cannot do this."

"We will make a special model." Okay. To run on your own hardware? For data inaccessible from the outside? For significantly better economics on the required load?

Or because the current universal one poorly performs three steps of your specific scenario and you had to invent twenty-seven workarounds?

Not every workaround deserves a separate line on the product committee's schedule.

Your budget is for three years. Someone else's release doesn't have to respect it. Will the next generation of models enhance your product or eliminate the reason for its existence?

If the value is now in the agent performing the work, the NG version - the Next Generation from the three-year roadmap - makes sense to build around it. The harness, the agent's wrapper: access to tools and data, permissions, memory, checks, recovery from failures. Useful parts of the old product can remain inside. The frontier model can be changed.

The harness also has an expiration date. Complexity that compensates for a model's weakness today may become extra work tomorrow. Compare your own construction with what a regular agent can already do, given the same tools and data with infinite tokens. This will be the standard subscription in six months.

Otherwise, you'll end up with a whistle development department. The most accurate in the industry. With support for 2600 Hz and built-in autotune.

Which are completely useless to anyone.

After sifting, different businesses may remain. A tool for other agents. A provider of observations or infrastructure. Your own agent that better solves a specific task. A service that takes responsibility for the result. An existing product that is still profitable to maintain and sell. Sometimes - nothing worth investing new money in.

Allow the answer "nothing" in advance. Otherwise, instead of a sieve, you'll get justification for an already approved budget. Look at yourself and the market. If the sieve can only be applied to competitors, it's a tool for corporate self-soothing.

Does the current version generate profit? Let it. But why would you spend money for three years on the next one?

Perhaps there is an explanation: the client has changed, new requirements have appeared, operations are eating into revenue, the market is moving to another way of solving the problem. Present it. Calculate it. Run it through Aykyn's sieve.

The history of successful sales of the previous product is not an argument.

It's an argument to continue sales. Submit the request for new development with prepayment from the sellers' pockets.

I'm not the only smart one. Forrester released the AI Disruption Model in August: 17 categories, two hundred markets, and among the factors are substitutability, business model, data, trust, and switching cost. Markets are divided into four baskets: disrupted, neutral, contested, accelerated. Aykyn's unit of analysis is the market. For Aykyn, it's currently a sieve, a cuckoo, cockroaches, and the unit is a specific purchase. The task is similar: to find out what will remain of the portfolio if the history of spent money is removed from the presentation.

The End of Productology and a New Beginning

When execution becomes cheaper, understanding the task becomes more expensive. Keegan in "A Vision For Product Teams" describes the shift from delivery to discovery - to figuring out what result makes sense to create at all.

For Aykyn, the wording is shorter.

Grow owners.

Look for them in all teams. In support, implementation, operations, analytics, presales. Where people already bypass inconvenient processes, write scripts, and create their suspiciously useful spreadsheets.

An SLA manager for consulting training suddenly sits down and writes a sufficiently good product. They don't have to be an excellent programmer and follow the hell of your corporate galley. But they know exactly what they want: what to consider a violation, where the data lies, what exceptions occur, and who to answer to later. They already have acceptance criteria. Previously, a queue of people stood between them and a working solution, who had to extract all this from them and package it into something. Now, they cover part of the distance themselves. Or all of it.

This is not yet a 100% proof of a market for 1000 Fortune 500 clients. But it's a person with whom it's worth testing such a hypothesis.

Give them access to the user, data, and a small budget. Give them the opportunity to release, measure the result, and redo. If, after a working prototype, they have to spend a year proving the right to initiative, the main limitation of your product organization has been found. It will coincide with the main reason for bad music in Russia. A subscription to a smarter model will not eliminate it.

Agree on what the owner gets paid for and what powers they have. If a career depends on the size of the team and maintaining its budget, the sieve will inevitably leak and show that the product is indispensable. A person should have the opportunity to prove that the old product is no longer needed and get the next project, not gratitude before layoffs.

The owner is responsible for the result, including the decision to build nothing further. Early exit is a saving, a regrouping; whoever demands the continuation of the feast pays for the desire to continue against reason.

In the old hacker culture, "show me the code" was a good way to separate talk from action. Now everyone who wants to will present code. With tests, documentation, a successful build badge, and amazing intuition. You need to see who needs it and why they will pay for it.

Talk is cheap. Code too.
