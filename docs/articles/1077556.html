Implementing AI in a Product: 9 Mistakes of a Product Manager

Implementing artificial intelligence into a product can easily turn into an expensive experiment with no clear business benefit. In this article, we'll break down 9 mistakes product managers make, from choosing the right problem and working with data to evaluating metrics, economics, and the quality of AI features. Hello, Habr. My name is Andrey Koptelov, I am a business architect with many years of experience at the intersection of business process management and corporate architecture.

One of my areas of development is education, where I manage several educational products. In recent years, I have been applying LLMs and AI agents in my practice.

In this article, I have analyzed 9 mistakes of a product manager when implementing AI in products.

The implementation of artificial intelligence (AI) in company products is no longer an exotic novelty; it has become routine and a standard user expectation.

However, for a product manager, this presents both an opportunity and a risk. The desire to quickly release "AI product functionality" often leads to the team spending resources on something that not only fails to solve real user problems but also has no impact on business metrics.

I have compiled 9 typical mistakes made when implementing AI in products, with examples from the education sector where I have experience.

Perhaps this checklist will help you avoid the pitfalls that others have already encountered. And yes, in this article, the terms AI agent, AI assistant, and chatbot are used interchangeably.

1. A Solution in Search of a Problem (AI for AI's Sake)

Product managers often propose implementing a chatbot assistant simply because "competitors have it" or "it's a trend," without verifying the hypothesis of real user benefit.

Real-life example: An online school launched a "career consultant" for its students, built on an AI agent, to help them choose their future development track.

However, surveys revealed that graduates didn't need a dialogue with a chatbot but a simple, up-to-date checklist of in-demand skills and links to the school's relevant courses.

Interacting with the chatbot was less convenient for users, and its support cost the company significantly more than maintaining an up-to-date checklist.

What to do: Before adding AI functionality to your product backlog, answer the question: what specific user problem are we solving?

If implementing an AI agent doesn't make the process faster, cheaper, or better compared to the existing solution, then it's okay to postpone the implementation for now.

2. Ignoring Data Quality (Garbage In, Garbage Out)

Practice shows that the expectation that a large language model (LLM) will magically sort through outdated or unstructured documents is often unfounded. Working with content is just as much a product task as understanding customer needs and designing a product's user interface.

Real-life example: An AI assistant was created on an educational platform and connected to the existing knowledge base. However, the content itself was not organized. As a result, the chatbot confidently began recommending students study materials on information systems that are no longer used in Russia, leading to user dissatisfaction.

What to do: Implementing AI agents begins with analyzing the correctness and relevance of existing content, as well as converting it into a machine-readable and structured format that AI can better process.

Therefore, it is essential to plan not only for content updates but also for its conversion into a machine-readable and structured format to improve the performance of AI agents. LLMs should only receive verified, labeled, and fresh data, and ideally, each piece of information should have a link to a specific course lesson.

3. Focusing on User Activity Metrics

In my opinion, measuring the success of implemented AI functionality by the number of requests and generated responses, instead of assessing its real impact on business or the learning process, is a risky path.

Real-life example: In one of the educational products, colleagues received a large number of student requests to the AI assistant. However, this did not reduce the number of requests to tutors (staff supporting students in the learning process) because students still asked them questions, not trusting the AI assistant's answers. This barely reduced the staff's workload.

What to do: Link the use of AI agents to existing process metrics, such as reducing the average time to grade assignments, decreasing the workload on tutors supporting the learning process, increasing the percentage of students completing the course on time, and so on. If the use of AI agents improves these metrics, you are on the right track.

4. Neglecting the "Human in the Loop" Principle

Attempting to fully automate critical processes or those involving subjective decisions, such as knowledge assessment, without the possibility of human oversight and appeal often leads to negative outcomes.

Real-life example: An AI-based essay grading system gave a student an unsatisfactory grade due to an overly standardized grading algorithm that didn't account for all possible nuances in the answer. There was no standard appeal process to the instructor, which resulted in a negative review from the student.

What to do: AI agents should handle routine tasks, leaving the processing of complex scenarios and arbitration of disputed situations to humans. There should always be a standard escalation path to a human, for example, a "clarify grade" button should be visible and accessible.

5. Underestimating the Economics of AI Implementation and Usage

When implementing AI, the focus is often solely on development costs, ignoring operational expenses, which include not only the cost of tokens but also infrastructure support.

Real-life example: A small online school launched a "Test your skills" promotion to generate leads. The service used calls to an external LLM.

It turned out that many users took the test multiple times, as at the end they received a badge with their final score, which they tried to improve. After a few days of operation, the LLM had to be urgently "removed" from the process, replacing it with a classic test, to prevent the LLM provider's bill from exceeding the marketing budget.

What to do: Calculate the cost of operational support during the AI functionality development phase for the product. Implement limits, such as daily request limits or routing simple questions to cheaper models. Or, if possible, do without AI altogether.

6. Data Privacy Issues

It is common to shift responsibility for data to the user or developers, while product decisions directly contribute to privacy violations.

Real-life example: Students, while completing assignments on process descriptions, might upload data from their employers to the platform. Since an external LLM is used to process assignments, this data could leave the organization's perimeter or even the country, potentially violating laws.

What to do: Product managers must proactively incorporate AI usage security into their product design. At a minimum, warnings should be added prohibiting the upload of confidential data. At most, information masking should be used, or even a local LLM should be deployed for processing.

7. Lack of Regular Work to Improve AI Quality

Designing a user journey assuming that an AI agent never makes mistakes and always provides factually accurate information is a dead end.

Real-life example: An AI assistant answered a student's question about calculating process costs. Based on this, the student completed an assignment that the instructor rejected due to an error in the calculation algorithm.

This raised questions about the AI assistant's performance quality.

What to do: Always manage user expectations through disclaimers like "AI can make mistakes, please verify the information." To improve the quality of AI agent responses, consider using retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) from a verified knowledge base, with a mandatory requirement in the LLM prompt to cite the source of the answer.

8. AI as an Add-on Feature, Not Part of the Process

Currently, AI is often implemented as a separate, isolated tab or service that requires extra steps from the user, instead of being a seamless part of the main workflow.

Real-life example: A "Help with assignment" button appeared on an educational platform. It opened a separate window where the student had to manually copy their task text and then copy the answer back into the learning platform window.

What to do: Make AI contextual and invisible. If a student makes a mistake in an answer on the platform, the AI assistant should highlight the line and offer a hint directly there, without forcing the user to navigate anywhere else.

9. Launching AI Functionality Without a Regular Feedback Loop

Treating AI functionality in a product as a completed project after implementation, without collecting data on how users interact with the AI, will lead to its rapid degradation.

Real-life example: An AI resume analysis assistant failed to include AI-related skills in its recommendations, which have become in-demand over the past year.

As a result, users lost interest in the service. The product team did not collect this feedback or adjust the algorithms, leading to user churn.

What to do: Implement feedback collection mechanisms from day one. This can be done through explicit methods like "useful/useless" buttons or implicit ones like reading time of a response or early exit from a dialogue. This data should be regularly analyzed to refine the product's algorithms.

In conclusion

AI is not a silver bullet, but a powerful tool. If you automate chaos, you will get scaled chaos multiplied by the cost of tokens.

For a product manager, working with the implementation of AI functionality in their product is not just about training LLM skills, but also about the "classic" experience of understanding user problems, working with metrics, controlling product economics, and designing a user-friendly journey.

Implement AI not because it's trendy, but because you've found a way to make your processes truly more effective.

The implementation of AI often starts with an idea: "let's add an assistant," "let's automate a process," "let's make the product smarter." But without hypothesis testing, clear metrics, and value calculation, such a feature can remain an expensive experimental option.
