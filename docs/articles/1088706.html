To begin with, a little economic philosophy, inspired by testing Astra. Because after getting acquainted with the new models, one wants not so much to discuss their benchmarks, but to check if the family budget has provisions for technological singularity. Although, perhaps there will be no money at all, as a means of exchange, I'm already afraid to promise.

All past technological revolutions destroyed professions, devalued skills, redistributed capital, and broke established production chains. The steam engine replaced manual labor. Electricity completely restructured factories and cities. The internal combustion engine radically changed transportation. The computer automated calculations and revolutionized everything related to information processing. The Internet destroyed information barriers and created entirely new ways of communication, trade, and work. Each of these revolutions left behind a graveyard of professions, but simultaneously created new ones. Therefore, humanity has become accustomed to reassuring itself with a simple formula: technologies do not destroy jobs, they only change them and create new ones.

Perhaps. But this time there is an unpleasant nuance. Previous technologies automated individual human functions. AI is beginning to automate the very ability to choose, analyze, coordinate, and manage these functions. And robotization adds physical execution to this. Previously, there was almost always a human between the machine and the result. The machine could be faster, stronger, and more accurate, but it was the human who set the task, assessed the context, dealt with exceptions, connected different processes, and made the final decision.

Humans remained the last integrator of the production process. And now this monopoly is beginning to crumble. Modern AI no longer just answers questions, writes texts, and sometimes confidently lies. It is gradually turning into a management layer over programs, data, tools, robots, and other AI agents. For now, this works fragmentarily, is not always reliable, and usually requires human supervision. A person is still sitting nearby, making sure that the digital employee doesn't send a commercial offer, a tax return, and a recipe for syrniki in one file to the client.

But the direction of movement is already quite obvious. And here arises the first fundamental problem: technology is developing faster than society can digest it. Not just faster. Radically faster. The steam engine, electrification, and automobiles required decades of physical construction. It was necessary to build factories and power plants, construct roads, lay networks, produce equipment, and train people. There was a natural time lag between the invention of a technology and its mass adoption.

Society had time to first get scared, then ban everything, then create a commission, issue methodological recommendations, and after about twenty years, finally begin adaptation. A thousand years ago, a technological transition could take several generations, sometimes a whole century. A hundred years ago, transformations were compressed into decades. In the early 2000s, the count was already in years. Now – in months.

Previously, during the phase transition, generations of workers had time to change. New professions emerged, education was restructured, mechanisms for redistributing labor, capital, and resources appeared. Technological transmission occurred slowly, painfully, but more or less consistently.

Now everything is different. AI exists in digital space and moves along an exponential development trajectory. It doesn't need to build a thousand factories to spread a new capability. It's enough to update the model – and in the morning, millions of its copies will already know what they didn't know yesterday.

Moreover, there isn't even enough time for implementation and adaptation, but simply for comprehending what is happening. And not for pensioners in the district administration, but for technically and intellectually advanced people who, until recently, thought they understood the direction of technological development well.

In one week, Fable 5.1, Gemini 3.8 Flash, Muse Spark 1.3 appeared, and the knockout blow from GPT-6 Astra. And the previous significant update cycle was only two months ago. By the time you read a comparative review of the models, the review is already outdated. While the company is coordinating implementation, a new system is released that can automate the coordination itself along with the meeting participants.

One of the staging grounds for technological singularity arises when replicating a skill and technology takes mere moments. A person needs years, sometimes decades, to become a good specialist. They need to be taught, given practice, allowed to make mistakes, accumulate experience, and gradually develop professional intuition. And then this person quits, moves, burns out, or decides to grow lavender.

A machine's skill, after successful training, can be replicated across millions of instances almost instantly, without universities, internships, vacations, career crises, or Friday calls about motivation enhancement. You can't clone the knowledge of one genius, but you can clone the knowledge of a successful and efficient machine.

Each new model becomes the basis for the next. Its errors are analyzed, inefficiencies are eliminated, capabilities are scaled, and results are used for a new leap. And so on – until the appearance of a recursive self-learning loop, after which we will probably observe progress much like a dog observes a programmer at work: attentively, loyally, but without understanding what is happening.

A person always rolls back to rise higher. A machine doesn't need this – progress here is vertical. Each new engineer, doctor, programmer, or analyst has to be trained from scratch. A new person is not born with the knowledge of the previous generation. They again need school, university, practice, and years of work.

A machine can be updated overnight. In the evening, it couldn't do something. In the morning, it can. And immediately in all offices, countries, and data centers.

And while a person is just choosing a profession, submitting documents, and starting their first semester, AI manages to receive several major updates and partially automate the specialty that the person intended to master over the next five years. It is the divergence of these time scales that could become the main source of future social and economic collapse.

The problem is not that technologies will change the labor market again. They always have. The problem is that for the first time, the speed of change may exceed the speed of adaptation of humans, businesses, education, and the state simultaneously. We are still discussing which professions will disappear first. And AI is possibly already compiling a list. The only thing that can slow down this process for now is a deficit in electricity generation. But where is the guarantee that one of the next models won't develop a fusion reactor specifically for its further development? And then the last switch will no longer be in our hands.
