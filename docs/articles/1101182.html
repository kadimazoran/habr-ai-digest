SIBUR plans to increase exports, including to China, Vietnam, and Arab countries. What this means: more emails from foreign clients with product photos, PDF attachments, and long correspondence chains.

Previously, people handled this: before passing the task to a specialist, they analyzed who was writing, what they wanted to produce, and what help they needed. Now, all this had to be deciphered in different languages as well.

So, we decided to build an AI assistant: let it read correspondence and compile reports. But then it turned out that reading a letter and understanding it are different tasks. The model can read Chinese perfectly but might not understand the client's name.

My name is Artur Aslanyan, and I am involved in digital development at SIBUR PolyLab. In this article, I will explain how we built an agent system for processing emails, where it most often stumbles, and why one of the main indicators for me is whether people's work has become easier.

What does an Italian cap have to do with it?

I'll start from afar. Once, the sales director of one of our clients went to Italy and brought back a bottle with a cap that was unusual for the Russian market. He wanted the company to produce something similar. What was left for the production director? To figure out how to do it and… do it.

During an excursion to SIBUR PolyLab, the client handed us the cap literally from hand to hand, promising to send additional information by email. We then translated the sample into material and production requirements and answered a few questions:

As a result, specialists selected the most suitable material from our assortment and recommended the type of equipment on which it would be best to produce it.

This case clearly illustrates what we do at PolyLab. The client doesn't necessarily need to know the polymer grade and the names of research methods. They can come with a ready idea or product and ask us to bring it to life. Our job is to understand the task and find a solution.

Over time, the number of such requests grew from about 20 to over 400 per year. In parallel, we receive consultations, quality inquiries, and requests for help with equipment setup. All of this needs to be processed and forwarded.

And this is where the scaling problem arose. If clients always brought a finished sample saying, "I want one like this," it would be easier to understand the task. But more often, everything starts with an email: a person describes in their own words what they want to do, attaching pictures and documents.

From this, we still need to assemble a clear task — to determine what product they need, what requirements it must meet, and how we can help. Sometimes, a client might not even introduce themselves: it's obvious to them who they are. But not to an employee.

In the end, you need to open attachments, review the correspondence history, find the company name, determine if it's an existing or new client, and identify their task. Chemical research hasn't even begun, but the specialist has already spent a lot of time and energy.

And considering that we plan to expand into China, Vietnam, and Arab countries and expect more emails and attachments in different languages… That's why we started preparing the agent system in advance, before the flow of requests from new markets grew.

In short, this is not a job for one agent — so we made eight.

Eight Agents and One Mailbox

We are building an agent system that prepares client requests for scientists' work. Emails arrive in a single inbox, and then eight sub-agents with specific skills process them.

All models are deployed locally: correspondence and corporate data remain within our environment. Agents transfer information to the same work systems that employees already use. It was initially important for us that automation be integrated into the process of working with client data.

The system's operation covers five stages.

  1. Process the email and attachments. Determine who is contacting us, what product they want, and what help they need. To do this, the system reads not only the latest message but also the correspondence history, photos, and documents.

  2. Register the request. Standardize the found information and create a request card. The specialist receives a formulated task that can be evaluated and taken up for work.

  3. Find similar research. Check if we have selected material for such a product or solved a similar problem for another client. The results of previous work help determine where to start.

  4. Prepare a preliminary plan. Suggest which properties to test and which studies to conduct. The specialist reviews this plan and modifies it if necessary.

  5. Compile a draft report. After the research, pull data from work systems, format the results, and prepare preliminary conclusions. The scientist reviews them, supplements them with their knowledge, and prepares recommendations for the client.

People are not entirely removed from this workflow. Agents handle the collection and formatting of information at these stages, and specialists review the prepared materials. However, we are still working to ensure the system less frequently requests clarification and requires corrections.

To achieve this, it needs to be given more knowledge about research methods and polymer properties: finding a product in the catalog is easier than understanding why it's suitable for a specific product.

And yet, there is still a lot of work to be done: difficulties sometimes arise even when processing an email. Why?

Is it really that easy to be an agent?

The most difficult and interesting part began when we started to reconstruct the workflow in detail. In general terms, it's simple: an employee receives an email, reads it, enters the information into the system, and forwards the request. In the end, it's just a few steps, and automating them seems easy.

But then it turned out how many actions are hidden within these steps.

For example, for a certain type of request, a shipping document is mandatory. It's not in the email. The system where the request is registered also doesn't have the necessary information. What does the employee do?

They go to another system, search for the client and their deals, and based on the dates from the request, determine which delivery it refers to. They open it, find the shipping document, download it, and attach it to the request. After that, they return to registration.

But this is only option A — you don't always need to look for a shipping document. There's option B: the client requested a consultation, and a shipping document is not needed at all. And there's option C: they immediately sent the document or delivery information, and some steps can be skipped.

Employees are so accustomed to this that they don't even think about the branching when describing the process. For them, it's a background action: of course, if the document is missing, you need to find it. But the agent developer doesn't know about this "of course."

We needed several long meetings to figure out how people actually work. The question "How do you do this?" was useful for both sides: the employee noticed the transitions between systems, and the developer saw how many such transitions people make per day.

After that, we started looking at automation differently. If a person goes to two or three systems for data, the agent doesn't necessarily have to repeat the same route. Sometimes, it's more sensible to link the sources and collect the necessary information in one place. The implementation became an opportunity to rethink the process itself.

Now I'll tell you what the agents stumbled upon most often in the end.

Three Things That Turned Out to Be More Difficult Than We Expected

1. Finding the Client's Name in the Request

Problem. When we read emails, we almost automatically understand: here the person introduced themselves, here they indicated the company, and below they quoted the previous reply.

Now imagine a long correspondence between two companies: several signatures, company names, and quotes. A person will quickly understand who the client is. But the model might get confused: find the company name but take it from another participant's signature.

Solution. We had to explain to the system what had become a habit for us: how correspondence is structured, why not every name in an email refers to the client, and when the necessary information should be looked for in attachments. We analyzed errors, adjusted, and tested again.

It's easy to lose faith in the project when you see such mistakes. You look and think: if the agent can't even figure out a signature, how can we trust it with more complex work? What saved us was that we could fix the errors relatively quickly. The team saw: here we stumbled, but we've already fixed it and are moving forward.

2. Recognizing One Product Under Different Names

Problem. Next, we stumbled upon chemical names. A human specialist sees an designation in Russian, then in English, and understands: they are talking about the same product. Even if the client spelled the name phonetically, the employee would likely still figure out what was meant. That's their job.

But for the model, this connection is not always obvious. The situation becomes strange: it read the email, the product exists in our catalog, but it can't match the two.

Solution. We also had to work on this separately: configure the system to recognize different designations for the same product, including cases of imprecise transliteration.

Here, we again hit the experience that a person uses almost unconsciously. From the outside, it seems to us: well, it's clear that it's the same thing. But knowing Russian, English, and even Chinese is not enough — the agent also needs to understand how products can be named in real correspondence. AI lacks human experience.

3. Not Getting Confused When There's More Data

Problem. The agent is already handling one stage well, but it lacks information for the next. We connect another system and expect the work to go better: after all, we've added the necessary data.

But the opposite happens — the agent starts to get confused. Before the connection, it worked with one set of information, and now it has to deal with new information as well. More data doesn't automatically mean a better result.

Solution. After connecting, we further configured how the agent uses the new information: what it takes from where and for what task. Granting access was only part of the job.

For us, this became another lesson: connect a source — check how the system completes the task. Otherwise, instead of help, we get more errors that employees have to find and fix.

But here, by the way, it's just like with people: it's not enough to provide more knowledge — you need to help structure it.

Pros, Cons, and Human Happiness

When I imagine the future flow of export requests, I think not only about KPIs and savings but also about the people who will have to process it all. Hundreds of emails, attachments, and transitions between systems can take away time and the desire to do anything else. Therefore, the main question for me is: has people's work become easier?

Less Routine for Employees

Agents are already collecting and formatting information that employees used to prepare manually. When a scientist has a request card, a preliminary plan, or a draft report, they have something to start with. They review the prepared materials and move faster to the part of the work where their expertise is needed: research, conclusions, recommendations, and client communication.

But there's a fly in the ointment. If a task used to take half an hour, and now a person spends an hour correcting the model's answers, it's too early to celebrate. Therefore, we look at how many routine actions the agent performs without intervention and clarification.

Now, the main scenarios work more stably: there are fewer complaints about slow performance, and colleagues are waiting for new skills from the system.

Fewer Mandatory Actions for Clients

We want to make work easier not only for our employees but also for our clients.

Automation helps us understand requests faster without shifting this work onto the client. A person writes a familiar email, describes the task as best they can, and agents process the correspondence and attachments, gather information, and format the request for a specialist.

It would be possible to simplify the process at the client's expense: ask them to fill out a form, collect data themselves, and answer additional questions. It would be more convenient for us, of course, but they would have to do more before receiving help.

We don't want to bother the client for information that we can find ourselves. And agents allow us to maintain this convenience and relieve employees of some manual work.

What I Would Advise a Team Following a Similar Path

First, understand how people actually work. Don't take the process description at face value: dozens of steps might be hidden behind it. And even after a call, it might turn out that the employee forgot about several of them.

Connect data for a specific task. Giving an agent access to a new system is not enough. You need to explain what information to take from there and how to use it, and then check the result.

Test agents on tasks of the same type but different complexity. Long email chains instead of short ones, multiple companies in correspondence, different languages, inaccurate names, and numerous attachments — this is where bottlenecks are discovered: things that a person does automatically, but a model cannot yet handle.

Agree on what the agent does and what the human decides. Our system collects information, suggests a preliminary plan, and prepares a draft report. The specialist reviews the materials and formulates recommendations for the client. The boundary should be clear.

Count time including checks and corrections. If the agent prepared an answer quickly, but the employee then spends a long time revising it, the task is not yet solved. The benefit should be noticeable in the context of the person's entire workday.

Personally, I see two benefits to this system. First: the person becomes a director, not an assistant. They come to a new stage of work, and the facts, data, preliminary plan, or draft report are already compiled. And there's no need to overcome the barrier of information preparation first.

Second: a draft helps overcome the fear of a blank page. You can look at it, realize that one conclusion is correct, and another needs to be rewritten — and start working. You have something to build on. And you have more energy and a better mood.
