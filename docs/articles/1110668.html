How I Stopped Being a Dispatcher for My AI Agents

I used to constantly check on my AI agents, like a nervous tic. With one agent, it was simple: give a task, discuss, review code. But with multiple parallel sessions, I found myself acting as a dispatcher, moving context between planning and implementation, explaining things to reviewers. Agents took over the coding but turned me into a manager: responding to this one, watching that one, waiting for another. If left unsupervised, they could get stuck in loops or endless review cycles. I realized I was recreating the same inefficient handoffs I'd tried to eliminate in human development. The key is to separate roles only where it's beneficial and ensure context flows smoothly without my constant intervention. For example, separating verification from implementation is useful when errors are costly, like in the evaluator-optimizer pattern where one AI checks another's work. A large context window doesn't solve the problem of AI losing details or getting stuck; separate reviewers with clear criteria and diffs work better. I explored various agent orchestration tools, finding many to be rushed and unreliable. I settled on Multica for development, OpenBot for personal tasks, and Paseo as a backup. The main benefit has been reclaiming my time and attention, no longer needing to constantly monitor the agents. The focus should be on how much time and attention we gain back, not just how many agents are running.

For a long time, I had a laptop with agents open next to my work monitor. Every twenty to thirty minutes, I'd catch myself glancing at it: had anyone gotten stuck on a question, or started going in circles? I didn't consciously decide to do this; the habit formed on its own, like a nervous tic. When there was only one agent, it was simple: give a task, discuss, look at the code. When multiple sessions started running in parallel—in different branches or repositories—they still wrote code quickly, but I had to manually transfer the results from planning to implementation, re-explain the context, and tell the reviewer what to check. The agents took over the coding but subtly turned me into a dispatcher: responding to this one, keeping an eye on that one, this one stuck in the middle waiting.

And if you just let them run freely without clear limitations, some truly comical things can happen. Once, I looked at a task that had been open for six hours, and the reviewer was calmly on their fourteenth round of review. I looked at it and thought, "Well done, fourteenth round, this time it'll definitely work," stopped the process, and gave the task to an architect to first figure out what we were actually fixing. If each new run finds fresh remarks, it means the problem statement itself is flawed, and we need to take a step back.

Here, I was the first to catch myself in a contradiction. For years, we've been fighting handoffs in regular development: the product owner describes the task, the engineer clarifies and does it, the tester checks, the product owner looks—and it turns out it was something else. While the task goes in circles, more time is spent waiting and regaining context than on the work itself; I called this the "tax on work handoffs" in my article on the product engineer role. And now I'm about to build exactly the same conveyor belt of analyst, developer, and reviewer out of agents. If I push every task through all roles and manually transfer context between them, it's truly a step backward—you just have to wait for your own bots instead of colleagues.

The difference appears when roles are separated only where there's measurable benefit, and context flows between them without my involvement. I'm still responsible for the final code, but there can be multiple executors. For example, it makes sense to separate verification from implementation when an error is costly: in Anthropic's analysis of agent schemes, alongside simple chains, there's a pattern called evaluator-optimizer where one model call checks the work of another.

Why a Second Agent When the Context is Already a Million Tokens

You might think that with a million-token window, one agent would suffice: let it finish the task and check itself in the same conversation. Imagine a common scenario—a list of records with a status filter. Somewhere early in a long session, the agent decided that without a filter, only active records are shown, wrote code and tests based on this assumption, and everything is green for it. If you give the result to a separate reviewer who only receives the criterion "show all records without a filter" and the diff, they won't know about the forgotten assumption from the old conversation and will immediately see a discrepancy.

A long window itself doesn't save you from this, because the model starts losing details within it. In the study "Lost in the Middle" by Nelson Liu and co-authors, on a test with twenty documents, GPT-3.5-Turbo answered correctly 75.8% of the time when the relevant document was first, and 53.8% when it was in the middle of the list. Moreover, the more new text is input, the longer you wait for the first token, and the more expensive each subsequent step becomes. In my experience, when the context grows significantly, the model starts forgetting agreements, getting stuck on its version of events, and going in circles.

Asking the same agent to simply "re-check" also works less effectively than you might expect. In the work by Jie Huang and co-authors, GPT-4 on a sample of 200 GSM8K tasks, after self-correction without external signals, dropped from 95.5% to 91.5%—the model corrected some of its own correct answers. Therefore, in practice, I tie verification either to a reproducible test or to a separate reviewer who looks at the diff and acceptance criteria with an empty context, without the entire history of our discussions.

In practice, a second agent is needed in three different cases: when a fresh perspective is needed for review (ideally by another model from a different provider), when there are independent subtasks that can be separated into different worktree without shared mutable state, and when you want to differentiate models by cost and quality—for example, a cheaper model writes the code, and a more expensive one does the review. With parallel work, it's important not to be fooled: if you let two agents into the same frontend, they'll create merge conflicts in different worktree, and in the same branch, they'll simply fight each other. And none of these scenarios, by themselves, require an orchestrator—you can copy a file to a second session by hand. A team with automation is needed when these manual transfers become a daily routine and you want them to happen without you.

How I Explored Tools and Why Half of Them Failed

There are now more tools for orchestrating agents than you can physically open. The awesome-agent-orchestrators catalog had 350 live projects and 19 more in the Resting section as of November 19th, with 36 new items added in the ten days from November 8th to 18th. I get sent a new one almost every day, and I find myself thinking: "When will you people stop?" Most of what I've opened feels rushed and raw, so there's no point comparing promises on landing pages and in READMEs.

To avoid drowning in names, I started dividing tools by what exactly I intended to manage: sessions (like in Paseo), background tasks with diff output (cloud Codex, Devin), teams with roles and handoffs (Multica, Paperclip, Agent Swarm), or personal assistants (Hermes Agent, OpenBot). The team here isn't above sessions; they're just different tasks, and the boundaries between classes are quite blurred. But this perspective immediately helps cut through the noise.

Next, I test any tool with one live scenario: connect my runtime, set a task, wait for a blocker notification where I expect it, and accept the result. Recently, I decided to try Agent Swarm—the setup wizard cheerfully announced that everything was checked, but the very first task failed with a gateway error. I looked through the logs and couldn't figure out where I went wrong or what exactly I'd configured incorrectly. Multica and OpenBot worked for me from the first try. It turned out that even when you've been working with agents for a long time, a clear interface and predictable behavior outweigh a long list of features in the documentation.

The Team in Multica: Where It Helps and Where It Breaks

In Multica, I initially created fourteen agents just to understand how the tool worked—it sounds like I'm already the director of a small department. In practice, you don't need that many roles for constant work; I created some for experimentation. As of November 19th, the cumulative counter on the board showed 763 runs for the team lead and 208 for the developer. This gap is due to the role: my team lead acts as a single entry point, knows which project is which and who is working on what, takes the result after the executor, and decides where to send the task next. Plus, at first, I constantly asked him "how are things?" while I was getting used to it. Two reviewers work in parallel on different runtimes: one via Claude (claude-opus-5-5, 48 runs), the second via Pi (83 runs). Tasks live on a configurable Kanban board, and when my decision is needed, the team lead mentions me, and the issue lands in the Inbox. Once, I opened the screen during the day, and there was an escalation pending for 29 minutes—I didn't even know about it because I stopped sitting and watching the tab.

Before an agent starts doing anything, I ask it to rephrase the task in its own words—just like with a junior: "How did you understand the task?" Often, you can tell from the rephrasing that it's about to solve something else, and it's easier to correct it immediately than to discard a finished branch later. Everything that can be checked by tests, linters, and a clear list of criteria, the team handles itself, while I keep data migrations, security, and product decisions for myself.

However, even with tests and criteria, common sense is still needed for acceptance by the agents. Besides that case with fourteen review rounds, another time an agent came to me with the idea to raise the timeout for mutation tests: "The tests take 180 minutes, we need to increase the timer." I replied directly: "Are you crazy, 180 minutes for tests? Redo the test." And it immediately realized: "Oh, indeed, how did I not notice." Until you point it out, it won't see such absurdity, so the final acceptance remains with me.

Kief Morris, a consultant at Thoughtworks, in his article on Martin Fowler's site, calls this mode "human on the loop" (on the loop): the human doesn't correct every line of code for the agent but the harness itself—specifications, checks, and operating rules. This is exactly how I try to act: when the result is bad, I go fix the instructions, criteria, or process. And in case the agent forgets to ask a question or tasks quietly stall after a failure, I run a regular check on an inexpensive DeepSeek Flash model. With fresh context, it reviews active tasks, looks for architectural problems and stalled tests, and notifies me if something has gone wrong. Once a day, I get a summary of all projects—what's been done and what decisions were made, and if desired, you can chat with the team directly in Telegram.

For tasks outside the development backlog, I keep a personal assistant separately—this is a different class of tools. Multica answers questions about what's happening with the code and the board, while the assistant (I used Hermes Agent from Nous Research for a long time, now I've moved to OpenBot) handles everything that requires memory between sessions, a built-in browser, scheduling, and access to the terminal on my machine. It's not advisable to mix this with a development orchestrator: different circuits, different permissions, and different risks.

Money, Security, and Vendor Lock-in

On the Multica expenses screen, I saw a figure that surprised me: about $1,000 for the week. In reality, I don't owe anyone a thousand dollars—when you make a request, some vendors return the price, and the system estimates what it would cost if paid via API. In fact, I paid with subscriptions: Claude for $100 and OpenCode Go, from which I get DeepSeek, and the week was a peak because I was actively running agents on several projects simultaneously. But you should still look at token consumption, because subscriptions mask the orchestrator's real appetite: someone who tried Paperclip right after its release said that its tokens flew away instantly.

Security is an even more serious issue. Plugins in such solutions often run outside a sandbox with the same permissions as your user, and it's physically impossible to check all the source code of third-party integrations. I definitely wouldn't install any of this on corporate devices without verification. The source code for Multica and OpenBot is open—if desired, you can fork it, read it, audit it, build your own version, and get approval for use. But before installing, it's worth checking the licenses too: Multica has its own license based on Apache-2.0, which allows internal use within one organization but prohibits hosting the service for third parties or embedding it in a commercial product without a separate license; OpenBot has a PolyForm Noncommercial license, which does not permit commercial use.

Why bother with your own servers when major vendors already have OpenAI dots and Claude Managed Agents? I like the same dots: a personal persistent agent in ChatGPT, Slack, and Teams with its own cloud computer and connectors, but it selects models itself, and you can't connect a third-party provider like DeepSeek in the announcement. With Claude Managed Agents, you can deploy the execution environment yourself and route inference through Amazon Bedrock, Google Cloud, Microsoft Foundry, or your own gateway, but the models there are still tied to Claude identifiers. If one provider is blocked tomorrow or the terms change, migrating tasks, roles, memory, and acceptance rules from there will be painful. I want to change the model and provider without rebuilding the entire team and workflow from scratch, and open-source tools that run on my server leave me that freedom.

What Stuck in the End

Currently, I have a combination of three tools: Multica for development, OpenBot for personal matters and anything requiring a browser, and Paseo as a backup when I just need to work with multiple sessions. The first two are still raw and sometimes work strangely, but they are convenient for me, and everything started up without much hassle.

If you're trying this yourself, I'd start with one task, one executor, and one verifiable acceptance rule, and add a second agent when it's clear why it's needed. A team and orchestrator are not always necessary: they require setup time and consume a lot of tokens, so they justify themselves when manual transfers between sessions have truly become a bottleneck.

For myself, I felt the main result in one everyday detail: the agents window no longer hangs constantly next to my work monitor. I glance at it in the morning and evening, I get notifications during the day only if I'm absolutely needed, and a summary arrives once a day. And now I try to ask myself not how many agents I have running, but how much time and attention I've regained.
