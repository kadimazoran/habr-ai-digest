In the second, it fires managers and leaves the company without revenue. The audience believes one or the other – depending on what happened at the last webinar.

Where it works like a full-fledged employee – it fetches data from various products, prepares summaries, and makes changes itself. And where I still treat it with distrust.

AI in Sales – Four Years Nothing Changed, and Everyone Decided to Storm

I'll start with the market. The sixth edition of the State of Sales 2026 report by Salesforce: salespeople spend 30% of their working time on actual sales. The remaining 70% goes to non-selling tasks – data entry, preparing proposals, internal meetings. In the 2022 edition, it was 28%. Four years, dozens of new tools – and a two percentage point increase.

The market reaction is predictable. According to the same study, nine out of ten sales teams are already using AI agents or plan to implement them within two years. The picture in Russia is similar: according to a joint survey by hh.ru and Touch Instinct, 26% of employees regularly use neural networks, and another 35% use them from time to time. Neural networks for business have transformed from pilot projects into working tools, and every fourth employer is already planning to check proficiency in them during hiring.

Everyone talks about how AI saves time. Almost no one talks about how to check its work – although, in my opinion, that's where the decision on whether it was worth implementing or not is made. This article is dedicated to that.

My Rule for Implementing AI – Cheaper to Check Than to Do

So much has been written about offloading routine to robots that I won't repeat it. My filter is different.

I only give a task to AI if checking the result is cheaper than doing the work myself.

A summary of six metrics takes an hour and a half by hand. Checking it takes two minutes: a glance at the list of deals that make up the numbers. Asymmetry in my favor, I offload.

AI will draft a strategic plan for the quarter in a minute. Checking it will take a week, and the check will require all the expertise for which I get paid in the first place. Asymmetry against me, I keep it.

From this rule, three zones emerge naturally:

  • Offload without hesitation – calculations, summaries, task assignments from a list, answers based on documentation: checked by eye in minutes;

  • Offload with preview – anything that changes records in the system: the assistant shows what it intends to do, I confirm;

  • Never offload – decisions about people, strategy, anything where you discover the mistake later through financial losses.

And the second rule, derived from the first: trust is built in stages. First, AI only reads and calculates. Then it searches and compares. And only then does it create records – with confirmation at each step. At first, I rejected every third suggestion in preview. I didn't understand how the assistant interpreted my wording, and rejection was cheaper than a surprise.

What Has Changed in the Department

The sales summary was the first thing offloaded to the assistant. One request – and a few minutes later, a report on six metrics is ready: leads, demos, calls, meetings, renewals, new sales. By my estimates, AI has taken more than half the time spent on this type of managerial routine.

But that's not the main change. With six metrics in one table, things became visible that were impossible to see in different sections of the system: leads are growing, but demos are declining. Applications are coming in, but not leading to meetings. Previously, this would surface after a month, when the plan was missed. Now – on the same Friday.

Project status with one question. Previously, to understand what was happening in a project, I would open the board, go through tasks one by one, and read comments. Now I ask the assistant: what has been completed on the project? It goes through the tasks itself and brings a summary – what's closed, what's in progress, where it's stuck. Five minutes of reading the board turn into one question.

Working with others' task descriptions. Descriptions are written differently here: some are a page long, others are like telegrams. The assistant reads the description and compiles a checklist for the task or condenses it into a couple of sentences – what exactly needs to be done. The developer doesn't have to reread a wall of text, and I don't have to figure out what was meant.

Mass task assignment. Once a month, a pool of similar tasks for a department is gathered – thirty to forty items. Previously, the list was sent to the responsible parties in chat, and after a month, it turned out that a quarter of the tasks were lost, and the rest had empty fields. Now the assistant creates tasks with descriptions, author, and assignee, fills in the department, sprint, funnel stage, effort, and category. Half a day's work is compressed into fifteen minutes. And the fields that people filled out haphazardly are always filled – and since the data is there, the same AI collects analytics on it.

Project rollout. At the launch of a department, the assistant assembled the entire framework: structure, tasks with descriptions and deadlines on the board, access rights for executors. Two days of preparatory work – three hours, most of which was spent on my review.

Technical support. More than half of inquiries are handled by a bot using our documentation: it resolves routine questions itself and forwards non-trivial ones to an operator. Specialists handle complex cases, and clients get answers immediately.

Onboarding and adjacent departments. New hires get acquainted with the product by asking questions instead of a week-long orientation program. Marketers stopped going to development for explanations, and the PR manager prepares the product for rating submission without delving into technical details – they need an answer to a specific question, and documentation via AI provides it in a minute.

An AI Agent That Works Like an Employee

Before the assistant and connecting external AI via MCP, all my experiments hit a wall: the model didn't have access to corporate data. The classic approach – export CSV from CRM, upload to ChatGPT, write a prompt. It works for one-off tasks, but quickly falls apart for regular routine:

  • the export becomes outdated the moment it's created – a summary based on yesterday's export is wrong today, and by how much is unknown;

  • customer data is sent to foreign servers;

  • the chat only advises – even with perfect analysis, you return to the system and manually assign tasks.

The key shift happened when AI gained access to corporate data. An assistant within the system – essentially a CRM with AI – works directly with the portal: it finds deals, tasks, projects, and finances within my permissions, creates and modifies records after my confirmation.

External AI via MCP does the same with other services and documents – it collects data from various products, makes changes to them, and maintains the overall work context. There was a separate article about MCP; here I'll state the main point: this is no longer a chat you need to bring data to. It's an employee who fetches it themselves – and has something no chat has ever had: the right to make changes to my systems.

The result is returned to where the data came from: as tasks, records, summaries. The universal bot remains with me for texts not tied to data.

AI Hallucinations and the Price of Trust

The first example is analytics. I asked it to review three years of data and suggest hypotheses. I received a neat, structured document with conclusions like "sales are lower in summer because of vacations." For a specific question about a particular segment – hypotheses that can be tested: after what event do renewals drop off, what triggers a drop in conversion for clients in a certain industry. AI is good for data analysis only as long as the question is narrow. A broad question always yields banality, a narrow one – material for solutions.

About fabricated facts and figures. I had fewer of them than boundary errors: the assistant invented almost nothing; it was too literal in deciding what to count as a renewal. This is the most insidious type of error – the numbers are real, the interpretation is not.

The second is interpretation. The first month, I rejected every third suggestion in preview: the assistant understood my wording differently than I intended. Not critical – with a preview, the error is visible before execution. But it was this month that explained to me why implementing AI with write permissions from the start is dangerous: until you catch it in a discrepancy a few times, you don't know where it's lying.

And here's what's important: both stories are not about weak AI. A 2025 study by Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon University showed a pattern: the higher the trust in a tool, the less effort a person expends checking its answers. And the reverse effect: people confident in their own skills check more actively. AI errors in my practice were correctable – more dangerous is my own habit of stopping the checks.

After this, I developed a foolproof procedure: for the first month, every new automation is duplicated with manual verification. Every summary, every report. After a month, discrepancies become predictable, and verification shifts to a selective mode.

Distrust Checklist

Everything I've learned in these months fits into four points.

Ask for a list, not a number. You can't see what a number is made of. The same request asking to show the deals that make up the number – and an error in the selection boundaries is immediately visible.

Duplicate manually for the first month. For every summary – parallel manual selection. This is the only way to find out what discrepancies exist in your data.

Suspect good numbers. A bad number triggers an automatic check – it's unpleasant, and the brain wants to disprove it. A good one slips past a critical gaze: you don't want to check something pleasant. Growth above plan in a declining month, smooth beautiful dynamics, improvement without reason – these are what are checked first.

Leave decisions to yourself. AI is your adversary, whom you must check, look for loopholes in, doubt. So that it doesn't use you, but you use it. And don't expect a strategy on the first request: it takes about six months to get to an AI that understands you and your data well.

Instead of a Conclusion

The manager is left with the main thing: to think and make decisions – now with a broader scope of data than I could gather manually. The decision doesn't automatically become correct. It becomes as close to correct as possible, and the responsibility for it is still mine.

The map of what to offload and what not to is not tied to our product – the rule "cheaper to check than to do" will work with any AI connected to your data.
