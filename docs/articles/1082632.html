How to Turn Habr from a Social Media Feed into a Library and Knowledge Base

Habr presents content as a social media feed: readers primarily engage with new posts and discussions, and after a couple of weeks, an article fades from view. It might resurface if external search engines or users from other platforms link to it. The feed favors frequent and voluminous publications. However, over 20 years, the platform has accumulated a rare knowledge base: some old articles still answer current questions, are saved in bookmarks, and their topics remain relevant for decades. I understand that every project needs to survive and profit. To Habr's credit, readers can focus on content without distracting ads and banners! But this advantage also has a downside: advertising becomes less overt and is woven into the text. You start reading an article and find links to Telegram channels from independent authors urging subscriptions. Corporate blogs recommend hosting virtual machines, AI chatbot aggregators, doses of "neuro-slop" for SEO, promoting company HR brands to create candidate queues, and so on. This situation is unlikely to improve. Moderation and platform policies won't save readers. The balance between the commercial interests of selling corporate subscriptions and motivating independent, unpaid enthusiast authors to create and publish useful material is disrupted. Knowledge Base from 34 Thousand Habr Articles

This is my subjective selection. I believe the most interesting or resonant materials here have either a rating of 100+, or the publication has 100+ comments, or it has been saved to bookmarks 100+ times, or read more than 100 thousand times. In total, the sample included about 10% of the total number of available articles on the resource as of mid-June 2026.

I already discussed which fields and data are extracted from each text in my previous publication "Non-Corporate Habr: Semantic Search and Filtering by Structured Fields in the Browser (llama.cpp in WebAssembly)". This time, I increased the scope of materials and the costs for their processing using Gemma-4-31B. Extracting features from these articles cost $75; clustering the results costs much less, but there were significantly more experiments with grouping and measuring their quality.

Statistics on Article Processing and Process Description

  1. Corpus.

    • 34,270 articles from 2006–2026. Each article is a markdown file with metadata: title, date, author, hubs, tags, views, rating, bookmarks, comments.

    • The application scans the folder and distributes metadata into database fields.

    • Selection criteria: rating of 100+, or 100+ comments, or 100+ bookmarks, or 100 thousand+ views.

  2. The model reads the article.

    • For each article, the model returns a JSON according to the schema, with about 30 fields: title, abstract, meaning, TL;DR, keywords, topics, key takeaways, audience, sentiment, genre, document type, subject area, completeness, contradictions, demagoguery techniques, advertising, personal data, NSFW.

    • The analysis result is in English; the reason for this is to save tokens and for better performance with English by compact local LLM models.

    • The response is validated against the schema.

    • Model: google/gemma-4-31b-it via an OpenAI-compatible API. The application can also work with local Ollama and llama.cpp.

    • The corpus used 266 million input and 52 million output tokens, averaging 7.8 thousand and 1.5 thousand per article.

    • Analysis via OpenRouter cost $75.30 — about $0.002 per article, or $2.20 per thousand articles.

    • The process ran in 30 parallel threads. The median call lasted 53 seconds; the entire corpus took 30 hours of active work, and with breaks, two days.

  3. Embeddings.

    • Separate vectors are generated for the abstract, meaning, and audience, as well as for each topic, subject, and takeaway.

    • Model: Qwen3-VL-Embedding-2B, totaling 384,756 vectors of dimension 2,048.

    • Everything is stored in a single DuckDB database, approximately 13.6 GB in size.

  4. Groups and Names.

    • HDBSCAN on a graphics card (CUDA or Vulkan) divides articles into groups, with a minimum of 3 articles per group.

    • The model names each group in Russian, describes common features, and can exclude irrelevant articles. Excluded articles are offered to neighboring groups.

    • Then, the group names themselves are grouped into tree levels, and articles outside groups are attracted by the vote of their nearest neighbors.

    • Quality is checked using metrics and an LLM judge: outlier test, title verification.

    • LLM calls for clustering cost $33.92, making the entire experiment cost around $109.

  5. What resulted in the "Meaning" space.

    • 30,300 articles (88%) are organized into 7,245 groups.

    • Tree from the habr_meaning export: 7,245 → 1,516 → 203 → 19 sections.

Grouping Materials

After features are extracted from articles and their embeddings are calculated, it's time for clustering.

When creating a new collection, I select the "Meaning" field and run the HDBSCAN algorithm in my application, which groups semantically similar articles using their embeddings. For each group, an LLM generates its name and description, outlining the common features of the publications within the group and identifying articles that the neural network considers unsuitable. Once all leaf groups are created by the algorithm, agglomerative clustering is used to form a tree structure.

Then, the hierarchy of article groups can be exported to HTML, Markdown, JSON, or XML. In the case of Markdown, you can use your familiar Obsidian and work with the prepared knowledge base within it. Alternatively, you can open/publish your material grouping as a static website with the ability to view the connections between groups in 3D mode.

I have published the results of grouping article values on GitHub Pages.

Problems for Readers and Authors of Technical Publications

The social media-style feed helps retain the audience and encourages them to return to Habr reading repeatedly. However, what works well for short news and photos of Friday cats is counterproductive for reading complex and lengthy technical publications. After a publication's position in the feed is overtaken by newer ones, the chances of that information being read, appreciated, or commented on diminish significantly. Does your target audience want to spend a lot of time scrolling, or do they appear spontaneously for very short periods?

And this is where it gets interesting. Article titles sometimes don't match the actual content, the publication is added to the wrong hubs, keywords are added randomly or jokingly. Also, the current hub taxonomy system is just one possible projection of thematic classification. Yet, an article also has conclusions, additional topics, and target audiences, by which one would also like to search. And this work is better offloaded to a neural network; it can monotonously, with minimal deviation from expectations, provide the article title by meaning, a brief summary, keywords, and the target audience for each material. Authors often cannot find out what has already been written on a given topic and repeatedly cover the same ground with minimal novelty. Yet, there are topics that can be discussed again and again if "packaged" in a unique authorial wrapper.

How This Can Improve Habr

With this approach, "Topic Pages" could be automatically generated, and later edited by humans to correct inaccuracies and artificiality. This would serve as a navigation method through the vast volume of materials in the technical library.

Based on this grouping, a "Previously Written on This Topic" block could be added under an article, allowing the author to see what has already been written and where they can be more original.

Fields like "tone," "demagoguery techniques," etc., could be added to articles so readers can understand in advance whether the publication is worth reading.

You can explore the classification and grouping of 34,000 Habr articles by meaning and view the semantic groups in a 3D navigator at https://igor-suhorukov.github.io/habr_articles/.

Conclusions

There is a lot of interesting and useful content on Habr – what's missing is navigation through what the community has written over the past 20 years. The feed shows the latest, and the built-in search only finds what you know how to look for. Hubs were created historically, and valuable materials await a random click from Google or Yandex. Grouping articles by meaning fills precisely this gap. The model processes articles and extracts structured information from them based on specified fields, embeddings and clustering algorithms group similar items together, and LLM gives these groups meaningful names, while agglomerative clustering organizes the groups into a tree. Both humans and AI agents can search this tree to fulfill user requests.

Moreover, this proved achievable for one person – 34,000 articles cost $109, including numerous experiments and bug fixes for the clustering program. This same approach will work for your own texts and notes, documentation, and material archives. It might even be possible to do this on a home computer or laptop with a powerful graphics card (16GB VRAM or more) if you don't want to send personal data to cloud APIs.

I would be glad to receive comments on the quality of the groups and their names: which topics were split, which were merged into one group, and by what criteria you would find it more interesting to search for articles.
