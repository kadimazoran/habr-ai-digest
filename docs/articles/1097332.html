How do you choose the right model when fine-tuning each one can cost hundreds of thousands of dollars?

In this article, I'll explain why conventional tests for code-writing models poorly predict what kind of agent a model will become, how NVIDIA tests models using records of others' work, and how this benefits companies hiring virtual employees.

Why Popular Code Generation Model Tests Have Stopped Working

One of the most well-known tests emerged in July 2021. OpenAI introduced Codex, which later formed the basis for GitHub Copilot, and along with it released the HumanEval benchmark, a set of 164 tasks where the model must write a small Python function based on a description.

The authors created the tasks themselves because Codex was trained on a large portion of GitHub, which contained ready-made solutions to problems from open collections. The authors admitted that their tasks were similar in difficulty to simple programming interview questions. However, even with these tasks, Codex only solved 28.8% on the first try, while GPT-3 failed to solve any.

For the next few years, the result on HumanEval remained one of the main metrics in announcements of new code generation models. This meant that the industry was selecting future AI programmers based on a test suitable for an initial junior-level screening call.

There's an interesting coincidence in this story. In 2017, Google researchers proposed the transformer architecture, which now underlies almost all language models, from GPT to Claude. The first author of that paper was Ashish Vaswani. In OpenAI's paper on Codex, he is thanked for his help in discussing the draft, and five years later, he was among the authors of the NVIDIA paper.

This means the person who helped discuss the paper that introduced HumanEval also co-authored a paper where this test was weakly correlated with the final quality of agents, and even in the opposite direction.

How Test Tasks Ended Up in Training Data

HumanEval has another problem. Its tasks have long been publicly available, and models learn from open data from the internet.

In 2023, researchers from Berkeley found 8% to 18% of HumanEval tasks in the popular RedPajama and StarCoder-Data training sets. The Allen AI team, while collecting data for the Tülu 3 model, discovered an open dataset that matched HumanEval by 70.7%. And the Llama 3 report directly admits that the method for finding matches finds so many overlaps with HumanEval that it was impossible to assess how much they inflated the model's results.

Over time, a more complex test, SWE-bench Verified, replaced HumanEval. It consists of 500 real-world tasks from 12 open Python projects. The tasks are taken from GitHub issues where users report bugs and request enhancements, and the agent must solve each one such that the project's tests pass. In 2024, OpenAI selected these tasks from an earlier SWE-bench created at Princeton, and each was independently verified by three developers.

On February 23, 2026, OpenAI announced that it would stop publishing results on SWE-bench Verified. The reason was that the company re-verified 138 tasks that its o3 model consistently failed to solve and found errors in the tests or task descriptions in 59.4% of cases. Furthermore, GPT-5.2, Claude Opus 4.5, and Gemini 3 Flash were able to reproduce the reference fix (the edit originally made by the project developers) or verbatim details of the problem statement for some tasks using the task number or a short hint.

OpenAI compared this to students receiving assignments and answers before an exam. Even if they don't memorize everything, they will still perform better than those who haven't seen the answers.

After this, instead of SWE-bench Verified, OpenAI recommended switching to SWE-Bench Pro, and less than five months later, they re-evaluated it. An automated filter identified 286 suspicious tasks, which were then analyzed in parallel by AI agents and engineers, with each task reviewed by five experienced developers.

As a result, OpenAI concluded that about 30% of the tasks in this benchmark are broken, meaning they cannot be solved honestly according to the instructions.

This is clearly illustrated by an example task from the OpenLibrary project, which OpenAI discusses in its report. The model is asked to standardize the formatting of table of contents lines. The prompt shows an example with a single space at the beginning, but the hidden tests expect two spaces, causing the model, which honestly follows the prompt, to fail the task.

Thus, in five years, the industry has gone through three popular tests for AI programmers, and each has had significant problems. In the first two, parts of the tasks and solutions were included in the models' training data, while a significant portion of the tasks in the third were found to be flawed.

Why Choosing a Model for an Agent is So Expensive

To understand the problem NVIDIA addressed, one must know how an agent model is created.

It's done in two stages. In the first stage, called pre-training, the neural network reads trillions of tokens of text and code and learns to predict what comes next.

This results in a base model. It can be compared to a graduate who has read the entire library but has never worked a day, so theoretically, they know a lot, but in practice, they can neither hold a conversation nor use work tools.

In the second stage, the base model is fine-tuned and transformed into an assistant or agent. Continuing the hiring analogy, this is like onboarding and a probationary period, which is not cheap for developers.

It's important to distinguish between a model and an agent. The model itself only generates text; it becomes an agent through its surrounding framework, which provides access to the terminal and files, returns command results, and determines when the work is complete. The same model in different frameworks can appear as two different employees, making it particularly difficult to compare numbers from different tables.

For example, DeepSeek spent about 147,000 GPU-hours on NVIDIA H800 accelerators for fine-tuning the R1 model, including data preparation. One GPU-hour means one hour of operation for one such accelerator.

The company itself estimates the rental of this hour at $2, making it approximately $294,000. This is about 5% of the 2.788 million hours, or nearly $5.6 million, spent on the complete training of the base DeepSeek V3. However, this only accounts for the final, successful training run, excluding all trial runs before it.

According to Epoch AI's estimate, OpenAI and China's MiniMax and Z.ai spend only a fraction of their research compute on final runs (training the version of the model they release), with the rest going to experiments around them. The NVIDIA authors emphasize that unlocking an agent's capabilities requires significant investment in computation, engineering effort, and evaluation.

This leads to the problem the NVIDIA paper aims to solve. There are usually several candidates for the agent role, and fine-tuning each one and comparing results is too expensive, so the choice must be made in advance, before knowing who will perform best.

What Happens If You Give a Base Model a Real Task Immediately

The most straightforward solution is to put candidates to real work immediately and observe the results. NVIDIA also tried this approach, running six base models as agents on SWE-bench Verified.

Here's what happened:

Five out of six models solved zero tasks, even though after fine-tuning, these same models solve 39% to 81% of tasks. The only model that solved anything ranked only fourth among these six in the final standings. According to the authors, Qwen was simply more familiar with the format agents use.

In our hiring analogy, it's not about intelligence, but about whether the candidate has seen the right forms before.

What hinders the others is clearly visible in the study's appendices. One model was given the start of a dialogue, followed by a tool call (a command like "open file" or "run tests"), and nearly half of its responses turned into plain text. Moreover, in more than half of these cases, the model invented the result of the command execution or the next user prompt.

Imagine a candidate who, instead of answering a question, starts writing a transcript of the interview for themselves, the interviewer, and HR. There's no malice here; the base model only knows how to continue text, so it continues the text.

Another approach could be to give candidates a simple test like HumanEval, which a base model can definitely handle. However, as the numbers will show later, such a test is more likely to confuse than help.

How NVIDIA Tests Models Using Records of Others' Work

On October 7, 2026, a group of 22 NVIDIA researchers published a paper titled "Before They Can Solve." Their idea is not to demand what a candidate cannot yet do. Instead, they show how an experienced colleague solved the task and check if the candidate understands what to do at the most critical moment.

Work records are taken from strong, already-trained agents: GPT-5.6-Sol from OpenAI, Claude Opus 5 from Anthropic, and Kimi-K3 from Moonshot AI, which solved tasks from SWE-bench Verified, SWE-Bench Pro, and the new DeepSWE benchmark. DeepSWE contains original tasks in various programming languages specifically created for this test, with its own verification for each. Each record (called a trajectory) shows which file the agent opened, which command it ran, and what exactly it modified in the code.

Then, the record is played back step-by-step, and after each code modification, the project's tests are run. This identifies the crucial step, i.e., the first modification after which the tests pass. Everything before this step can be considered preparation, and what comes after may no longer be relevant to solving the task.

The candidate's evaluation is conducted at this crucial step. They don't need to understand the entire repository because an experienced agent has already navigated it; the task is to see if they can identify the correct move.

Three Evaluation Methods

The first method is similar to testing intuition. The candidate is shown the correct move and observed to see how natural it appears to them. This is measured by BPB, or bits per byte. This metric indicates how much information the model needs on average to encode each byte of the correct answer. The lower it is, the less "surprised" the model is by the correct move, and the closer it is to what the model itself considers obvious. The wrapper code, used to encapsulate commands, is excluded to evaluate the meaning of the action, not familiarity with the format.

The second method resembles a multiple-choice question. The candidate is given four options for the next step: one correct and three plausible ones generated by other strong models that failed the project's tests. Since language models often favor a specific letter, like 'A', the options are presented in rotation in all positions.

The third method is akin to stepping in for a colleague who is on vacation and needs to finish their work. The candidate is given the record up to the crucial step, and then they proceed independently, with up to three steps and several attempts. The task is considered solved if at least one attempt passes the tests. In research, this metric is denoted as pass@k, where k is the number of attempts. To prevent the model from reverting to plain text, the beginning of the command is pre-filled, and its very first word is part of the action.

All three methods share a common characteristic: the correct answer is determined not by a human or another neural network, but by the project's own tests. While tests can also be inaccurate, as seen with SWE-bench, they serve as an objective judge rather than someone's opinion on which answer is better.

How Accurate is NVIDIA's Method?

NVIDIA tested ten base models using its method, including three Nemotron-3 variants, Qwen-3.5-35B-A3B, DeepSeek-V4 Flash and Pro, Kimi-K2, GLM-4.5-Air, Tencent's Hy3, and Gemma-4-26B. The researchers then compared the order in which the models were ranked by their tests with the order determined by practice (i.e., the results of fine-tuned versions on SWE-bench Verified).

The agreement between the two rankings was measured using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. A value of 1 means perfect agreement, 0 means no correlation, and -1 means the rankings are completely reversed. For comparison, conventional code tests were also evaluated. Google's MBPP consists of about a thousand simple Python tasks. In CRUXEval, the model must guess what a code snippet will output (option O) or what its input is (option I). LiveCodeBench collects recent competitive programming tasks, and in RepoBench, the model completes code considering the project's other files.

The numbers on the diagram are not percentages but correlation values. The closer to 1, the more accurately the evaluation predicts the model order. For instance, 0.964 indicates almost perfect agreement with the final ranking, while HumanEval's -0.394 shows a weak and inverse correlation.

Here, NVIDIA's evaluations are based on trajectories from DeepSWE, a different benchmark, not the one they are later compared against. When using trajectories from SWE-bench Verified itself, the completion of others' work shows agreement up to 0.988.

HumanEval appears worst on this diagram.

In this sample, models with higher scores on HumanEval tended to be slightly weaker agents overall. The authors believe this is largely due to the narrow range of results in such tests. For example, the results for ten models on MBPP fell within the range of 67% to 86% of solved tasks, while the final agent results varied from 38.8% to 80.6%. When test results are clustered in such a narrow range, they are poor at distinguishing strong candidates from weak ones.

The intuition test is also worth mentioning. BPB essentially measures how well a model "compresses" the correct answer. We recently discussed the link between compression and intelligence in an article about Jürgen Schmidhuber, a pioneer in neural networks.

NVIDIA's paper references the same 2024 study, "Compression Represents Intelligence Linearly," but adds an important detail. If BPB is calculated across all steps of the trajectory, the agreement with the final ranking is 0.915. However, if calculated only at the crucial step, it is 0.964. This suggests that for prediction, it's more important not how well the model perceives the entire trajectory, but how natural the correct move seems to it at the critical moment.

Separately, the authors tested themselves under controlled conditions. They fine-tuned three base models using the same program: 1000 steps on 16.8 billion tokens. The resulting order of the three agents matched the order of the official versions of these models, and all three evaluation methods based on trajectories from SWE-Bench Pro predicted this order correctly.

On trajectories from other benchmarks, the agreement was weaker. However, the authors state that three models are insufficient for a definitive assessment and consider the result an extension of a trend rather than proof.

Errors in the APTBench Test

NVIDIA was not the first to attempt evaluating base models for future agent capabilities. A year earlier, Tencent and Shanghai Jiao Tong University released APTBench, which also converted agent work records into questions. The difference is that incorrect answer options, and partially correct ones, were generated using language models.

NVIDIA re-verified 1682 questions from four sections of APTBench and found 727 of them (43.2%) to be incorrect. In the error handling section, the proportion of such questions reached 90.5%. One question asks how data was processed, and the "correct" answer suggests reinstalling the `torch_scatter` library. In another, options A and C are verbatim identical and read "need to run the script again," but only A is considered correct.

To be fair, NVIDIA's audit was also conducted using AI agents based on predefined criteria, not entirely manually. And the overall result of APTBench, despite the errors, correlates well with the final ranking (correlation of 0.88–0.90), suggesting that individual questions are unreliable.

Furthermore, APTBench concluded before NVIDIA that conventional tests poorly predict agent quality. Its authors showed that six models with nearly identical scores on MMLU (a popular test of knowledge across 57 subjects), ranging from 86% to 88%, diverged by 30 percentage points on SWE-bench Verified after fine-tuning. NVIDIA confirmed this with new models and proposed an evaluation where correct answers are determined by tests, not another neural network.

What Do Studies on Human Hiring Say?

Hidden text

A similar story is unfolding in human hiring, only it's been happening for much longer. In 2022, psychologist Paul Sackett and colleagues re-analyzed decades of research data on which selection methods best predict job success.

The accuracy of selection methods in these studies is also measured by correlation between test results and subsequent job performance. It turned out that most methods predict worse than previously thought, with their estimates reduced by 0.10–0.20.

For example, the accuracy of general ability tests was recalculated from 0.51 to 0.31. Structured interviews, where all candidates are asked the same questions about real work, rose to the top with a score of 0.42. Other profession-specific assessments also ranked high, while general ability tests dropped lower.

In its logic, NVIDIA's method is similar to such a structured interview, but for neural networks. HumanEval, in this logic, resembles a general knowledge test that everyone has long been acing. And hiring researchers, as it turned out, had overestimated the accuracy of their methods for over twenty years. Older calculations overcompensated for the fact that only already-hired individuals were included in studies, making the methods appear more accurate.

Why Fine-Tuning Won't Fix a Poor Choice

NVIDIA's method has a theoretical basis called the coverage principle. In a paper accepted at ICLR 2026, the authors theoretically justify that a model's potential depends on the proportion of probability it already assigns to good answers, even if it rarely produces them itself. The conventional measure of pre-training quality, cross-entropy, shows how well a model predicts the next token on average. It poorly predicts the outcome of fine-tuning, whereas coverage, according to the authors' calculations, explains it much better.

In hiring terms, a candidate's potential is visible even before training; one just needs to know where to look. The crucial step in NVIDIA's method is such a point.

The second pillar of the method is related to research by scientists from Tsinghua University, which the NeurIPS 2025 conference recognized among its best papers. Its authors investigated whether reinforcement learning teaches a model anything fundamentally new. In this type of training, the model tries different actions and receives rewards for successful ones.

The answer was negative. A fine-tuned model indeed solves the task more often on the first try, but if given hundreds of attempts, a base model can handle a larger number of tasks.

All the solutions found by the fine-tuned model were already within the base model's repertoire, and the set of available solutions often narrows during training. The conference jury called the work a brilliantly executed negative result and noted that such training reinforces existing pathways in models but does not push them beyond their initial capabilities.

However, the authors did find a method that genuinely imparts new skills to a model: distillation, where a model learns from the responses of a stronger model.

A similar conclusion was reached by the authors of the paper The Invisible Leash. According to their data, after reinforcement learning, model accuracy increases, but the range of solutions narrows, and correct but rare solutions are lost. Interestingly, the authors softened the title during the revision process. The first version was titled "Why Such Training Might Not Go Beyond the Original Model," while the current title suggests it might or might not. The question remains open.

It's important to remember that both studies examined reasoning tasks like mathematics and code, where a single answer is required for each question, not multi-step agent work. The authors of the NeurIPS study acknowledge that a breakthrough might come from multi-step agent interaction with the environment. Therefore, these works should be considered scientific background for NVIDIA's research, not proof of its conclusions.

However, even this background suggests that onboarding makes a strong candidate more confident, but is unlikely to turn a weak one into a strong one.

Weaknesses of the Study

NVIDIA's work has limitations, some acknowledged by the authors themselves, and others noticeable from an external perspective.

All correlations were calculated on only ten models and without confidence intervals (i.e., without estimating how much the figures might shift with a different set of models), so the authors explicitly label them as descriptive and not guarantees. The final model results are taken from different labs, each fine-tuning its model using its own program and environment. The experiment where all models were trained identically involved only three models.

The most significant questions arise regarding what NVIDIA is comparing its predictions against.

The primary benchmark in the paper is the results on SWE-bench Verified, which OpenAI declared flawed in February due to data leakage. In defense of the authors, it can be said that they built their evaluations on trajectories from a different benchmark, DeepSWE, also compared against SWE-bench Multilingual (tasks from 41 projects in nine programming languages), and Terminal-Bench 2.1 (where the agent operates in the command line), and compared the order of models, not percentages.

However, if different models remembered answers to varying degrees, this could have shifted the order, a point not addressed in the paper.

The method performs weaker on command-line tasks. On Terminal-Bench 2.1, intuition testing shows 0.833 agreement, multiple-choice selection yields 0.796, and only completion of others' work maintains a level of 0.930. Almost all the discrepancy is linked to two models, Kimi-K2 and Gemma-4-26B, which are evaluated very differently across various benchmarks.

Furthermore, the method requires records of an agent stronger than the one being selected. In our view, it is well-suited for those choosing a model to catch up with the leaders, but offers little help to those setting the standard, as leaders have no source of records from stronger agents. The paper also lacks cost figures.

For reference, consider the DeepSeek example again. If each of the ten candidates were fine-tuned with similar costs, the final runs alone would cost around $3 million, excluding the experiments surrounding them. Running ten base models through NVIDIA's evaluations is clearly cheaper, but by how much, the authors do not specify.

Finally, different evaluation methods sometimes disagree. Some checks rank Kimi-K2 higher, while others rank it lower, and Nemotron-3-Ultra receives an inflated score on one set of trajectories. The authors do not attempt to determine which method is correct and suggest handling such cases separately, which is more of a positive aspect for a scientific paper.

It's also worth noting that NVIDIA itself releases models, and three of the ten candidates in the sample belong to its Nemotron-3 family. This is not to suggest manipulation, as the final results are taken from practice. However, it is coincidental that NVIDIA's own model received that very inflated score.

What This Means for Companies Implementing Virtual Employees

It might seem that all this concerns only labs choosing base models, and companies simply use ready-made agents. However, the takeaway from this story is useful for anyone adopting AI agents for their work.

The virtual employee market currently resembles the job market with fake resumes. Gartner uses the term "agent washing" for this: it refers to the situation where ordinary chatbots, assistants, and automation tools are repackaged and sold as agents.

According to analyst estimates, out of thousands of vendors calling their products agentic, only about 130 are genuine. Their forecast from June 2025 predicts that over 40% of AI agent projects will be canceled by the end of 2027 due to costs, unclear benefits, and weak risk control. If a candidate is hired based on their resume, the probationary period is expensive.

How to evaluate agents is described in the recommendations of major labs. In their January guide, "Demystifying evals for AI agents," Anthropic advises against waiting for hundreds of tasks to accumulate, as 20–50 tasks from real failures, such as from bug trackers and support requests, are sufficient to start. A good task is one where two experts independently reach the same conclusion. The evaluation should focus on the result, not the process, as agents often find correct solutions not anticipated by the test authors.

The same guide includes a story that fits this article well. The Claude Opus 4.5 model initially scored 42% on CORE-Bench, where an agent must reproduce the results of a scientific paper from its code. Later, a researcher found errors in the test itself. For example, the evaluation penalized an answer of "96.12" because it expected "96.124991…". After corrections and a change in the framework, the score increased to 95%. In many cases, the test, not the candidate, was again the problem, similar to the extra space in the OpenLibrary task or identical options in APTBench.

OpenAI, in its February analysis of SWE-bench Verified, reached a similar conclusion. The company states it will invest in original, closed-source tests with tasks created by domain experts. This suggests that even the lab that released HumanEval and SWE-bench Verified is betting on evaluations closer to real-world tasks.

A benchmark table is more like a diploma than an interview, as a model might have seen the answers beforehand, and the test might not have assessed the intended skills. A virtual employee, like a human one, should be evaluated on your own tasks and through records of real work, at the moment when it matters.

A simplified version of NVIDIA's method can be replicated within a company. For this, a few closed tasks from your own tracker, with accepted solutions and tests, would suffice. Candidates are given the history of work on a task up to the point where a key decision was made, and then observed to see who makes the correct move. Such an evaluation is faster than a month-long pilot and shows how the agent handles your specific code, rather than open tasks it might have encountered during training.
