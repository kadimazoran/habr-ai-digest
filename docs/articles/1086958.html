❯ How Spaghetti Showcased the Progress of AI Video

In 2023, a video of a generated Will Smith eating spaghetti went viral on Reddit.
It looked funny and a little creepy. His face changed shape, his hands got tangled, and everything seemed unnatural. The video became a meme. Later, people started creating the same scene using new generators and comparing the results. Thus, a simple meme became an unofficial benchmark for the progress of AI video.

Over time, it became harder to laugh at new versions. By 2025, the scene looked more convincing, although some minor details still gave away the generation. But in a 2026 video, it became harder at first glance to tell if you were looking at generated content or real footage. If you didn't know beforehand that the video was made by a neural network, you might not even notice.

Not long ago, such videos were a fun toy: viewers watched to see what the neural network would mess up next. Now, generative video is used not only for short clips in feeds but also in full-scale content production. An author can quickly test an idea, rework a flawed scene, and move on to the next version. Along with this, the amount of soulless AI slop is also growing. But the main change wasn't in the quality of individual clips. The neural network ceased to be a joke and became part of the regular workflow.

❯ The Share of AI Content in Feeds

Slop is Already on Your Phone

You open your feed, watch a couple of videos, play some music in the background. At what point does content made by a neural network appear among them? Sometimes it's obvious from the first second. Sometimes, only after watching do you notice that the voice, the picture, or the "artist" themselves are not real. But not every use of AI makes the work slop. It's one thing to clean up audio or correct a frame, and another to release dozens of almost identical videos for views.

I don't have an exact figure for the "entire internet," so I'll analyze studies that measure different platforms, languages, and types of materials. Available measurements show a general trend. Generation increases the volume of available content, and it appears in recommendations noticeably more often than expected.

What's Happening on the Global Internet

Pew Research Center studied English-language pages from the Common Crawl web archive. In a random sample of 10,000 pages from July 2026, significant signs of AI authorship or editing were found in 10%. If we consider only pages published after ChatGPT's release, this figure rose to over a third.

Another study, Graphite, looked not at all pages, but at English-language articles. In the first quarter of 2026, researchers classified 49.9% of the materials in their sample as predominantly AI-written. This does not contradict Pew's data. The samples, definitions, and methodologies differ. None of these figures measure the proportion of slop, the proportion of the entire internet, or the proportion of what people actually read.

What About Us?

I couldn't find any open studies with transparent methodology that would assess the proportion of AI texts on all Russian-language websites or the proportion of AI videos in all feeds on VK, Telegram, Rutube, and other platforms.

However, there are studies on how often the Russian audience encounters such content and whether they notice it. According to OMI, published in March 2026, 42% of Russians encounter AI content daily, and 44% weekly.

Most often, people notice it in short videos – 67% of respondents named this format. Next are images – 66%, and music was indicated by 34%.

A study by LAMPA and Rambler&Co from late 2025 also confirms that AI has become a part of everyday media consumption. According to its data, 82% of respondents noticed an increase in AI content over the past year, and 65% encounter it daily. Participants most often mentioned images, videos, and texts. These results highlight the trend's visibility to the audience but do not allow for calculating the percentage of generated publications on the Russian internet.

The fact that the problem has become public is also indicated by an analysis by MTS Link in collaboration with "Medialogia." In Russian-language social media from January to August 15, 2026, the term "neuroslop" was mentioned almost 20 times more often than the previous year. The total number of mentions of AI terms increased from 15.3 to 23.9 million, or by 56%. These figures measure conversations about the phenomenon, not the volume of slop itself, but they show that users are increasingly noticing and naming it.

What the Feed Shows

The scale of the problem is already palpable in short videos. In May 2026, Kapwing created a new TikTok account and manually checked the first 500 recommendations. Researchers classified 294 videos, or 59%, as AI slop.

In a separate check of a new YouTube account, the authors of the Kapwing study classified 104 out of the first 500 Shorts as AI slop, about 21%. This measurement was conducted earlier, in October 2025. It's worth noting that the results reflect the statistics of new accounts. Another person's personalized feed might look different.

Music: Charts, Releases, and Streams

Isn't it striking that the vast majority of songs on the charts are AI tracks?
According to a preliminary estimate by "Yandex Music" as of September 2026, AI was used in approximately 27% of new releases, but such tracks constituted about 4.4% of the entire catalog.

This calculation includes both fully generated songs and recordings where a neural network assisted at a specific stage. The service reported that most of these new releases do not gain a significant number of streams and added track labeling and an "Less AI" setting in recommendations.

Despite this, many tracks break through and capture the minds of many listeners who may not even suspect that the song was written by a "soulless machine."

For comparison, Deezer's data is useful, although it's not entirely accurate to transfer it to Russian services. On peak days in June 2026, fully AI-generated tracks accounted for over half of new uploads on Deezer. That's about 90,000 per day. However, they accounted for only 1-3% of streams. Deezer excludes detected AI tracks from algorithmic recommendations, so this difference reflects, in part, the service's own policy. In a Deezer and Ipsos survey, 80% of participants favored clear labeling of fully generated music. People might listen to such a song, but many want to know that a real person made it.

And according to "KION Music" data provided to RTVI, about 70% of compositions in its sample were created using AI. The analysis covered releases from June 1 to August 26.

Neural networks are already competing with human works for viewer and listener attention. At the current pace of technological development, the share of generated content is likely to grow. But how the feed will change depends not only on AI capabilities but also on audience reaction. Viewer preferences and platform rules influence changes in the feed. Therefore, it's too early to talk about an inevitable "complete replacement" of human content. There are several scenarios, and it's unclear which one will be realized.

❯ Why We Watch to the End

You can recognize that a video was made by a neural network, laugh at it, and still watch it to the end. This is not a contradiction. We don't always look for something outstanding in our feed. Sometimes you want to know how a strange story ends. Sometimes you want to send it to a friend. And sometimes a song just gets stuck in your head, even if you find it absurd.

Lately, my friends and I have been encountering Chinese AI series on TikTok.

Despite the absurdity, it's hard to tear yourself away from these series. Each episode ends on a cliffhanger, and familiar plots about revenge, wealth, and love make you keep watching. Even the neural network's mistakes work in favor of engagement: strange movements and sudden plot twists turn viewing into a separate form of entertainment.

According to Chinese regulators, about 128,000 micro-dramas were released in the first three months of 2026, and approximately 95% of them contained AI generation. On the Douyin platform alone, the Chinese version of TikTok, about 50,000 new AI series appeared in March. That's about one new project every 90 seconds. In the first half of the year, 220,000 AI series on Douyin accumulated a total of 500 billion views. The scale is hard to imagine, but in the feed, it feels like an endless stream of stories that keep you hooked until the very end.

With the song "Don't Be Afraid of the Night", it was even more interesting. The track by artist Enzro was released on April 15, 2026, but only gained significant popularity in the second half of the summer. The author mentioned using AI in its creation, as reported by the publication "T-Zh."

Some users called the song tasteless and created ironic videos about "ordinary life" with mortgages, marketplace shopping, and driving Chinese cars to it. Others simply listened to it because they liked the song.

As a result, one song had two groups of listeners: the first perceived it as a meme, while the second listened to it quite seriously.

Even I initially dismissed this track as another piece of nonsense and didn't pay attention to it. But within a week, I started humming the chorus at every opportunity.

Watching a video to the end doesn't always mean you liked it. The viewer might be curious to see the resolution, wait for the next absurd twist, or understand why the video is being discussed. A study by "Anketolog" cited humor, absurdity, beautiful visuals, an unexpected ending, and curiosity as reasons for watching AI videos to completion.

The same principle applies to short series. An episode cuts off before an important event, and the viewer starts the next one. The neural network merely speeds up production; familiar plot schemes, curiosity, and the desire to share the video are what keep attention. Therefore, one person watches such content seriously, another ironically, and a third simply wants to know how it ends.

❯ Why Content Is Increasing

Previously, a long chain existed between an idea and a finished video: script, actors, filming, lighting, editing, voice-over, and publication. AI doesn't eliminate all this work, but in some projects, it allows a smaller team to complete some tasks. A small team can now quickly assemble a video that previously would have required a full film crew.

For businesses, it's particularly important that not only successful publications but also failed attempts have become cheaper. If the first option didn't work, you can quickly change the protagonist, ending, style, or language and release a new one. Previously, such experiments were often too expensive. Now, you can test several ideas and keep the one that best holds viewers' attention.

After publication, recommendations come into play.
TikTok considers views, watch completion rates, skips, likes, comments, and shares. YouTube looks at viewing history, search queries, reactions, and viewer feedback. The platform doesn't necessarily need to determine if a video was made by a neural network. If a video holds attention, it starts being shown to people with similar interests.

This creates a feedback loop: the author releases several versions faster, viewers react, the platform finds a suitable audience, and the creator uses this data for the next attempt. AI content is increasing not because algorithms specifically promote neural networks. It's cheaper to produce, easier to test, and faster to scale. Even if a video evokes laughter or irritation rather than admiration from the viewer, it can still get views and lead to the creation of more similar videos.

❯ What's Good About It, and What Raises Concerns

For the viewer, there are more unusual stories, songs, and visual experiments. Among the large volume of mediocre clips, successful ideas sometimes emerge that might not have appeared otherwise. But the same flow creates a problem. There's too much similar content, and along with it, the number of copies, empty shells, and videos made solely for clicks is growing.

Information Security.
As long as the viewer understands that they are looking at obviously generated content, it's mainly a matter of taste. But realistic video can be mistaken for footage of a real event, and the image or voice of a real person can be used without permission.

Therefore, it's worth distinguishing three things: how the material was created, how good it is, and how it is distributed. AI doesn't automatically make work high-quality or poor. It only changes the production method, and the result itself and the content in which it is shown should still be evaluated.

❯ Conclusions

AI content is increasing because it is cheaper and faster to release. Authors can test more ideas, and platforms quickly find an audience for videos that hold attention. Therefore, feeds feature not only successful stories, songs, and visual experiments but also a large number of copies, empty shells, and materials created for clicks.

The viewer is not obligated to like neural networks or consider such content high-quality. They might watch a video to the end for a joke, curiosity, an absurd plot, or the desire to know what happens next. One person follows a series seriously, another watches it ironically, and a third sends it to friends as a meme.

This accessible production also has a positive side. A small team or an individual author can test an idea in days that would have taken weeks before. But along with this, the flow of similar materials grows, and realistic video becomes easier to mistake for real footage. Separate issues also arise around copyright, the use of voice and likeness, and the reduction of orders for specialists.

Therefore, AI itself does not make material either good or bad. It's necessary to separately evaluate what resulted, how it was created, and how the material was presented to the viewer. It is the combination of these factors that determines whether we are looking at a successful work, a harmless experiment, or just another empty shell.
