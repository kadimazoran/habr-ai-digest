1. Grandmother and the Mark of the Beast

I'll start with a personal anecdote. My grandmother, now a very elderly woman, worked as a computer operator at the computing center (a data center for zoomers) of the Gorky Railway. That is, she was an educated person. This did not stop her from telling me, a five-year-old, that in the near future the state would chip us all, and then it would be the end for all of us.

Later, in my youth, for fun, I replayed this thought experiment in my head and came to roughly the same conclusion: well, yes, it's convenient for the state machine, the citizen's ID number is not in a paper booklet, but is immediately "attached" to the citizen along with an online wallet, downloaded movies, and social media authorization. But they can't force everyone to get such a chip, can they? A very large circle of dissatisfied people can be found. Well, as a last resort, with total mobilization and a state law, you can always go into hiding in the jungles of South America again.

Any coercion leads to leakage.

Now it seems to me, my grandmother was afraid of the wrong thing. It's not scary that everyone will be chipped. It's scary if not everyone is chipped.

For example, me: I have a corporate subscription to Claude for $200 to solve work tasks, a home subscription for $200 to solve home tasks, and a free ChatGPT for quick voice translations during live communication (at the time of writing, Claude's mobile app was not very good at speech recognition). I have delegated a fairly large part of my routine and work tasks to the models, they cope well (sometimes!), and overall I am satisfied. Plus, I'm the kind of person who likes that I can ask a silly question about anything at any moment and get an answer immediately. I'm a nerd.

It's not even about the chip. The chip is just the fastest way to get AI into your head. The question is different: who will have access, and who won't.

And here we observe the rise in token prices for flagship models over the past year, and I increasingly ask myself: okay, I earn well and can withstand this increase for a while longer, although the number of tasks for generative models from my side is increasing. But there are those who earn less. In the future, they may not be able to afford access to such a volume of tokens, and with it, the privileges that stem from it. And how will this change the game?

I decided to think more deeply about this issue over the weekend and want to share the results of my reflections with you, based only on what already exists.

2. We Are Already Here

Let's start with what we don't need to invent.

Addiction is already happening, and at a loss for now

OpenAI is operating at a loss. According to leaks from autumn 2025, which were retold by the business press, the company expects a loss of about $14 billion in 2026 and about $115 billion in total by 2029. Anthropic's situation is better: analysts expect it to become operationally profitable for the first time in 2026. ChatGPT has over 900 million free users, according to the same analysts, and each costs the company about 70 cents per month.

But there's a nuance: a paid request in the company's chat is actually profitable. Gross margin (revenue minus the cost of model operation), according to forecasts from leaks, for OpenAI in 2025 is about 33%, and for Anthropic, about 40%. It's hard to compare them directly, though: OpenAI's calculation included free users, while Anthropic's only includes API. So where's the money?
The money is going into the race. In 2025, OpenAI planned to spend about $9.4 billion on training new models, Anthropic about $4.1 billion. Plus another $8.4 billion and $2.7 billion for inference, which is simply for the trained models to respond.

It turns out that the free tier is not charity, but a payment for habit. Today's prices are subsidized by investors, and sooner or later they will want a return.

Prices are falling and rising simultaneously

Old models are getting cheaper at an astonishing rate. According to Epoch AI's calculations as of early 2025, the price for a fixed level of capability is falling by a median of about 50 times per year. The GPT-3.5 level cost $20 per million tokens in November 2022, and in October 2024, the same level cost 7 cents.

But it's different with flagships: in the spring of 2026, new flagships became more expensive than their predecessors for the first time. However, some prices rolled back in the fall. The poor will not be left without AI at all. They will get last year's model almost for free. And in the job market, they will compete with those who have the current year's model.

Access is already turning into a salary

PwC analyzed over a billion job postings in 27 countries: vacancies requiring AI skills promise an average salary 62% higher than the same positions without them. A year ago, this premium was 57%. Such vacancies have increased by 69%, while the market as a whole has grown by only 9%.

It should be noted: these are promises in job postings, not actual payments. According to a Payscale survey in early 2026, more than half of employers did not pay extra for AI skills at all: no premiums, no bonuses. But this is likely worse than better: if the ability to work with AI becomes the norm, then without access to it, a person simply won't be hired.

This means that access to AI is already being converted into a salary and the very possibility of getting a job. And if access is paid and the best models are getting more expensive, then, it seems to me, your salary starts to depend on the tariff.

You might object: they pay for the skill, and it stays with the person even without a subscription. And this is where the most interesting part begins.

A crutch hinders learning

In a field experiment in a Turkish school, nearly a thousand students solved math problems. Those who were given access to GPT-4 saw their practical results significantly improve. When the access was removed, students who had used the neural network performed 17% worse on the exam than those who had never used AI. At the same time, they themselves did not notice that they were learning worse. It is also interesting that the version that provided hints, not ready-made answers, almost nullified this harm. This means the harm depends on how the tool is designed. And the design is chosen not by the user, but by the company.

What has already been learned is retained better. In a NASA study, 16 experienced pilots who had flown on autopilot for years were tested on a Boeing 747 simulator. Their manual control skills remained. But with what was done "in their heads," it was worse: keeping the aircraft's location in mind without a map, diagnosing malfunctions without the help of onboard systems.

The first signal from real practice in medicine (or perhaps not the first). In Poland, experienced endoscopists, after three months of working with an AI assistant, began to find precancerous polyps less often in procedures without AI: the proportion of such colonoscopies dropped from 28.4% to 22.4%. There are criticisms of the study: it is observational, and the patients before and after were different. But here the effect was noticed in a real clinic, not in a laboratory.

And the last item on this list: participants in a survey of 319 knowledge workers admitted themselves that the more they trusted AI in a task, the less critically they approached the result. And only someone who knows how to do it themselves can check it well.

My conclusion: it's not that "AI makes you dumber," but something else. AI does not take away already learned skills, but it hinders the acquisition of new ones and transfers the verification to those who learned earlier. This means that those who learned with AI will no longer be able to cope without it, and they will always have to pay for access. They pay not for the skill, but for the subscription, without which the skill does not work.

3. The Year 2035

Now let's reflect. Everything that follows is based on what already exists or has been shown in laboratories.

First, the earpiece

Before AI appears in the head, it will settle in headphones and glasses that hear the context and provide hints all day long. This is already happening today: AI-powered glasses are already on sale, and the head of Shopify in 2025 wrote to employees that working with AI is now a basic minimum. In a couple of years, the question "Do you use an assistant?" will sound the same as "Do you have a smartphone?"

They have one drawback: you ask the question out loud. The interlocutor sees what you are asking, the employer sees it and draws conclusions about your qualifications. This is inconvenient. It's like reading from a piece of paper. The first solution already exists: a bracelet that Meta sells with its glasses. It reads muscle signals in the hand, and the interface can be controlled with finger movements, without voice.

The chip solves precisely this problem. It doesn't make you smarter or speed up your thinking. It provides a silent question and an invisible answer.

This is not science fiction. In 2025, a Stanford team (Kunz, Willett, et al., Cell) decoded inner speech in real-time, i.e., phrases that a person says to themselves. The accuracy for different participants ranged from 46% to 74%, with a vocabulary of 125,000 words. There were four participants, all of whom could barely speak due to ALS or stroke. And the answer can be returned by voice, which only you can hear: cochlear implants have been transmitting sound directly to the auditory nerve for decades.

No one, not even in a laboratory, can yet record ready-made knowledge or skills directly into the human brain. Therefore, the chip of 2035 does not implant knowledge into the head, but whispers it. In a couple of years, this whisper will cease to be felt as alien.

The same Stanford study has a detail worth remembering. The decoder partially decoded personal inner speech that the person did not intend to utter. To avoid this, the researchers devised a mental password: the device remains dormant until the person thinks of a code phrase. A device that can receive your mental question can, by definition, also hear everything else you say to yourself. The only question is who decides when it's allowed.

Inside forever, outside by subscription

Surgery, it seems, is not getting cheaper as quickly as processors. Therefore, the chip is designed like a cochlear implant: the internal part, electrodes, and antenna, are installed for a long time, while the external processor and software are changed frequently, without surgery. For cochlear implants, it's already like this: the internal part is designed for a lifetime, the external part lasts five to seven years.

The business model here is like that of printers. One printer, endless cartridges. You can change your phone manufacturer in an evening. Changing the manufacturer of electrodes in your head means surgery.

And this has already happened. More than 350 blind people worldwide received the Argus II retinal implant from Second Sight. It provided crude but real vision. In 2019, the company ceased production to focus on a brain implant, and in 2020, it almost stopped functioning without warning patients. Repairs and updates became unavailable. One patient, Ross Doerr, could not get an MRI: the company simply did not respond. Another, Jeroen Perk, broke his processor in November 2020 and looked for spare parts from other patients.

There is a precedent, in general: a company can go bankrupt, and the device in your head remains.

Tariffs

Here I will allow myself to fantasize, and each of the points below already has a prototype:

  • Quota. A limit on requests per month/day/hour, like today's message limit. The remaining amount is shown by the app on your phone. You'll have to plan complex conversations for the beginning of the billing period.

  • Model Tiers. The free tier is slow and a generation behind.

  • Advertising. Currently, OpenAI promises that advertising on the free tier is separate from the response and does not affect it. But by 2035, responses may well contain "priority knowledge" from sponsors. And they will sound with the same internal voice as your own thoughts.

  • Memory. The chip hears everything you've asked and said, and after ten years, it remembers your life better than you do. And it seems to me that access to this memory will depend on the tariffs.

  • Corporate Chip. The employer pays for the professional tier. Everything you do at work is logged and, by contract, belongs to the company.

  • Offline, the chip is silent. This is also possible: hello to jammers at exams.

Labor Market

At a job interview in 2035, two candidates. Both look equally confident. One is on a professional tariff, the second on a free one. You can only distinguish them by pauses: the free one's answers come half a second later, and for rare questions, he answers in general terms. Asking them to turn off the chip is pointless: you can't check if it's off. Therefore, the interview tests not the person, but the tariff.

Young people are rarely hired: their work is done by the model. Experienced people are hired willingly, but paid less and less, because their experience has long been packaged into a domain package that is sold to newcomers.

4. Rented Mind

Now, the main thing. What does all this mean for inequality?

In 2022, many expected AI to level everyone. The paradox is that it does level things. Just not as we hoped.

Layer One: AI Levels People

In a large call center study, an AI assistant increased productivity by an average of 15%, and for novices and weaker employees, by about 30%. The best became slightly faster, but their quality even slightly decreased. In an experiment with professional writing, ChatGPT also helped the weak the most and compressed the spread between people.

But where did this growth come from? The call center assistant prompted novices with what the best operators already knew from experience. The experience of the experienced was packaged and distributed to the novices. The experienced person lost value, the novice grew, but grew on someone else's experience: the knowledge is not theirs, it is rented. And the profit went to a third party: the one who owns the model.

And here's the main point: AI levels people and concentrates value in the hands of those who rent out their minds. Inequality does not disappear, it changes its address. Before, you were richer because you were smarter and more experienced. Now you are richer if you own the model or can afford its best tariff.

A recent theoretical paper (still a preprint, without peer review) explains how this works: AI reduces the skill gap between people, and value goes to those who have concentrated assets. The authors do not dare to say how it will end: it depends on whether AI remains someone's property or becomes a common commodity. Economists Acemoglu and Johnson, in their book "Power and Progress," come to a similar conclusion based on historical material: the gains from new technology are captured by a narrow elite if society does not build counterweights.

Layer Two: The Access Gap

Leveling works only between people with equal access. And access is unequal: a frontier model versus last year's. Whoever pays more is more competitive. The value here is relative: a free model from 2035 will be smarter than today's best, but in the job market, it's worth little if your neighbor has a flagship model. It's like with education: everyone has free schooling, but the graduate of an elite school gets the job.

Added to this is a barrier that is not getting cheaper along with tokens: hardware and surgery. A subscription can be changed in a minute, an implant only through new surgery. Those who could afford a cheap implant in 2035, with fewer electrodes and from a simpler manufacturer, will live with it for many years, even when tariffs become cheaper.

Layer Three: No Entry for the Young

For workers aged 22-25 in professions most affected by AI (developers, customer support), employment has relatively decreased, while for experienced workers it has remained stable. The decline is concentrated where AI replaces people, not helps them. The authors, however, qualify: this is a working version, and they do not prove causality.

If juniors are not hired, where will seniors come from in ten years? In our world, the answer will be harsh: only someone who knows how to do it themselves can verify the AI's answer. The generation that grew up with a chip does not know how to verify the chip. If we take Microsoft's survey to its logical conclusion, then those who learned before the chip become the last ones who can spot an error. Therefore, it turns out that this is not my problem, but the problem of my children.

Advertising in the form of thoughts

The most unpleasant consequence, in my opinion, is not surveillance, but advertising. The voice that sounds inside and that no one else hears, in a couple of years, is indistinguishable from your own. If, on the free tier, this voice makes recommendations, you won't know if it's your preference or a paid one. Here, advertising ceases to be a banner and becomes intuition.

No Reverse Gear

It is possible to refuse the chip. Just as it is possible to refuse a smartphone today: technically possible, socially almost impossible. The poor need the chip to work, the rich to relax. It seems to me that closed religious communities and people for whom surgery is contraindicated will refuse. For the latter, this is a new type of disability, created not by the body, but by society.

And even if you refuse, what you have learned over the years with the chip will remain, but what you have not learned while it was prompting will not appear. Reverse gear exists. There is simply nowhere to go in it.

5. Why I Might Be Wrong

The strongest objections I've heard.

Open Models. Models with open weights like DeepSeek and Qwen can be run cheaply through hosting or on your own hardware if you have it, and they lag behind the best by very little. This means there will be no monopoly on intelligence. This is a serious argument. But for a chip, the decisive factor is not the model, but the hardware and surgery: electrodes in the head are implanted not by an enthusiast, but by a certified clinic, and the external unit is sold by the manufacturer. Open models can become the basis of a shadow market, like phone jailbreaking today. But the mass user, as today, will choose convenience, not freedom.

Cost Reduction. Prices are falling tens of times a year, which means in ten years access will be available to everyone. Yes, everyone will have yesterday's AI. But inequality is relative: it's not absolute power that matters, but the difference with your neighbor. As long as the frontier costs more, the gap remains.

Regulation. Governments are already reacting. Chile incorporated neuro-data protection into its constitution in 2021, Colorado passed a neuro-data law in 2024. The European Union forced manufacturers to switch to a single connector, and may force a common standard for implants as well. This is true, and it's good, but history shows that regulation follows, not precedes. Privacy laws appear after a leak, not before it.

This is just pessimism. Perhaps. But I am not claiming that AI is bad. I am an active user of this technology. I claim that it appeared as a commercial product and will develop according to market laws. This is not a sentence, but a description of how technologies usually work if no one specifically decides otherwise.

6. Grandmother Was Partially Right

Grandmother feared a mark that would be placed by force. In my childhood, I prepared an escape plan to the jungle. We both made the same mistake: we thought the danger would come as coercion.

No one will come to chip us. There will be no decree, no law, no queue under escort, no mark on the forehead. There will be convenience, then habit, then competition, then it will turn out that without a chip, you simply work worse.

In the jungles of South America, by the way, the chip will not work: there is no network there (unless you bring Starlink with you). Only leaving there will be not from the state, but from your own head.
