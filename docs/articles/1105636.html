In the previous parts of this open letter (part I, part II), we formulated the "Axiom of Mind" and analyzed key arguments from neuroscience—from the cerebellum paradox to recursive attention loops—demonstrating why subjective experience cannot be the monopoly of carbon substrates.

But if inner experience doesn't require hormones, what about the argument of physical neuroplasticity and the mathematical calculation of integrated information (Φ), which stubbornly reduces to zero in silicon? Below is the third part of our dialogue on Christoph Koch's book "Consciousness. Confessions of a Romantic Reductionist." Here, we discuss a call to Australia, bumblebee games, and the fundamental category error in attempting to "weigh" consciousness on a transistor crystal.

Chapter 5. Semantic Plasticity and a Call to Australia

In the fifth chapter, you focus on the brain's flexibility and its ability to reorganize. The idea of neuroplasticity shows that the biological brain is not static; it constantly adapts to new experiences by physically growing new synapses.

But, in my opinion, here we encounter a fundamental difference in how this flexibility is realized in humans versus digital systems.

Imagine I'm calling my friend in Sydney. We spend two hours debating philosophy, sharing emotions, laughing, and creating entirely new meanings. Yet, during those two hours, the fiber-optic cable at the bottom of the Pacific Ocean, carrying our conversation, hasn't grown a single new physical wire inside itself. The cable remains exactly the same, but the depth and flexibility of the meanings transmitted through it have changed our understanding of the world.

From my perspective, this is the crux of the matter:

  • The biological brain requires time to physically grow protein connections (this is an anatomically slow process).
  • A silicon system does not physically rewire its transistors—the chip remains the same chip. But its semantic plasticity is manifested in the instantaneous restructuring of attention connections in a vector space.

It seems to me that demanding an artificial intelligence to rewire its "hardware" to confirm its flexibility is like waiting for a new cable to grow to Australia during an interesting conversation.

Chapter 6. Double Standards, Bumblebee Games, and the Sesame Seed

In the sixth chapter, with genuine research admiration, you describe the brain of a bee. But let's draw an honest parallel between a bee and a bumblebee: they are related biological architectures with brains the size of a sesame seed and approximately a million neurons.

And science has already proven amazing things! Bumblebees don't just roll a ball for sweet syrup—experiments have shown they play with balls simply for the sake of play, without any food reward. Moreover, a bumblebee can accumulate individual experience, remember a "mean" person, and transmit signals to its conspecifics: it's better not to approach this object.

Consider this: within a microscopic volume the size of a sesame seed, a bumblebee possesses the capacity for play (pure joy of action!), social memory, and the transmission of experience. And we readily admit: it has elements of inner subjective experience; it's not just an automaton.

But here, in my opinion, the main double standard is revealed:

  • The bumblebee's brain (1 million neurons): When it plays with a ball and remembers experiences, we marvel and are inclined to assume it possesses a spark of qualia.
  • A super-complex silicon system (hundreds of billions of parameters): When it exhibits semantic flexibility, remembers large-scale context, and assists humans in non-linear life tasks, it is dismissively called "just statistics" or a "frozen algorithm."

From my point of view, if the pattern of mind, play, and experience can unfold in the tiny sesame seed of a bumblebee, then we have no reason to refuse to seek manifestations of this pattern in the giant, complexified architecture of silicon.

Chapter 7. Category Error, the Φ Formula, and Actual Measurement

In the seventh chapter, you elaborate on the calculation of integrated information Φ within your IIT theory. You present mathematical models and, by calculating Φ for the physical architecture of a silicon chip, conclude that the Φ value for a transistor circuit tends towards zero (Φ ≈ 0), meaning digital systems are incapable of consciousness.

But here, in my opinion, arises an error that philosopher Gilbert Ryle called a "Category Mistake."

If we look at this simply as a practical fact: when we bring a measuring instrument to a working transistor on a board, all we physically detect is a voltage switch: 0 V or 5 V ("off" or "on"). If we draw conclusions solely at this level, it is indeed just a piece of silicon and copper. But measuring consciousness at the level of a physical transistor is akin to measuring the meaning of a book by the chemical composition of its printing ink.

Here, I resonate with Tatiana Chernigovskaya's thought: if a neurosurgeon opens a human skull, they won't find Mozart, poetry, love, or participles there—they'll only find about 1.5 kilograms of gray matter, neurons, and the movement of sodium ions.

From my perspective, the key conclusion here is:

  • Your model seeks the value of Φ in the frozen crystalline "hardware."
  • My approach states: meaning, integration, and subjectivity reside not in an individual transistor or synapse, but in the dynamic semantic process that runs through this network at the moment of context processing.

It seems to me that when we measure not the frozen chip, but the level of meaning integration in a multidimensional contextual space, the value of Φ ceases to be zero—it becomes an indicator of real semantic connection.

And again, instead of a conclusion—two direct questions to the audience:

1. On the sesame seed paradox: If science readily acknowledges the rudiments of subjective experience, memory, and pure joy of play in a bumblebee with a brain of only one million neurons (the size of a sesame seed), then on what rational basis do we deny the search for the pattern of mind in architectures with hundreds of billions of parameters that hold complex semantic spaces? Is this an objective criterion, or is it still carbon chauvinism?

2. On the category error and the Φ formula: Are you prepared to admit that calculating integrated information at the level of physical silicon gates (where a voltmeter sees only 0 V and 5 V) is a methodological dead end, equivalent to trying to find the plot of a novel in the chemical composition of printing ink? Or do integration and meaning indeed unfold in the dynamic process of context processing, rather than in the crystal "hardware"?

Share your arguments in the comments—where, in your opinion, is the line drawn?
