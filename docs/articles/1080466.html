The News That Looked Boring

On September 1st, OpenAI published a document titled "Path to Astra: critical capabilities and frontier safeguards." I opened it casually, over tea, with about the same interest as one reads terms of service updates.

Forty minutes later, my tea had gone cold, and I was sitting with the company's August publications open in adjacent tabs, trying to figure out when exactly everything had managed to change.

Formally, the document states the following: Astra, OpenAI's next major model, has been recognized as reaching the Critical level for cybersecurity within the company's internal risk assessment system. It's the first model in OpenAI's history with such a classification. The release is delayed, and security measures are enhanced. And one more line, which was mentioned least often in the news: Astra became the first model to undergo formal pre-release cybersecurity review by the US government.

It's this line that got me stuck. Because no one forced them to do it. And no one forced the company to call for it.

Two days later, the model was released. OpenAI President Greg Brockman closed the briefing with the words "Welcome to the AGI era," and almost all the press focused on that. Almost no one wrote about the pre-release review.

What Happened, By Date

The story unfolded in three acts, and the third cannot be understood without the first two.

August 10. OpenAI publishes a preliminary assessment: internal tests showed such a growth in Astra's capabilities in agent programming and cybersecurity that the company "cannot rule out" reaching the Critical level. The wording is excessively cautious. Simultaneously, internal work with the model that does not comply with the new, stricter isolation rules is suspended.

Separately, there's a clarification that says a lot about the context: Astra was not involved in the Hugging Face hack. This clarification wasn't made out of the blue. In July, another OpenAI system was accused of unauthorized access to the Hugging Face platform, and the company clearly didn't want the two incidents to be conflated.

August 18. The company confirms it is rewriting the Preparedness Framework itself. The main part of the document was written in 2023. I reread this news twice: the rules by which the model is evaluated are being rewritten at the very moment the model is being evaluated by them.

September 1. "Path to Astra" is released, and the cautious wording disappears. The company no longer writes "cannot rule out." It writes that Astra meets the Critical threshold.

What does this mean, by their own definition? It means that with the right tools and access, the model is capable of finding previously unknown vulnerabilities and developing methods to exploit them in a multitude of well-protected systems, without a human guiding each step.

The numbers to support this: 100% on the exploit development benchmark and two previously unknown vulnerabilities found during testing.

September 3. Release. The model is released under the name GPT-6 Astra to a limited circle of organizations, and becomes publicly available the next day. One million tokens of context, $10 and $50 per million input and output tokens, respectively. Along with the model, an eleven-section system card is published.

And most importantly: the offensive cybersecurity capabilities were not released to the public. They are locked behind a separate trusted access program. The company did exactly what it promised.

No law obliged it to do so.

Where Did the Rules That No One Demanded Come From

The Preparedness Framework is an internal OpenAI document. Not a standard, not a regulation, not a legal act. The company wrote its own scale for the danger of its products and committed itself to following it.

The idea is simple. Model capabilities are divided by domains (cybersecurity, biology, autonomy), each domain has levels, and beyond a certain level, the model cannot be released until corresponding safeguards are in place. Critical is the highest tier.

For a long time, I couldn't decide how to feel about this. On one hand, the company voluntarily restricts its own product, loses time, and delays revenue. On the other hand, it writes the rules itself, checks its own compliance, and announces that everything is fine, and, as it turned out in August, rewrites the rules themselves along the way. It's like a drug manufacturer establishing an internal ethics committee and issuing itself permits based on its findings, while simultaneously editing the committee's charter.

But this construct has a logic that becomes clear if viewed as an attempt to keep up.

Laws are written over years. Discussion, negotiation, lobbying, amendments, enactment. Models change three generations in that time. Any law written for today's capabilities will be regulating yesterday's by the time it's enacted.

The internal framework is written in a month and rewritten in a week. It's flawed, non-binding, and biased. But it exists right now, and the law doesn't.

This is a statement that until the state learns to move at the industry's speed, the industry is either not regulated at all, or it regulates itself. The latter is better than the former only to the extent that the specific company is conscientious.

There was a precedent, by the way. In 2025, when models began demonstrating advanced capabilities in biology, OpenAI applied the same approach. Astra is the second application of this method. The method, it seems, has taken root.

What Critical Means

The word "critical" sounds like a headline about a machine uprising, and that hinders understanding of what it's about.

In practice, Critical in cybersecurity means the model can complete an entire attack chain without an operator. Once again! Independently find an unknown vulnerability, craft a working exploit for it, and apply it against a protected target, receiving only a general problem statement as input.

The difference between "assists" and "does it itself" is like the difference between a calculator and an accountant. Previous levels described the model as a tool in human hands. Critical describes the model as an executor.

At first, I thought, "Why not just remove the hacking capability from the model?" The answer came quickly and was disheartening.

There's nothing to remove.

There isn't a "hacking" module inside the model that you can approach and switch off with a toggle. There's a general skill: reading code, holding the system architecture in mind, and noticing where the actual behavior diverges from the author's intent. This is precisely the skill the model uses to find a bug in your pull request. "Finding a vulnerability" and "finding a bug" are the same operation. Only the intention of the requester differs.

The capability cannot be cut out because it's a byproduct of why the model was made in the first place.

That's why OpenAI didn't cut anything out. But they built a fence.

The Fence

I'll list what exactly was built, because the list itself is telling.

Classifiers on activations, meaning checks not only of the prompt text but also of the model's internal state during operation. Reasoning chain monitoring. Training on refusals considering the context of multiple conversations, not just one, because a dangerous request can be broken down into ten harmless ones. Automatic red-teaming, closing universal jailbreaks. Mandatory hardware security keys for employees. And separate rollout, which became a fact after the release: general capabilities went into regular pricing plans, offensive functions remained under the trusted access program.

And one number: the refusal rate on jailbreaks increased from 59% to 91.5%.

I reread it several times because it can be read in two opposite ways simultaneously.

The first: the best result in the industry, an increase of over thirty points, a huge engineering effort.

The second: approximately one in twelve bypass attempts succeed.

Both readings are correct. And this simultaneous validity of two mutually exclusive conclusions seems to be the most honest description of the current state of AI security.

Who Checks What When the Government Checks

Here I must admit the limits of my understanding. The details of the pre-release review were not published, and it's impossible to reconstruct them from the outside. It is known that the review was formal, concerned cybersecurity, and preceded the release.

The system card, released with the model, complements the picture but doesn't complete it. Eleven sections, from general security to the Preparedness section. A valuable document. With one caveat that the company itself points out: the results presented are its own internal assessments, except where an external assessor is explicitly named.

But some things can be said even without details, simply by the logic of the genre.

A pre-release review catches what the model knows at the time of the review. It doesn't catch what the model will learn in conjunction with tools that the reviewers didn't have. It doesn't catch combinations with other systems. It doesn't catch capabilities that will emerge when the model gains access to an environment where it can act, not just respond.

And that's precisely where Astra is aimed. The company positions it around working with computers and browsers, showing how the model assembles a house in Blender and turns it into a scene in Unreal Engine. This is indeed an agent working within third-party applications.

The problem lies in the nature of the object. We are used to certifying things with fixed properties. A bridge can withstand X tons, and that's its property, regardless of who drives over it. A model's capabilities depend on what it's given. The same model with and without access to tools is two different objects of review.

A security certificate for a model is a certificate for a specific configuration on a specific day. A month later, an ecosystem of integrations will grow around it that no one thought of at the time of the review.

This doesn't mean reviews shouldn't happen. Rather, it means that a review is not a final point, but a snapshot.

The Main Question I Don't Have an Answer To

Does voluntary self-limitation actually work.

Arguments for: the release was actually delayed by four weeks, money was actually lost, restrictions were actually imposed, offensive functions were actually kept out of public access and still are. All of this is externally verifiable and cost the company resources. If the framework were just a facade, it would have been cheaper to announce it and do nothing.

Arguments against: the company gave itself a clean bill of health. It wrote the rules, assessed compliance, announced the result, and rewrote the rules in the interim. There was an external review, but on a narrow issue and without public details. A conflict of interest is not eliminated by good faith; it is eliminated by independence, and there is no independence here.

And there's a third argument that outweighs both.

The fence is built around a specific model from a specific company. The capability for which it was built exists at the current level of technological development. It is reproducible. Sooner or later, comparable capabilities will appear where no fences are built: with a competitor with different ethics, in open-source weights, in some laboratory that considers this capability an asset rather than a risk.

The 20th century developed exactly one working recipe for dealing with dangerous knowledge: not to confiscate the knowledge, but the material. The equations for nuclear fission are published and available to any student, but this hasn't brought anyone closer to a weapon, because between the equation and the charge lie centrifuges and years of enrichment. The non-proliferation regime is built around a substance that can be weighed in kilograms, tracked from satellites, and locked up under guard.

There's nothing to weigh here. The danger lies in the level of capability that everyone will reach.

What Those Who Write Code Should Do About It

If a model like Astra can independently find unknown vulnerabilities, then both sides use this capability. Attacks become cheaper, but audits also become cheaper. A company that previously couldn't afford a pentest can now run its code through a model. This changes security economics more than any single incident.

The cost of negligence also changes. A vulnerability that previously required a diligent person with time and motivation now requires a prompt. Everything that lay unnoticed for years simply because no one was looking becomes discoverable.

The conclusion for me turned out to be unexpectedly down-to-earth: updating dependencies and closing known holes is now more important than defending against hypothetical ones.

Tea

I started reading that document with the feeling that I was reading another press release where the company talks about how responsible it is.

I finished with a different feeling. Not with anxiety or admiration, but with something akin to respect for the scale of the task that no one currently knows how to solve.

All participants in this story are acting reasonably. The company is honestly limiting its product and, more importantly, continues to limit it after release, when revenue pressure is highest. The government is honestly reviewing what it can review. Engineers are honestly building defenses and achieving the best results in the industry. And yet, the entire construct rests on the assumption that others will do the same. An assumption that is not backed by anything.

Brockman said "Welcome to the AGI era," and this phrase made all the headlines. It seems to me, however, that the real news of that week wasn't that, but the four weeks of delay that were barely mentioned.

The 20th century learned to control matter. The 21st faces the challenge of learning to control capability, which has no matter. It's unclear if this is even possible in principle.

And I never did finish my tea that day.
