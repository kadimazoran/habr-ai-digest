Breathing Life into the Fly

Before moving on, it's worth explaining how our model differs from a realistic biological simulation.

Our model has several levels: a neural network based on the connectome, a model of the organism's needs, a behavior controller, and a physical body control system. Observable behavior emerges from their joint work.

From the connectome, we take the connections between neurons and the number of anatomical synapses. However, this data is insufficient to uniquely reconstruct the network's dynamics. In our implementation, the connection weight is calculated as the number of synapses multiplied by a general amplification coefficient and the sign of excitation or inhibition.

The sign is assigned according to a simplified rule based on neurotransmitter annotation. These are our assumptions: the number of anatomical contacts is not a direct measure of the physiological strength of a connection.

Each neuron is represented by an identical LIF (leaky integrate-and-fire) model. It accumulates input, gradually returns to its initial state, and generates a pulse when a threshold is reached.

All cells have the same parameters: a membrane time constant of 20 ms, synaptic effect decay of 5 ms, and transmission delay of 2 ms. Calculations are performed with a 1 ms step. The threshold and other parameters were chosen by us; they are not provided with the connectome as measured characteristics of each neuron.

External world signals also require adapters. Image brightness is converted into input for selected photoreceptors; taste and contamination into corresponding groups of input cells, some of which have only preliminary functional correspondence. Hunger amplifies taste input, and sleep pressure reduces artificial excitation of certain descending neurons.

Additionally, the network receives a constant background and additional inputs to selected groups, including neurons used for locomotion control. Therefore, the mere presence of activity at the motor output does not prove that the signal correctly passed from the sensory organs through the biological circuit.

Action selection in the operational mode remains largely external. The controller directly receives hunger, sleep pressure, local taste signals, contamination, and support status. Based on predefined rules, it selects feeding, walking, grooming, or sleeping.

Odor tracking is handled by a separate algorithm: moving against the wind when an odor is detected and performing a cross-search when it is lost. In the current configuration of the search scene, the walking command is a mix of 75% from this algorithm and 25% from the neural decoder.

Similarly, in a hybrid escape scene, object approach is detected by a software image processor. It identifies the object by color and estimates the increase in its area. Therefore, a correct escape in this scene does not, by itself, confirm the operation of the connectome's visual circuit.

After an action is selected, a motor program is initiated: steps, proboscis extension, grooming, or sleep posture. It controls the joint actuators, and MuJoCo calculates body movement and contacts. Feedback exists: for example, food intake depends on the physical contact of the mouth with the food patch. However, the "neuron → muscle" correspondence is not restored in our model; engineering decoders and motor programs stand between neural activity and the body.

The Ordinary Fly World

So, the fly can walk around the arena. This is already great news, but a real fly can do many other things – groom itself, search for food, eat, avoid danger, and so on.

All of this needs to be implemented in our arena, and it needs to work autonomously, without manual activation of each action.

To achieve this, we added a model of internal needs. The fly now has an energy reserve, a food reservoir, a need for sleep, and contamination on specific body parts. These values change during the simulation: life processes consume energy, ingested food is gradually assimilated, wakefulness increases sleep pressure, and rest reduces it. Hunger depends on both the energy reserve and the fullness of the food reservoir.

The behavior controller matches these needs with available actions. Hunger increases the priority of feeding and searching, contamination increases the priority of grooming, and accumulated sleep pressure increases the priority of rest. Thresholds and minimum action durations are provided for transitions, so the fly doesn't switch between feeding and grooming at every calculation step. The order of actions is determined by the current state of the organism and the environment.

Food search begins with local sensations. The controller receives the odor concentration at two antennae and the local wind direction; it is not given the coordinates of the food source. The arena simulates an intermittent odor plume, carried by the wind and dissipating.

Upon detecting an odor, the fly moves against the wind. If it loses the scent, it begins to alternate cross-searches, gradually expanding the search area. This is a navigation algorithm we designed, operating on sensory feedback.

When the front legs detect the taste of food, the option to feed becomes available. The fly stops and extends its proboscis. Food enters the organism only upon physical contact of the mouth area with the food patch, considering the remaining food and available space in the reservoir. If the proboscis doesn't reach the surface, the attempt fails, after which the fly may move closer and try again. Odor helps find food, but does not provide food or energy on its own.

Grooming is also linked to the physical consequences of movement. The fly can rub its front legs together and run them over its head and antennae. Contamination reduction is calculated based on contacts and sliding: during head grooming, some contamination is transferred to the legs, and rubbing the legs together removes it.

Sleep has its own state and transitions. The fly assumes a stable posture, falls asleep, and sleep pressure begins to decrease. Awakening depends on the organism's state and the strength of external stimuli. In test scenarios, a weak tactile impulse did not wake the sleeping fly, while a strong one caused awakening with a delay.

Then, a reaction to an approaching object was introduced. Through two cameras, the fly receives images in which a software detector identifies an object of a specific color and estimates the increase in its area. If the signal is strong enough, the controller interrupts feeding or grooming, turns the body, and initiates a short escape maneuver.

A sleeping fly first goes through awakening. In a control run with vision disabled, the same approaching object did not trigger an escape.

The result is a connected system with feedback: sensations and internal needs determine the action, movement changes body position and access to resources, and the consequences of the action influence the next choice.

In one test run lasting 60 seconds of model time with the full connectome, walking, two feeding episodes, both types of grooming, sleep, and awakening were observed. The fly consumed 4.18 arbitrary units of food and did not starve; food in this demonstration environment was periodically replenished.

At this stage, autonomy meant that after launch, the fly switched between actions on its own according to predefined rules. The connectome participated in the hybrid system, while behavior selection and movement execution were supported by software controllers.

How closely the processes within the neural network correspond to a living fruit fly remained a separate question for the next stage.

Moving Towards Biological Plausibility

The current fly has one minor problem: we take its connectome and try to activate it in a way that stimulates the desired "fly-like behavior," acting partly through an external controller. But this doesn't guarantee that the activations in the brain occur as they do in a real fruit fly.

Fortunately, researchers have studied this issue and found out quite a bit.

For our transition to biological modeling, a relevant criterion is needed: a stimulus should evoke the expected response in the correct cells, with appropriate temporal dynamics and selectivity. For example, the response to an expanding object should be distinguishable from the response to a simple darkening.

Then, it's necessary to check how the response changes when the input or intermediate neuron groups are disabled. Externally convincing body movement without these checks confirms the operability of the entire assembled system but does not yet establish the biological mechanism of behavior.

Unfortunately, the transfer to a biologically plausible model was unsuccessful. The most we could achieve was for the fly's retina to distinguish contrast, but object movement was poorly handled.

What If We Had Gladiator Fights?

In reality, the social behavior of flies (and fruit flies in particular) is more complex than many of us might have thought. Even outside of mating rituals, individuals can warn each other about danger or large food reserves.

Just like many other animals on this planet, fruit flies can fight over food if they believe there won't be enough for everyone.

The repertoire of male fights is divided into distinct phases and is well-studied:

  • Approach and "wing threat" – raising one or both wings at an angle, a display

  • Lunge – a sudden upward-forward lunge of the entire body at the opponent, the primary unit of aggression measurement (lunges per minute are counted);

  • Front leg fencing, rear leg boxing;

  • Tussling – grappling, rolling into a ball, the only truly "unrestrained" element.

As a result of the fight, the winner usually takes the patch of nutrient mass (if it's yeast), while the loser lingers at the edges and fights worse afterward for some time. If a female is in sight, the males fight more aggressively and fiercely.

Female fights are less aggressive and less often related to food, mainly concerning egg-laying, for example, there can be competition between females for a safe and secluded place for eggs.

Now, let's try to implement a fight between males over food in our simulator.

The next experiment was "gladiator fights": we placed two males in the arena and offered them a common limited food supply. Each received its own organism state and behavior controller, while the food patch and physical environment were shared. Food eaten by one fly reduces the available supply for the other; collisions between body parts are calculated by MuJoCo.

In our model, proximity itself does not trigger a fight. As long as the fly successfully obtains food, conflict is suppressed. If feeding stops, hunger persists, an opponent is nearby, and the memory of recently found food is still active, the "frustration" level gradually increases.

Upon reaching the threshold, the controller permits aggressive behavior. Therefore, in tested scenes with a large food supply, males ate peacefully side-by-side, but with a small portion, they resorted to brawling. The controllers do not read the total remaining food – they react to their own feeding experience.

The brawl consists of turns towards the opponent, slow pushing, and front leg "fencing": raising, extending forward, a short downward movement, and returning to a stance. Motor programs control the joint actuators, and contacts and displacements occur in the physical simulation.

During the conflict, a conditional effort accumulates; upon reaching an individual threshold, the male retreats and recovers for some time. The threshold is randomly chosen for the episode by the same rule for both flies, so there is no predetermined winner.

A Dead Fly HAS SPOKEN to Humanity!! Her Answers Will Shock You.....

It would be interesting to try to communicate with a fly, at least about some everyday matters, and perhaps even convey useful information to it.

Before attempting to make the fly talk, we should determine what fruit flies can even talk about among themselves.

Quite expectedly, it's all related to their "daily life" (food, competition for food and mates, danger), and the coordination between individuals in the wild is not as complex as, for example, in honeybees and ants (this is due to the fact that fruit flies are solitary, not social, species).

In our project, we started with an artificial communication channel with the simulated nervous system. The first task was to read one need – "Need food." For this, we created the message NEED_FOOD, to which the interface assigns a fixed signature.

The need for food was already calculated by the organism model. We began encoding it with an additional input to 32 selected connectome cells. This is an artificial input: these cells are not declared as the discovered biological "hunger center."

The main check was to ensure that the signal corresponded to what was happening in the physical simulation. A hungry fly should maintain the message, after sufficient feeding – remove it, and after a small portion – continue to report the need.

For future reverse communication, we have prepared a protocol of messages "food to the left," "food to the right," and "food ahead," but the fly has not yet learned to use such hints; they are disabled in the demonstration. A console interface, a message log, and saving the channel state along with the simulation already exist.

The result is a preliminary one-way interface: we artificially introduce a need signal into the network and learn to recognize it by subsequent activity.
