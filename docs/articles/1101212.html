One of the most significant changes in interface design in recent years is happening almost imperceptibly. The screen has ceased to be an independent product with a fixed information architecture. Now the user formulates a request, the agent performs the task and returns UI components along with text — a table, a graph, a card with an action, or a map. This approach is called Generative UI, or GenUI. But a reconfigurable screen by itself is not a revolution. The industry has been creating personalized feeds and adaptive layouts for a long time, but each time the user has to learn the system's language, just a different one.

Two changes are now observed. The first is that the user's life situation, described in their own words, becomes the entry point into the scenario for the first time. The second is that the unit of work for a designer becomes a rule executed in millions of generated screens. Hence the term "human-centricity," which is so often heard alongside GenUI, and it should be interpreted literally.

What is human-centricity

Human-centricity is an approach to designing products and services where the starting point is the user's needs, context, emotional state, and well-being.

In the context of design, it boils down to three principles.

  • Contextuality. The interface is built around the user's life situation, not the catalog structure.

  • Reduced cognitive load. The system takes over the translation of the task into the service's language and does not require the user to learn its terminology.

  • Ethical interaction. At points of user vulnerability, manipulative patterns are excluded, and the rules designed by the designer protect their interests — including from the interests of the service itself.

First example: a marketplace, from uncertainty to purchase

The first two principles are classic user-centered design. Human-centricity distinguishes itself from it by the third principle, which is the least developed in the industry and, moreover, conflicts with revenue. I will provide two examples where all three principles are evident in action.

In classic e-commerce, the main point of customer drop-off is a cognitive dead end at the entry. The user has a task but not the name of the product. A selection from hundreds of cards and a panel of thirty filters create choice paralysis, motivation drops, and the session ends. Of course, they can Google it first, but these are extra steps in the user journey, and the search engine itself already suggests products, not necessarily from the platform they initially intended to visit.

Imagine a user comes to a marketplace with the request "I need something to wean my child off diapers."

The catalog turns into a digital concierge and handles the scenario in four steps.

  1. Clarifying uncertainty. The first screen clarifies the context ("It seems like the potty training stage is starting — the child is around two years old?") and suggests that the item is called "training pants." The user regains control over the request.

  2. Narrowing the context. Instead of a filter panel, the user sees four routes within the familiar marketplace anatomy — pants (direct answer), potty (related item), "transition kit" (ready-made solution), and reward stickers. A concierge differs from cross-selling by not offering unnecessary items.

  3. Dialogue instead of forms. In response to "I have a potty, but the child is afraid of it," the screen rebuilds, but doesn't completely remove everything from the previous request; the system must remember the context. The potty is removed from the results, a toilet seat with a step appears, and in the training pants cards, models with cartoon characters come to the forefront — the focus shifts from fear to play.

  4. Seamless completion. The purchase does not require continuing the dialogue because each card has a standard "Add to Cart" button in its usual place, and the size is selected by the child's weight. After clicking, the generation ends — the user proceeds to the standard checkout. The system does not experiment where muscle memory works.

In this example, the source of the context remains an open question. The remark about potty training is an inference from the request, and it hides the assumption that a mother is writing. If a grandmother is buying training pants for her grandson, instead of feeling "they understood me," she gets "they confused me with someone else"; if the child is three and a half and training is taking too long, the same remark will increase anxiety. Empathetic selection is based on hypotheses about the life situation. The cost of error is a pang of anxiety that the catalog would not have caused, so the concierge must err more cautiously than a filter. The accuracy of hypotheses can be improved with long-term customer memory, but caution is needed. There is a difference between rules that the user set themselves and can view and edit, and a dossier that the service has compiled based on their behavior — no one likes to have a detailed card catalog kept about them unnoticed.

However, the marketplace is an example where the change looks modest, as ultimately it's an improved search.

Second example: government services, from a catalog of services to orchestrating a situation

The government services portal is structured as a catalog of departmental services. To get something, you need to know its name, what services are included, and in what order to process them. "Life situations" sections have already appeared on the first screen — but they are designed manually for a few dozen of the most common cases. A person whose situation is not on this list either figures out five services on their own or goes to an MFC (Multifunctional Center) to a live employee. This employee is the orchestrator who breaks down the situation into services, collects documents, and knows where everything goes.

A human-centric portal makes the assistant part of the interface itself. On the first screen are the most common life situations; if yours is not among them, the user describes it in their own words, for example, "attach my grandmother to a polyclinic, she lives with us without registration." Then the scenario is processed in five steps.

  1. Situation analysis. The agent asks clarifying questions and breaks down the situation into a chain of services — temporary registration, attachment, and, if necessary, a power of attorney. The user sees this chain as a single plan with steps and documents.

  2. One application instead of several. A composite interactive form is assembled from blocks certified by departments. The bulk of the data is pulled from the profile and registries; the user fills in only what the government doesn't have.

  3. Invariant confirmation. The verification window and signature are a fixed host screen, the same for any chain. Here, the user must know exactly what they are agreeing to.

  4. Routing. After signing, parts of the application go to different departments. If something is missing, not a rejection comes, but a request for clarification or an interdepartmental request that the agent initiates themselves.

  5. Tracker. The user has a status of execution, like a postal item, and they don't wait but see that their issue is being handled and at what stage it is.

Here, it's not the screen that changes, but the unit of service, which becomes the person's situation, not the department's service. The agent orchestrates a chain of requests within policies — much like it's done in agent scenario builders — and the interface becomes an imprint of this chain. GenUI in this scheme is the simplest part. The complex part is the legal basis for the composite application (who is responsible if the chain is assembled incorrectly), data access between departments, and the regulation by which a missing document generates a request, not a rejection. Without these layers, it remains a beautiful chat that collects a form, and then five separate rejections arrive.

And here lies the conflict of interest, and it's structured differently than in the marketplace. Initially, "rejection by default" appeared as a compromise between departments and a way not to overcomplicate the service. But for the user, it works as a dark pattern, as the system shifts all the work of data validation onto them. Conversion to successfully provided services and payments drops, which formally saves the budget of the executing department but harms loyalty to the state as a whole. The service will have to evolve towards a "clarification instead of rejection" policy, and here lies the main difficulty. In the marketplace, the host and the interested party are two entities with whom one can negotiate. In the state, the policy will be written by one department responsible for digitalization, and executed by dozens of others, who have their own interests and workload.

The two examples show two types of conflict — commercial and institutional — and the same boundary. To understand why the boundary lies here, we need to analyze how generation works.

What's under the hood: four degrees of freedom

GenUI is a methodology with several degrees of freedom, and the higher the degree, the more decisions about screen composition the model makes, and the less the designer does. For a human-centric platform, choosing the level is a decision about who is delegated responsibility for the screen.

Static level. The designer pre-designs components — product card, graph, form — and the agent simply selects the appropriate one and populates it with data. This is how Vercel AI SDK UI and most corporate assistants work. The interface is fully predictable, but each new scenario requires development.

Declarative level. The agent describes the screen in JSON, using only components from an approved catalog, and the client renders the description itself. This is how Google's A2UI protocol and the Open-JSON-UI format, attributed to OpenAI, are structured. The agent freely combines blocks but cannot create an element absent from the design system.

Open level. The model generates HTML, CSS, and JavaScript from scratch for each request. The only major production example is the dynamic view in Gemini and AI Mode in Google Search, launched on November 18, 2025. Maximum expressiveness with minimal predictability.

Hybrid MCP Apps. The MCP protocol extension, official since January 26, 2026, stands apart, where the interface is designed by the service, and the agent merely calls it. This is how Figma, Canva, Slack, Asana, and Salesforce brought their own widgets to Claude and ChatGPT, which run in an isolated iframe within the dialogue.

Do people like such interfaces

Judging by research — yes, with reservations. The most cited, "Generative UI: LLMs are Effective UI Generators," was published by Google Research in February 2026. The authors compared five response formats to a single request: a website designed by a designer, a website generated by Gemini 3 Pro, the first Google search result, plain text, and markdown. In pairwise comparisons, the generated page won against markdown in 82.8% of cases, against the first search result — in 90%, against plain text — in 97%. The expert page was stronger, winning 50% of the time against 35% for generation. People were shown finished pages; generation time was not included in the evaluation, and visual consistency between generations was ensured by a detailed "Style" section in the system prompt.

Critical works present a less optimistic picture. A systematic review of twenty studies (Computers journal, 2026) showed that identical prompts yield different interfaces both between tools and upon repeated launches of the same one, and multi-page interfaces lose navigation coherence. The authors call generative systems "probabilistic design agents" and recommend giving users the ability to fix structural decisions.

The study "Design Theater" (AIES 2026) tested how well tools fulfill stated requirements. Out of a sample of 120 interfaces, over a quarter of design decisions were not implemented, and among functional requirements — a third. Incidentally, it was found that different tools produce similar layouts for the same request.

Nielsen Norman Group takes a broader view. Jakob Nielsen called generative AI the first new interaction paradigm in sixty years, where the user sets the intent and outcome, and the sequence of actions is left to the system. His colleagues describe the future of design as outcome-oriented — the designer designs goals and constraints — and warn that constantly changing interfaces will create usability problems.

Users prefer a screen assembled for their request as long as it preserves their mental models and performs the stated behavior. Both reservations boil down to the question of who is responsible for the generated screen.

Who owns the interface: the service, the host, or the model

Behind the technical classification lies the question of ownership and power. When an agent shows the user a result card, who made the decision about its composition and visual language?

In the MCP Apps model, the service owns the interface. Slack brings its widget, Figma brings its own, and each conforms to its team's design system. This is the logic of an app store, where the brand is preserved, complex interactivity is possible, but there is no unified visual language between services. ChatGPT is already limiting this freedom with strict guidelines. They require system fonts and colors, prohibit custom typography even in full-screen mode, allow branded accents only on main buttons, and fix display modes — card, carousel, full screen.

In the A2UI model, the client where the agent runs owns the interface. The client has a catalog of trusted components, and any service can only populate them with data. This is the logic of a native platform with a unified design system, which provides complete consistency but dissolves the service's brand.

In the Gemini dynamic view model, the model owns the interface. It determines the style based on the request, and neither the service nor the host directly controls the result. Google compensates for this with detailed style instructions in the system prompt and returns control to the designer through it.

What the market showed

In ChatGPT, according to independent counts, about two thousand applications have been published; Claude's connector catalog grew by several hundred positions in one summer. Gemini is currently showing generated pages only to subscribers in the US. Neither OpenAI nor Anthropic publish usage metrics, so real prevalence has to be judged by indirect data.

The only major case with numbers turned out to be negative. On September 29, 2025, OpenAI, together with Stripe, launched Instant Checkout — purchasing directly in the chat — and promised that over a million Shopify merchants would connect to it. According to data Shopify provided to Forrester in February 2026, about thirty stores were actually working; The Information counted about a dozen in early March. In March, OpenAI phased out native checkout and moved purchases to the retailers' own apps. Walmart, which listed about two hundred thousand products in ChatGPT, told WIRED that conversion within the chat was three times lower than when redirecting to its own website. Google responded with the Universal Commerce Protocol with Shopify, Target, and Walmart under the "find in AI — buy in store" model without in-chat purchases.

Forecasts, however, remain positive. Gartner expects that by the end of 2026, 40% of enterprise applications will have built-in agents, and by 2028, a third of the user experience will shift from native applications to agent frontends. At the same time, the same Gartner warns that more than 40% of agent projects will be closed by 2027 due to high costs and unclear value.

The boundary between mutable and invariant

What is missing from the research is data on the long-term effect. No one has yet measured the dynamics of muscle memory, task execution speed, and cognitive load in people who have been working for weeks with interfaces that are reassembled with each interaction.

The strongest objection against generative interfaces is that if the screen is new every time, the user loses the learned pattern, and with it, speed and confidence. The answer to this is quite simple — the agent should assemble the screen from a fixed set of patterns and templates — that is, work at the declarative level described above. Templates solve four tasks at once.

  • Consistency. The interface remains recognizable from session to session.

  • Safety. The agent cannot generate a payment button that is not in the catalog.

  • Testability. A finite set of components instead of an infinite space of generations.

  • Accessibility. It is incorporated into the component once and reproduced in every response.

The fifth argument comes from "Design Theater," which showed that models still converge to templates. The only question is whether these templates will be explicit and controllable or implicit and random.

But templates don't cover everything. Muscle memory is formed for a specific layout of a specific screen. An accountant who clicks the same button in the same position hundreds of times a day will gain nothing from a "similar" screen. They need the exact same screen. Where is the boundary between a screen that the agent reassembles and a screen that should remain invariant?

On one side are all those that will remain for classic websites and applications, and there are four reasons for this. Transactional and regulated scenarios require auditability and reproducibility, because it is necessary to know exactly what the user saw at the moment of agreement, and a generated screen is irreproducible. Daily work tools are the domain of muscle memory. And finally, addressability, because bookmarks, sharing, SEO, and legal links to terms require a permanent URL, which is absent in the agent's response.

On the other side are all those where generated pages already win, i.e., one-time reading, research, comparison, selection, action plan. Google's research showed that such a page surpasses the average site from search results.

This boundary is the first thing a human-centric platform designer designs. The selection screen on the marketplace and the situation plan for government services are on the mutable side because they must adapt to the person. The "Add to Cart" button, checkout, signature screen are on the invariant side because muscle memory and trust are built on them.

The designer's new job: rules instead of screens

In the new approach, the designer's work is layered into three levels. The first is a strict design system of atomic components with described anatomy, spacing, states, and adaptive behavior. The second is UX policies in a machine-readable format, i.e., prompt design as a discipline. The third is training the agent in ergonomics, where Fitts's Law, Jacob's Law, and Gestalt principles should be as immutable for it as the laws of physics are for an engineer.

The main risk of a generative interface — the stochasticity of the model — is mitigated by an architecture where generation is divided into two independent stages. First, the agent, understanding the request and data, makes a decision only about the semantic structure and outputs a structured description. Then, a deterministic UI engine takes components from the design system and assembles them according to the policies written by the designer. The agent is the chef who chooses the ingredients; the kitchen, which only knows how to strictly follow the recipe, assembles the dish.

It would seem that if the model takes over screen composition, the designer's role should shrink. But the opposite happens, as the scale of decision-making changes. When a designer manually designs a screen, their error affects one screen. When a designer designs a policy, it is executed in millions of generated screens. The designer transforms from an author of individual screens into an architect of the interface environment.

The design system transforms from a library into a machine-readable contract for the agent. The component catalog becomes the only vocabulary accessible to the agent. Color and typography tokens set the framework. Composition rules are described in a format that the model reads. Google Labs already offers the DESIGN.md format, a text description of the design system that the agent loads before generation.

Personal catalog and five decisions of the hybrid model

If the component catalog and policies can belong not only to the service and the host, they can also belong to the user. Imagine an agent that assembles any interface — ordering pizza, stock analytics, doctor's appointment, moving plan — in the idiom that a particular person is accustomed to, whether it's the bottom tabs and iOS typography for one, or the Material logic for another. The common vocabulary of cards does not need to be coordinated between hosts, as it can be personal.

Nielsen Norman Group states this bet directly. Today, interfaces are designed for the average user, and GenUI will allow each user to have their own assembled. At first glance, this contradicts their warning about the harm of constantly changing interfaces. The contradiction is resolved if personalization is a one-time chosen and saved idiom. The authors of the Maru system (UIST 2026) call this the persistence problem. Currently, the user has to rephrase preferences with each generation, whereas they should be saved as named rules.

Human-centricity in this logic is a set of specific design decisions and forms a hybrid model of five points.

  1. The skeleton is fixed by the host. System actions, navigation, confirmations, signatures, and payments are invariant.

  2. The body of the screen is assembled declaratively from the component catalog.

  3. Deep scenarios — editor, map, dashboard — live as the service interface in a sandbox.

  4. Open generation is allowed only for one-time content, such as explanations, simulations, and visualizations.

  5. The user-approved appearance is saved and reproduced wherever they encounter an agent.

This model is presented more as a hypothesis that still needs to be tested. With the advent of GenUI, practice has emerged, and a set of standards for it has not yet been assembled. Norms in design are established by business, research consensus, and regulators, but the first standards come from practice and user habits.

Trust versus conversion

In the agent channel, trust converts into retention more effectively than pressure into conversion, and in the future, this will manifest in customer retention, for which several arguments speak.

  1. Economic. In the agent channel, trust is structured differently than in a catalog. In a catalog, the user chooses themselves, and they at least see the service's pressure. They delegate the choice to the concierge, and one noticed deception is enough for it to stop.

  2. Regulatory. The Digital Services Act in the EU explicitly prohibits platforms from using interfaces that manipulate user choice, and generative interfaces are not exempt from this prohibition.

  3. Structural. An agent living with the host — ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini — acts on behalf of the user, the host writes the policies, and services are left to compete for the agent to choose them. The more scenarios move to hosts, the less room the service has for pressure.

The main objection hits the first argument. It hinges on the word "noticed." In a catalog, the user sees alternatives, and thus sees the pressure. They delegate the choice to the concierge — and see no alternatives at all. The agent channel makes manipulation less visible. A concierge who simply doesn't show the best offer won't cause a pang of distrust because there's nothing to compare it with. The fragility of trust works against gross deceptions; subtle ones are safer for business in this channel than in a catalog.

The invisibility of manipulation is an argument for the explicitness and portability of policies. If the user cannot check the concierge from within one session, the check must come from outside — from comparing channels and from machine-readability of rules. If the concierge's policy is explicitly written, in a format that the model reads, then the regulator, auditor, and the user's own agent can read it. Manipulation embedded in the model's behavior, however, cannot be checked.

The service has two channels, its own concierge and presence in the host's agent, and it's unprofitable to abandon the second — it brings traffic. In its own channel, the service writes policies itself; in the host's channel, the host writes them, and if the user's personal catalog lives there, the host enforces it as a condition of access to the channel. A user accustomed to the protected host agent evaluates the service's own concierge by the same standards, and the discrepancy between two responses to the same request becomes that visible alternative. Thus, human-centricity ceases to depend on the goodwill of business — through competition between channels.

Where the approach is most useful and what to assemble it from

The effect of the approach is proportional to the gap between the human's words and the system's vocabulary. In trade, it is strongest in industries with "lost words." In hardware stores, "a thing to hang a shelf on drywall" is a "butterfly anchor," and the buyer almost never knows the term. In auto parts, they say "something is knocking in the front on bumps" instead of "stabilizer link." In pharmacies, they ask for "something for knee pain after running, but not pills." Here, the agent replaces the sales consultant, without whom a purchase often simply doesn't happen, and scales it to all visitors.

Next are services with implicit requests and high error costs. In travel, "warm in November, fly no more than four hours, with a child" is a request that cannot be expressed with filters. In real estate, they look for "close to work without transfers and a good school nearby." In banks and insurance, they ask to "save for daughter's education, but with the option to withdraw if necessary." And government services are the most grateful area because the gap between the language of departments and the language of people is maximal there, and the chain of services is the longest.

The approach works poorly in daily work tools like trading terminals, ERP, dispatch systems, cash registers, and medical information systems. There, the user knows the subject vocabulary better than the agent. The agent is useful there as an assistant within the interface, not as its author.

A ready-made solution into which you can load a catalog and get such a marketplace or portal does not yet exist. But the building blocks are mature and reproduce the described architecture, where the agent determines the structure, and the engine assembles the screen from your catalog. For your own website and application, the main candidate is json-render from Vercel Labs. The developer describes the catalog of allowed components and actions, the model generates only JSON within this catalog, and from one specification, React, Vue, Svelte, React Native, and PDF are rendered. Nearby is Tambo, an open-source toolkit for React with a built-in agent loop, streaming, and MCP support, and CopilotKit, the most complete framework with agent runtimes, focused on product teams. assistant-ui provides chat primitives with generative UI based on a whitelist of components. A2UI from Google is a protocol with ready-made renderers and a Material catalog.

Who will set the norm first

The industry is at a crossroads between a unified vocabulary of cards understood by all hosts and a personal catalog where the vocabulary and policies belong to the user, and only the data transfer format and constraints are standardized, as A2UI and MCP Apps already do. Which bet will pay off is unclear, but both require the same thing: to draw a human-centric boundary through services so that the user doesn't have to re-learn each "empathetic" agent. A unified interaction layer should emerge — a UX cocoon that protects the human's mental space from the chaos of hundreds of different services.

Waiting for market maturity is a reasonable strategy. But users' mental models are being formed right now, and according to Jacob's Law, all subsequent interfaces will be evaluated by the standards of the one they first learned. Whoever starts first takes on the risks but also gets the chance to become a trendsetter in the literal sense. Their rules will become heuristics that will be taught in five years.

The smartest agent does not guarantee victory. The winner will be the one whose interaction rules become the norm first, and the rules will be in favor of the human.
