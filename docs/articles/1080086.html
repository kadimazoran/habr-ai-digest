Why does the four, upon crossing the equals sign, suddenly change its sign? What kind of portal is this? On the left, it was +4, it flew through = — and came out on the other side as -4.

If a child asks "why?", the answer "well, that's the rule" sounds suspicious even to someone who graduated school long ago. And if you ask "why?" again — you might suddenly discover a rather large rabbit hole.

Thus began MathRoots.

I Never Really Understood Teachers at the Board

Perhaps I should make a confession here. In my youth, I can hardly recall a time when a teacher explained something at the board, and I sat there thinking: "Oh! Everything is clear." Usually, the opposite happened. The teacher wrote something, the class nodded in unison, I also nodded just in case, and then I'd go home and start literal archaeological excavations.

Why is there a minus here? Where did the two come from? Why is it allowed to simplify here, but not there? Why did the fraction suddenly flip? Why did the signs change when opening parentheses? Why is it allowed to do what we are doing right now?

I had to piece together the answers: from one textbook, from another, from friends, from examples, sometimes just starting with the answer at the end of the problem book and trying to reconstruct the entire path backward.

And after many years, I realized something quite simple. Perhaps it wasn't mathematics itself that was difficult for me. Perhaps it was the format of transmitting mathematics.

There Is No Such Thing as "Moving"

Let's return to our equation:

Imagine a regular balance scale. On the left pan, there are two identical closed boxes of unknown mass and four more one-kilogram weights. On the right, there are ten such weights. The scale is balanced.

This, in essence, is the meaning of the = sign. It's not a calculation arrow or an invitation to move something from left to right. It's a statement: the left side currently has the same amount as the right side.

We want to find the mass of one box. The four weights on the left are in the way, so we want to remove them. But if we remove four weights only from the left pan, the balance will be disturbed. Therefore, we remove the same weight from both sides:

And this is no longer just a "rule for solving one equation." The child has received a model: equality is balance; if you change one side, you must make an equivalent change to the other side.

Much later, this can be called an equivalent transformation, a property of equality, and so on. But first, there is a physical model that can be grasped mentally.

For a programmer, I would describe this using the word invariant. We can transform the system's state as much as we want, as long as the main condition is maintained: the left side remains equal to the right side.

After this, "moving across the equals sign" becomes just convenient syntactic sugar.

And Then the Programmer in Me Completely Ruined the Lesson

After the balance scale explanation, I thought: why should a math problem be presented as a linear list of steps?

Typically, a textbook looks like this:

Step 1
Step 2
Step 3
Step 4
Answer

But in programming, we rarely perceive complex systems this way. A function has dependencies, a package has dependencies, a bug has a root cause, one abstraction is built on top of another.

Why not show mathematical knowledge in the same way?

Thus, the main idea of MathRoots emerged:

Every complex problem has roots.

Instead of a linear instruction, I decided to represent the problem as a graph of concepts. At the bottom are the most fundamental things, higher up are the properties of operations, then algebraic constructs, intermediate states, and finally, the goal itself.

For 2x + 4 = 10, it looks something like this:

Layer 4         x = 3
                  ▲
Layer 3         2x = 6
                  ▲
Layer 2    Dividing both sides by 2
                  ▲
Layer 1    Subtracting 4 from both sides
                  ▲
Layer 0       Maintaining equality

And here came the thought that I like the most: if a person doesn't understand the top node, there's no need to explain the top node ten more times. Perhaps the problem lies three levels below.

That's Why the Interface Isn't Quite Like a Textbook

When knowledge turns into a graph, a regular text page becomes a bit of a hindrance. Therefore, the central interface became a large interactive canvas — something like an Infinite Canvas.

The "Why?" Button Turned Out to Be More Interesting Than the "Answer" Button

One of my favorite ideas in MathRoots is the "Why can I do this?" button.

Most educational solutions perfectly answer the question: "What to do next?" But the question is much more interesting: "Why am I even allowed to perform this step?"

Then a Math Debugger Appeared

And here, the professional deformation of a programmer finally won.

If a program crashes on the fifteenth level of the call stack, we don't run the same function fifty more times hoping it will "get trained." We look for the root cause.

Why can't a mathematical error be treated the same way?

Thus, the Gap Finder / Stuck Debugger was born.

Suppose a student makes a mistake while solving an equation. Instead of immediately showing the correct answer, you can go down through the dependencies of the current step.

For myself, I call this cognitive debt. It's very similar to technical debt. A small problem in the foundation might not cause issues for a long time. New levels of abstraction are built on top of it, then more, and one day the entire structure becomes so shaky that the person concludes: "I guess I just don't have a mathematical mind."
