They formulate questions as they please, omit important details, or limit themselves to a short "it doesn't work" or "help." As a result, article selection becomes almost random: articles that are only loosely related to the request appear in the search results.

It can be worse: the required article isn't in the database at all. Then the LLM tries to assemble an answer from what it found on related topics. The result is a coherent text that may have only a distant relation to the user's problem. The model, in general, tries its best, but no one provided it with the necessary input data.

In this article, I'll explain how I decided to tackle both problems simultaneously: change the way requests and articles are matched, and then use the same thematic markup to identify gaps in the knowledge base and prepare drafts of new materials.

For the examples below, we'll use typical requests to an internet provider's support: problems with connection, payment, and tariff limitations. The idea itself, of course, is not limited to this subject area.

Why Ordinary Vector Search Was Not Enough

Vector search works well when the query contains enough information about the subject of the conversation. But support requests often look very different.

"It doesn't work."

"Help, please."

"The internet is down again."

"Payment failed."

For such messages, it can sometimes be difficult for a person to understand what exactly happened. A universal embedding model also doesn't read minds: it converts text into a vector and allows searching for semantically similar materials. And when the original text says almost nothing about the problem, similarity becomes a rather conditional guide.

As a result, articles that are formally related to the request may appear in the search results but do not help solve it. For example, articles about payment and router setup might be placed close together simply because both relate to situations where the client has something not working.

But there's a more serious problem: what to do if there's no suitable article in the database at all?

In this system, the LLM is not allowed to invent facts or suggest solutions that are not present in the source materials. Therefore, when the search returns only indirectly related instructions, the model is forced to assemble an answer from what is available. In the best case, the client receives general recommendations that do not solve their problem. In the worst case, the answer looks quite coherent but avoids the main issue.

Moreover, it's difficult to understand what went wrong based on the answer itself. The search might have failed to find the correct article, even if it exists, or the necessary information might genuinely be missing from the database. For the client, the difference is negligible, but for support, these are two different tasks: in one case, the article selection needs improvement; in the other, the knowledge base needs to be supplemented.

Therefore, I decided not to look for another universal embedding model but to change the approach to matching requests and articles.

Instead of a Universal Space, a Space of Topics

The new approach is based on a set of topics, each describing a specific type of problem or user intent. Slugs are used for the machine representation of topics. For this specific project, there were 18 topics identified through working with real requests.

The source material consisted of approximately four thousand tickets. The taxonomy was formed iteratively: the initial set of topics was checked against real data, then refined when overlaps, overly broad categories, or cases that couldn't be described by existing labels were discovered.

The number 18 itself is not important here. For another product, there might have been more or fewer topics. The point is to describe the subject area in sufficient detail to solve the practical task, without turning classification into an endless catalog of specific cases.

Jev from TypeSafe AI is used for labeling. The model analyzes the text and returns scores for relevance to different topics. As a result, a request is represented not by a single vector in a universal semantic space, but by a set of topics with corresponding scores. When calculating completeness, these scores are used as topic weights.

For example, the message "Paid, but no internet" might receive scores for two areas: payment and lack of connection. This doesn't necessarily mean the model has identified two independent problems. Rather, it indicates which topics might be relevant to the request and how pronounced each one is.

Knowledge base articles are labeled in the same way. As a result, both the request and the article are described in the same coordinate system: you can compare not only the texts as a whole but also how well a specific article corresponds to certain topics.

This changes the nature of the search. Instead of the question "Which articles are semantically similar to this text?" a more specific one arises: "Which topics does the request cover, and which articles truly address these topics?"

The model's scores should not be interpreted as probabilities in a strict statistical sense. They are numerical estimates of thematic relevance used in further calculations. Their practical meaning is determined by how the labeling is structured and what thresholds are applied in the algorithm.

How to Determine if Found Articles Are Sufficient

Article ranking alone does not solve the problem of knowledge base completeness. Even if the best article is a good match for the request, it might only cover part of the issue. Conversely, one article might not seem very close to the entire request, but together with another, they might cover all the necessary topics.

Therefore, I evaluate not only the relevance of the best article but also the completeness of the entire set of found articles.

First, the system selects significant topics for the request. Each topic has a score, and only those that pass a given significance threshold are considered. Insignificant scores and pre-excluded topics that might create noise are not taken into account.

Then, article selection begins. The algorithm selects no more than a set number of articles—three by default. At each step, it finds the article that best matches the topics not yet covered by previous articles.

There are two separate actions here. First, articles are ranked by their thematic relevance scores. Then, the selected article is checked against each remaining topic: is it considered sufficiently covered in this article? A separate threshold is used for this. If the article's score for a topic is below it, the algorithm does not consider the topic covered, even if the article was the best among the remaining candidates.

Let's break it down with an example.

Suppose a user writes: "Paid, but no internet." The system identifies two significant topics: payment with a weight of 0.5 and lack of connection with a weight of 0.4.

There are two articles in the database. The first explains how to check payment, and the second is about connection diagnostics. Each covers its topic well, so the algorithm can select both articles and cover the entire set of significant topics. The completeness of the article set will be 100%.

If, however, only the article about payment is available, the topic of lack of connection will remain uncovered. The completeness will be approximately 56%: out of the total weight sum of 0.9, only the topic with a weight of 0.5 will be covered.

Why not simply count how many topics are covered? Because topics can have different weights. If a request relates to two topics, scored as 0.7 and 0.3, covering the first gives 70%, and the second gives 30%. The simple count of topics would not reflect this difference.

Formally, completeness is calculated as the sum of weights of covered significant topics divided by the sum of weights of all significant topics. The weights themselves don't have to sum to one: by dividing by the total sum, we still get a proportion.

In implementation, this is a greedy cover algorithm. It sequentially selects the most useful article at each step, rather than iterating through all possible article combinations to find a mathematically optimal set. For the current task, this is a practical compromise: the number of articles is limited, and the calculation itself remains relatively simple.

The default completeness threshold is 0.55. If the found articles sufficiently cover the significant topics of the request, the system does not consider such a request an indication of a knowledge base gap. If the completeness is below the threshold, the system further analyzes the relevance of the best individual article.

And here begins the next part of the logic. Low completeness doesn't necessarily mean a new article needs to be created. If an individual article is already sufficiently close to the request, the problem might be that it doesn't cover the topic thoroughly enough. In such cases, the request is classified as a reason to improve existing articles. If, however, there is practically no suitable article, the system suggests creating a new one.

Thus, the algorithm distinguishes between two scenarios: the information is in the base, but it needs to be explained better, or the necessary information is genuinely missing from the base.

Of course, this is a heuristic, not proof that the bot will definitely answer correctly or incorrectly. The completeness threshold, topic significance, and minimum article score influence the result. But even such an assessment allows moving from a vague signal of "the search found nothing useful" to more specific analysis.

From Repetitive Requests to Article Drafts

Once the system learned to identify requests for which the completeness of the found articles was insufficient, the next question arose: how to turn this signal into a useful update to the knowledge base?

Manually reviewing all such requests would not be particularly interesting. Support already has the conversation history, and employees are not required to reread it daily looking for topics that lacked a dedicated article.

Therefore, the next step is to find repetitive requests.

The system groups similar tickets and selects cases that occurred several times. In the current implementation, the minimum group size is three requests. This helps filter out isolated non-standard situations, although, of course, repetition itself doesn't prove that a topic requires a separate article.

Then, operator responses are extracted from the correspondence. Short and uninformative responses are filtered out: for example, messages where the employee merely asks for clarification or states that they are looking into the situation. Such replies might be appropriate in a dialogue but are almost useless as a basis for an instruction.

The remaining question-and-answer pairs are passed to an LLM, which prepares a draft article. Essentially, the model compiles material from existing conversations, trying to turn disparate answers into a coherent instruction.

There is an important limitation here: the quality of the draft depends on the quality of the original responses. If operators provided conflicting recommendations or if essential details were missing from the correspondence, generation alone cannot fix the situation. The model can structure the material but should not invent missing facts on its own.

To prevent automation from turning into a factory of new, questionable-quality articles, no more than five drafts are created per run. The process is initiated manually from the administrative panel, where you can select the analysis period: a day, a week, or a month. The main parameters are in the settings, so redeploying the application is not required to change thresholds and limits.

This mode allows the system to be used as a regular audit tool. It doesn't try to rewrite the entire knowledge base in one pass but gradually identifies topics that are worth addressing.

Why Publication Remains a Human Task

At this stage, the temptation arises to automate everything completely: detect a gap, gather responses, generate an article, and publish it immediately. Technically, such a pipeline can be built. The question is how reasonable it is to trust it without verifying the content.

In support, errors in the original dialogues easily turn into knowledge base errors. An operator might have responded in the context of a specific client, considered details that were not included in the ticket, or simply made a mistake. If such a response is transferred to a universal instruction without verification, the error will start to be reproduced in the bot's automatic responses.

Therefore, the LLM prepares a structured draft, and a human employee checks the facts, assesses the article's completeness, and decides whether the result can be published. The routine work of finding similar tickets and preparing the text is automated, but the decision to add an article remains with a person.

This is the human in the loop—not just a safeguard against model errors, but a division of tasks considering the strengths of each participant. LLMs process large volumes of correspondence faster and assemble material into a coherent structure. Humans are better at assessing the reliability of recommendations, noticing missing context, and understanding whether the text is suitable for actual support work.

As a result, the employee doesn't have to start every article from scratch. They receive specific material based on repetitive requests and can focus on content verification rather than searching for source data.

What Was Discovered

The most telling result was discovered when analyzing requests related to exceeding the traffic limit.

Such cases occurred rarely, but users occasionally asked relevant questions. However, the knowledge base had neither a separate article on this topic nor an instruction to help understand the situation. The problem was not new to support; it simply hadn't been reflected in the documentation.

Previously, the bot would still try to answer automatically in such cases, using found articles on related topics. The result was an answer that might have seemed quite convincing but didn't explain what to do specifically when exceeding the traffic limit. The model lacked the necessary information and assembled the answer from what was at hand.

The system detected repetitive requests for which existing articles were insufficient and prepared a draft article based on operator responses. After verification, the article was added to the knowledge base. Now the bot has a suitable instruction to rely on when answering such questions.

For me, this is the practical value of the approach: not just to improve search within an existing base, but to find topics that users actually encounter but, for some reason, don't end up in the documentation. Even rare requests can reveal a gap that leads to an inaccurate automatic response.

Limitations and What Else Needs to Be Tested

The current implementation solves a practical problem but does not claim to be a universal method for measuring knowledge base quality.

First, the taxonomy of topics itself requires attention. If topics are defined too broadly, different problems might fall into the same category. If too narrowly, classification becomes noisy, and minor changes in wording start affecting scores. The set of topics needs periodic review with new requests and adjustments as the product evolves.

Second, the completeness and thematic relevance thresholds are currently configurable heuristics. They allow controlling the sensitivity of gap detection but do not replace validation on a labeled dataset. For a full assessment, a set of requests needs to be collected, manually determine which articles truly match each one, and measure how well the algorithm's results align with expert evaluation.

Third, Jev is used as an external service with a closed implementation. This simplifies integration but limits the ability to control the model's internal workings. The result depends on the quality of its thematic assessments, so if the model or subject area changes significantly, the system should be re-evaluated.

Finally, the generation of new articles is limited by what already exists in the correspondence. If operators did not provide a complete answer or used different approaches to the same problem, the model cannot reliably reconstruct missing information. This is precisely why human review of the draft remains part of the process, not a temporary limitation of the first version.

Separately, the practical effect needs to be assessed: how often the system finds real gaps, how many proposed articles are actually published, how the proportion of requests the bot answers with existing articles changes, and how accurate the answers are after new articles are added. This is an area for future work, not a result that can be considered already measured.

Instead of a Conclusion

In this task, I was interested not so much in replacing one search mechanism with another, but in using the same thematic model for two related purposes.

First, it helps understand which articles match a request. Then, it allows assessing whether these articles are sufficient and identifying repetitive questions that the knowledge base doesn't yet cover. From such requests, drafts of new articles can be compiled, reviewed, and the documentation can be gradually updated based on real demand.

This creates a feedback loop: requests show the problems users are facing, thematic markup helps match them with existing articles, and identified gaps become a reason to update the knowledge base.

The idea is not limited to internet provider support. It is applicable wherever there is a flow of requests, a set of articles, and an automated assistant that needs to respond based on these instructions. In such cases, the knowledge base ceases to be a static set of articles: its completeness can be regularly checked based on what people are actually asking.

And this, perhaps, is more useful than endlessly trying to find the perfect article for a question that the base doesn't yet have an answer for.
