Why Signatures Are Hard to Distinguish from the Background

When processing documents automatically, it's necessary to verify that mandatory fields are filled. For a contract or an act, one such check might be the presence of a signature in the designated area. An error in this check could lead to an unsigned document proceeding to the next stage of processing or require the user to resubmit an already signed document.

A signature lacks a consistent form. It can consist of several short strokes, resemble a handwritten surname, or be a flourish with long loops. Its size and position also vary: a signature might occupy a small corner of the field, stretch across its entire width, or extend beyond its boundaries. Even if the signature area is known in advance, it contains a lot of extraneous information.

Separating a signature from this information is not easy. Its strokes can intersect with form lines, printed text, or stamp impressions. A security pattern might contain similar curves and intersections. Often, the background is more contrasting than the signature itself: the model might react to a clearly visible pattern and miss faint strokes over it.

Shooting conditions further complicate the task. When photographing at an angle, image proportions change, blur smooths fine lines, and glare and shadows obscure parts of the signature. After downscaling the fragment to the model's input size, some details become even less discernible. These difficulties also arise in other stages of document processing. We discussed in more detail how our recognition system works with photos and video streams in an article.

Thus, a pre-defined area narrows the search but doesn't remove the main difficulty: distinguishing a fragment with a signature from one without, despite the background and loss of detail. This is precisely the task we address with binary classification.

Problem Statement

We consider the binary classification of a pre-defined document area, or Region of Interest (ROI). The model receives an image fragment that may contain a signature and classifies it into one of two classes: "signature present" or "signature absent."

ROI selection is performed before the classifier runs. For documents with a fixed structure, the field's position can be set in a template—we've discussed this approach on Habr before. In other cases, the area can be determined by document structure analysis algorithms. We do not assume that the fragment is cut precisely along the signature's boundaries; text, form elements, and other noise may remain within it.

At this stage, we are only interested in the presence of a signature. Determining its exact boundaries, isolating individual strokes, and verifying its attribution to a specific person are separate tasks. Therefore, for training the classifier itself, a label for each input fragment is sufficient; pixel-level signature annotation is not required.

Such a classifier can have two types of errors. A False Positive occurs when the model detects a signature in a fragment where none exists, for example, mistaking text or a security pattern for one. A False Negative occurs when a signature is present, but the model fails to detect it.

ResNet18 Model with Spatial Attention

We used a lightweight version of ResNet18 as our base: we reduced the number of channels in the blocks and adapted the network to work with grayscale images of 128x128 pixels. The resulting model has about 3 million parameters, compared to 11.7 million for the standard ResNet18.

We added a spatial attention module to the convolutional part of the network. It calculates weights for different positions in the feature map and uses them to weight features before classification. This allows the model to amplify the contribution of some areas and attenuate others.

Training and Testing Data

For training, we prepared 170,000 image fragments, or patches. The dataset includes three groups:

  • 50,000 patches based on real signatures from Tobacco800. For each of the 705 original signatures, we generated random crops of varying scales and aspect ratios. This simulated inaccuracies in ROI selection: the signature could occupy different portions of the input fragment and be at varying distances from its boundaries.

  • 30,000 synthetic patches with signatures. Signatures from public datasets were binarized and randomly placed on backgrounds of documents, forms, and noise images. This allowed for creating additional combinations of signatures and backgrounds.

  • 90,000 negative examples. This group included empty fields, text blocks, and other fragments without signatures. Such examples are necessary for the model to learn to distinguish signatures from other document elements.

For testing, we used photographs of ID card mockups from the open dataset MIDV2020. Unlike the synthetic training compositions, these images were obtained by photographing physical documents and contain typical distortions: perspective, blur, and uneven lighting.

The attention model's results on the simplified set are higher than on the complex set. This aligns with the hypothesis that background and shooting conditions make classification more difficult.

What Does It Mean to See a Signature?

In a document image, all elements are equal: a form line might be darker than a signature, a stamp larger, and a security pattern more complex in shape. The significance of these elements emerges with the task. To verify the presence of a signature, a few faint strokes might be more important than all other content in the fragment.

Spatial attention allows the model to learn such a selection process. Furthermore, the attention map gives us a reason to scrutinize our own expectations: which features we consider significant, which noise we underestimate, and how closely the experimental conditions resemble those in which the system will operate.

Behind the seemingly simple task of detecting a signature lies a general computer vision question: how to highlight what is significant in an image where nothing is inherently marked as important. In our work, this question takes a concrete form: can the model spot a signature even when the background attracts much more attention?
