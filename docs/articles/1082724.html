How is the model distributed across cards?

After V67, the next major experiment was distributing the model between video cards.

To make it clear why PCIe is needed here at all, let me briefly remind you of the two main stages of LLM operation.

When I send a request to the model, it first needs to read this entire request – this is Prefill.

An AI Agent can simultaneously receive a system instruction, driver source code, kernel logs, results of previous commands, documentation, experiment history, and my new question. All this must first be processed by the model. This stage is called prefill. For a short conversation, it's easy to overlook. For an agent working with large volumes of source code and logs, prefill becomes a very noticeable part of the total time.

The difference between a conditional 250 tok/s and 2000 tok/s is felt with literally every request.

After processing the input, the response generation begins – this is decode.

When they say a model works at a speed of 30 or 70 tokens per second, they usually mean this stage.

Both metrics are important for my server. I need fast generation, and I need fast input because local agents constantly read large amounts of technical information.

With llama.cpp, the model can be distributed across video cards in various ways.

The simplest to understand is layer split.

In this mode, different layers of the model reside on different GPUs. One card computes its part of the model, after which the intermediate result is passed to the next.

Another option is tensor split.

Here, one large operation is distributed across multiple GPUs simultaneously. The computational load is distributed more efficiently. The number of exchanges between video cards increases sharply.

BUT! CMP 90HX does not have NVLink. All this data travels through PCIe. This is where the old mining card began to remind of its origin...

Important disclaimer about the results

All these experiments stretched over time, and the software environment changed slightly during the project.

In individual tests, I used very similar, but still slightly different models.

In terms of size, architecture, and load characteristics, they are close, so the overall picture is well discernible, especially when the change is tens of percent.

However, it would be incorrect to perceive all the values presented below as a laboratory test where strictly one parameter changed.

These results are not guaranteed to match token for token. The scale of the effect is more important to me here.

The video card forgot to include PCIe in the package

The CMP 90HX is physically built on the GA102. A full PCIe x16 is routed on the board. The board knows about x16. The connector knows about x16.

Several dozen small passive components simply decided not to participate...

On the additional transmission lines, decoupling capacitors were missing. In the first article, I had already installed 220 nF and upgraded some cards to x8. After the tensor split experiments, it became clear that it was time to finish what I started.

I soldered the remaining lines.

After this, each card received a full PCIe x16.

In terms of useful throughput, PCIe Gen1 provides approximately:

Gen1 x4 - 1 GB/s

Gen1 x8 - 2 GB/s

Gen1 x16 - 4 GB/s

One PCIe Gen1 lane operates at 2.5 GT/s. But due to 8b/10b encoding, the useful throughput is about 250 MB/s per lane in each direction.

This is where the physical contacts finally ran out.

Why can't you just write "Gen2"?

The PCIe controller inside the GPU has several levels of control.

What the processor sees is only the external part. Inside the GPU, there are its own XVE registers, privilege masks, service processors, and protected areas. And here, what I did in the second article proved very useful. V67 was already a working key into the privileged context of the GPU.

In the second article, it opened FEAT_OVR_PLM and removed computational limitations. I already understood V67. I needed to find the right door.

A small note – why did I even go into this and not use someone else's method completely – someone else's methods didn't work :). No, seriously, I spent two days honestly trying to fix build errors, register write errors, and tons of other things... Nothing I found online simply worked. No options.

Silicon began to tinker with its own driver

The base was NVIDIA Open Kernel Module 610.43.03.

The research used previous compute unlock and rejoin16 developments, and then the agent experimented with the initialization order, internal PCIe registers, and lane retraining.

It quickly became clear that writing to the PCIe register alone is not enough. The state of the GPU and the order of operations matter.

The working sequence ultimately turned out to be approximately as follows: first, the standard NVIDIA 610.43.03 initializes the GPU normally. After that, control is transferred to the modified module, through V67/fwsec the necessary privilege masks are opened, FLR and re-initialization are performed, and then PCIe retrain.

Unfortunately, NVIDIA did not leave me a red ENABLE_GEN2 = 1 button. I had to assemble the sequence piece by piece.

Another interesting effect appeared during startup.

If the modified module is loaded immediately after a cold boot, the card may crash on RmInitAdapter.

Therefore, the CMP first goes through normal initialization with the NVIDIA driver. After that, the already running card is transferred to the modified module. In essence, this is simply transferring an already normally initialized GPU to a patched driver.

Out of the entire mass of registers, two areas ultimately proved particularly important.

The first:

0×823800

This is the FEAT/PLM part of the mechanism, which I already encountered when removing computational limitations.

Through V67/fwsec, the mask is opened to:

0xffffffff

The second:

0×088fe8

It relates to XVE and the internal protection of the PCIe part of the GPU.

It is also set to:

0xffffffff

Full speed selectors are also used in the procedure:

ss0 = 0×88888888

ss1 = 0×00000008

After this, Function Level Reset is performed, which can be thought of as a local reboot of the PCIe device.

The GPU is reset, re-initializes, and then the PCIe lane trains again.

And then PCIe became Gen2

The physical lane indeed trained to Gen2.

Checking with real data transfer

The register readings are great.

I still wanted to check the data transfer.

A CUDA DMA bandwidth test was run with a pinned memory region.

Result:

RAM -> GPU: 6.33 GB/s

GPU -> RAM: 6.40 GB/s

And now for the best part.

PCIe Gen1 x16 physically provides about 4 GB/s of useful bandwidth. It's impossible to push 6.4 GB/s through it. PCIe Gen2 x16 provides about 8 GB/s.

The actual 6.3–6.4 GB/s looks completely normal, considering the protocol overhead and DMA implementation.

Result of all the torment and future plans

After all the modifications made, I am gradually removing limitations one by one.

Now there is compute unlock, full PCIe x16, PCIe Gen2, and cooling with a huge margin.

The memory on the cards runs at about 9500 MHz. I don't want to squeeze a few more percent out of GDDR6X at any cost for now. It's perfectly capable of turning electricity into heat without my help.

The core is much more interesting.

Under a sustained LLM load, I saw up to about 200 W per card with an available limit of around 250 W. This means I'm not even hitting the power limit yet. The thermal margin after the air conditioner is also huge. This results in a situation quite rare for old mining hardware: temperature doesn't interfere, power limit doesn't interfere yet, PCIe is gradually ceasing to interfere. I can calmly investigate the dependence of performance on GPU frequency.

During prefill, the model effectively loads the compute units with large matrix operations, so additional core frequency can potentially yield a noticeable result.

Decode of large dense models often depends more on memory bandwidth, so the effect there might be more modest.

And this will also be a useful result.
