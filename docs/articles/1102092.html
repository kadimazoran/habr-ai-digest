The AI revolution has already changed many professions, and DevOps engineers are no exception. Neural networks can excellently debug issues with crashed Kubernetes pods, parse huge volumes of logs, correlating all events literally second by second. They perfectly know the syntax and use cases of all CLI utilities and understand how the Linux kernel works at the source code level. Competing with them in debugging and diagnostics capabilities is simply useless; I've tried.

Yes, they still write Ansible code terribly, which is then impossible to reuse anywhere else, but there's a lot of routine in the work of sysadmins, DevOps, and SRE that we do for the majority of our time, and it's very easily automated, freeing up a lot of time.

The Sun Shines Brighter, and the Grass is Greener

If 20 years ago an administrator had only a few servers at their disposal, then virtualization came into wide use, followed by containerization, and for the last decade, additional layers of abstraction in the form of clouds and orchestrators like Kubernetes or once Swarm (who still remembers it?) have been heavily built on top of all this. The explosive growth of digitalization in society has been supported by a constantly developing technological base.

For the end-user, this makes things easier, as their simplest landing page can be deployed with one click in a convenient web interface of a cloud provider, and inside there's an immense S3 replicated across different locations, CDNs for caching, tons of hardware under the hood, and an endless release cycle to update this machinery, deliver critical patches, back it up, and restore it in case of failures.

This colossal expansion of the technology stack led to an exponential growth in specializations. Previously, a single administrator, whom ordinary users were so afraid to talk to, could replace printer cartridges, maintain server hardware, assist development with load testing and deployment, and much more. I myself worked in such a mode at the beginning of my career. And then came DevOps, SRE, build engineers, monitoring specialists, and many others.

Sooner or later, a reverse process of collapsing all this diversity had to begin, because now even DevOps and SRE can communicate with each other in different languages. And it has started.

I'm Getting Dumber

A little over a year ago, I subscribed to Cursor and tried to complete a task using only its capabilities. I didn't intervene myself and fundamentally didn't do anything manually. I wanted to understand what artificial intelligence was capable of.

The first experience was a failure; the neural network couldn't debug a medium-complexity problem. Commands were constantly failing, and after another attempt, it imperturbably stated: "Everything is ready." To my remark about the lack of results, it replied: "You are absolutely right, the task was not completed, and here's why..."

Just a year ago, vibe coding was called vibe coding and was something unserious, but then agent-based development arrived, and it changed the world of technology.

Claude Desktop became my single interface for everything. Through it, I review merge requests in Gitlab and ask colleagues for responses there; through it, I make code changes and release versions; with its help, I debug problems on servers and grep logs. I only use the IDE for syntax highlighting.

Sometimes I get the idea to ask the neural network to explain which commands it used for debugging, and it turns out to be very interesting and informative. However, after reading a couple of commands, I collapse them without even trying to run them in my local environment.

I catch myself thinking that as an engineer, I've become so lazy that there are no limits to it. The degree of my own degradation feels like something unstoppable and elemental, which cannot be resisted.

Sometimes, from the depths of my subconscious, a sense of anxiety reaches me. It shows me a world without this neural network dopamine and sober me up for a little while. Then I fall back into the bliss of madness. All this makes me think about what the world and our professions will look like in even 3 or 5 years. What an amazing discovery that will be.

I'm lucky; I'm an old-school engineer and have spent most of my professional career working only with my own hands and mind. I started as an IT support technician, going to install Windows from a bunch of DVD-RWs. Then I was a regular sysadmin. For the last 10 years, I've been automating development processes and reducing the total cost of ownership for solutions.

I'm not afraid of returning to manual work, but I've realized that I don't want to. Engineering has always been my hobby, and I won't give it up, but I no longer want to perform my daily work the old way.

Will There Be Downsides?

Neural networks have given engineers a unique opportunity to delegate their tasks to another tool. This was impossible before. Various tools, programs, and other services don't count; they only automated the process and simplified it, but the final decision was always made by the engineer. Now, the decision can be made by AI based on your requests and permission levels.

During my university studies, old-school professionals would laugh at my generation for not even seeing punch cards and not working with soldering irons. Perhaps because of this, we were dumber in their eyes, but technological progress has still been moving forward for decades, meaning that not having experience with punch cards clearly didn't become our weakness. Just as accountants didn't become dumber because they switched from paper and manual calculation of statements with a calculator to 1C. Automation allowed them to process a larger volume in a shorter time and with better quality.

I think a similar development awaits the situation with neural networks; it's just that their niche hasn't fully settled yet, as their development has been too rapid. Technological progress has always created abstractions over our work, and we shouldn't resist this process; it's better to lead it.

But let's take a critical look: what has really changed? A lot has changed: today, even people with humanities backgrounds can vibe-code their own applications without delving into technical details. At work, I often see economists, HR staff, and accountants bringing us quite viable prototypes of their own applications. They understand better than others how to automate their own work and take action.

I have no illusions and understand perfectly well that the quality of such creations is not always high, and that's putting it mildly. With the development of models, it is still growing insignificantly, but I urge you not to dwell on this. This trend has given us engineers a completely unexpected advantage: people in these professions have become interested in the technical side of developing their own applications. Now, when I publish another post about the development of my own B2B SaaS on my Telegram channel, I understand that it might be interesting not only to my colleagues but also to people from various specializations who were very far from this topic until recently.

At one time, the market's demand for rapid code delivery greatly popularized the sysadmin specialty, transforming them from unkempt geeks in stained sweaters into fashionable, well-paid, and in-demand DevOps engineers. I expect a similar outcome from the neural network trend.
