Therefore, I was interested in looking at the summit from different angles and understanding how the startup and venture ecosystem is structured today. The Moscow Startup Summit took place on September 29-30 at SberCity, in the "School 21" campus. For two days, startup founders, investors, representatives of corporations, and government agencies gathered here—four categories of participants were indicated on the badges to make networking easier. The scale is impressive: over 4500 participants from different countries, 150 experts, more than 40 discussions in the business program, and 100 startups at the exhibition. I walked through the key activities and sessions, and in this article, I will tell you what I saw.

It was my first time at SberCity. Construction is in full swing all around—dozens of buildings are under construction simultaneously. Against this backdrop, the "School 21" building resembles a ship in a sea of change. Inside, the main stage with a three-story amphitheater was set up in the central atrium (the building has seven floors in total), and three other venues were operating in parallel: a foresight studio, an expert laboratory, and a project workshop.

I arrived just in time for the opening. Sergey Mezentsev, also known as Sergey Meza, warmed up the audience—a blogger who hosted the reality show "Startup Battle" on STS a couple of years ago, where our team took first place.

Opening: Technology as a Habit

The main event of the first day was a dialogue between German Gref and Sergey Sobyanin on how technologies are transforming from a news novelty into a daily habit. Gref started with statistics: by the beginning of 2027, 75% of corporate loans and 100% of retail loans at Sber will be issued automatically using AI. Currently, a corporate loan is approved in seven minutes.

"I once sincerely didn't understand why I needed to go to a branch, stand in line, and rewrite the bank identification code and correspondent account number. This bewilderment led to the linking of phone numbers with bank accounts—a simple innovation that changed the market."

German Gref, President and Chairman of the Management Board of Sberbank

They also discussed hardware: the main reason for the lag of domestic neural networks behind global giants is the severe deficit and high cost of computing power. According to Gref's estimate, we are currently about six months behind China.

Sergey Sobyanin, in turn, reminded about the cost of mistakes:

"Does a person have the right to make a mistake? Yes. But artificial intelligence today does not have the right to make a mistake—the final signature in medicine, for example, should always be made by a doctor."

Sergey Sobyanin, Mayor of Moscow

In this conversation, another point seemed important to me: technological transformation doesn't start with the technology itself. It requires an environment into which it can be integrated, and it is startups that become this environment.

Five Industries Changing Before Our Eyes

This year's key idea of the event was to show how startups can transform five key industries: Agrotech & Foodtech, Cretech & Edtech, Medtech & Biotech, Robotics & Devices, and Buildtech & Urbantech. Futurist Rohit Talwar predicted the development of these areas over the next 10 years at the foresight session:

  1. Robots and Devices. The market expects a transition from mechanical toys to utilitarian machines—precision manipulators, construction robots, and autonomous inspection drones. For example, in China, robots already cost less than $1000, and the government supports micro-companies of one person.

  2. Agrotech. Replacing intuition with algorithms: drones, soil analyzers, AI weather forecasts, and bioengineering. Later, vertical autonomous farms and genetic modification of crops for a changing climate.

  3. Medtech. Moving from accelerating molecule development to fully autonomous surgery and personalized medicine tailored to a specific patient's genome.

  4. Edtech. Rethinking the role of the teacher. AI takes over routine tasks and endless patience in explaining material. The ISA (Income Share Agreements) model is being introduced—free education with a percentage of future salary paid back.

  5. Buildtech. Modular construction, self-healing concrete, kinetic energy sensors in floors, and smart city systems that automatically reroute traffic in case of accidents.

Robots and Devices

At the "Million Robots: Betting on Robot Stack" session, the conversation shifted from futurism to a more pragmatic and important topic—economics. It's difficult for private investors to invest in "bare metal": competing with Chinese manufacturers on price is almost impossible, and the profit margin on the equipment itself is low. Therefore, participants consider the "robot as a service" model more promising than selling hardware. This primarily applies to cleaning, warehouse logistics, and delivery.

In other words, the customer wants to buy a result. For example, in a warehouse, this could be faster order fulfillment, fewer errors, and a clear cost of operation. That's why Yandex Robotics is developing a subscription model with a performance guarantee. And the most significant economic effect, according to participants, comes from the robotization of order picking. But here, a vicious cycle arises: for a robot to work effectively, businesses need to reconfigure processes, prepare data, and infrastructure. And to invest in this reconfiguration, one wants to see the economic effect first.

Participants are waiting for their "GPT moment" for robotics: a situation where a robot can learn new tasks without lengthy and expensive retraining. A separate direction here is the Robot Stack, i.e., the software infrastructure that will allow controlling different robots and adding new skills to them. MTS Web Services, for example, is developing a vendor-independent physical AI platform with a skills marketplace, and Sber, together with X5, is testing robots in retail stores.

Moreover, robots are already moving beyond warehouses. For instance, since the end of 2023, autonomous ferries have been operating on the Ust-Luga—Baltiysk route. Citronix KT was responsible for equipping them with autonomous navigation systems. Today, this developer of solutions for the shipping industry is also developing a line of civilian unmanned boats for logistics, monitoring, and hydrography.

Agrotech and the Field as a Data Source

From robotics, I moved to agrotech—here, they discussed problems that, at first glance, sound un-startup-like: how to feed a growing population without depleting resources (land, water, and fertilizers).

About 1.7 billion hectares have already undergone degradation. The climate is becoming less predictable, and agriculture remains an industry with a long decision-making cycle. A mistake with the harvest is not a bug that can be fixed in the next release.

Therefore, agrotech is increasingly turning the field into a data source. Today, satellites and remote sensing help to see that different parts of the same field are in different conditions. Sensors collect information about the state of plants, AI correlates it with weather, diseases, and pests, and helps determine where water, fertilizers, or treatments are needed.

For me, agrotech was a good reminder that technology does not exist in a vacuum. What works in one country may not work in another: climate, regulations, economics, and the farms themselves differ. Therefore, the principle I heard at the summit again and again is particularly important here: it is not enough for a startup to demonstrate technology—the result is what matters.

Medicine, or Where AI Must Learn to Work with Humans

In medtech, the cost of error is even higher. At the session "New Approach in Medicine: How the Role of Data, Doctor, and Patient is Changing with AI Implementation," they discussed where neural networks can replace doctors without risks to patients.

One obvious scenario is to take over routine tasks and data analysis. For example, about 80 million medical check-ups are conducted annually in Russia, and part of this information can already be processed automatically: AI can identify deviations, sort patients by risk level, and help the doctor determine who needs additional attention. Priorities are set according to the traffic light principle: green—monitor, yellow—check more carefully, red—refer further. However, the final decision remains with the doctor.

Telesurgery is no longer just a laboratory experiment: by mid-2026, about two thousand such operations had been performed. The "Kurikan" project is preparing the first cross-border robotic surgery for a child on the Shanghai-Irkutsk route with a signal delay of up to 120 ms. But even if a robot is technically capable of performing surgery, it doesn't mean the technology will be in every clinic tomorrow.

At another session, "Engineering Breakthroughs in Biomedicine," they discussed the path from scientific hypothesis to product. And here, the "valley of death" familiar to startup founders appears: a prototype already exists, but mass production is still far off. It requires registration, proof of reproducibility, and finding materials and equipment. For example, industrial silicone for biocompatible devices is still difficult to produce in Russia, and there are also limitations with electronic components.

Cretech and Edtech: More Content, Less Attention

In the next zone, the conversation was about content—something familiar to every Habr author and reader. The session was titled "Technologies Relaunch Media: How Major Market Companies Are Changing." In short, AI has made content production much cheaper and faster. But there's a problem: users haven't gained extra hours in the day.

This leads to an interesting race: the easier content production becomes, the harder it is to get people to choose it. And here, another deficit emerges—trust. Users react negatively to low-quality AI content and attempts to pass it off as genuine. Platforms are learning to recognize such content and reduce its distribution.

Buildtech: How to Build a Building Digitally

Finally, I went to explore the construction and urban technology part. At the "Building as a Digital System: How Big Data and AI Are Used in Construction" session, they discussed making a digital building model a system that accompanies the object from the initial sketch to operation.

Architects can use AI to analyze initial data. BIM models collect information about the object. Computer vision monitors safety and equipment downtime. Laser scanning combined with machine vision compares the actual construction status with the 3D model with millimeter accuracy.

But another idea caught my attention more—the "digital square meter." "Hals-Development" is already incorporating robotization at the design stage. For example, they plan for technical rooms, the necessary corridor width, and the integration of robots with elevators. The company has created a unified building management center and is developing a platform that will allow robots to interact with each other.

In other words, the building of the future is designed not only for people. It is pre-planned to accommodate robots, sensors, data, and control systems. And here again, the same question arises as in other industries: how ready is the environment around the technologies?

Currently, the digital maturity of the construction industry remains low—participants estimated it at about 20% of companies in Moscow and about 5% in some regions. There are problems with data, employee resistance, model hallucinations, and responsibility for AI decisions.

Therefore, the autonomous city remains more of a future stage. The immediate goal is more prosaic: to integrate the entire building into a single digital system and learn to manage it based on data.

What the Five Industries Have in Common

After exploring the five areas, I realized I had stopped perceiving them as separate markets. Robotics is entering agriculture and construction. AI works with medical data, helps agronomists, and simultaneously generates content. Computer vision monitors patients and construction workers. Data is becoming a common infrastructure for completely different industries.

At the summit, all this was happening literally on adjacent floors: here, they discussed what would happen in ten years, and a few meters away, founders were already trying to convince investors that their product works today.

As someone who once stood on the other side of the pitch stage, I found it particularly interesting to observe these conversations.

The Smarter the Machines, the More Important the Human

The summit was given a special flavor by the performances of invited stars. Against the backdrop of discussions about AI, they unexpectedly spoke about the same thing—the limits of technology and what neural networks still cannot do for us.

For example, Robin Sharma, author of "The Monk Who Sold His Ferrari" and "The 5 AM Club," dedicated his speech to the human side of successful business.

Robin coined the acronym HUMAN:

  • H (Human) - Humility and creating real value.

  • U (Unkillable) - Resilience to chaos.

  • M (Mastery) - Striving for unparalleled quality.

  • A (Awake) - Emotional intelligence.

  • N (Nuts) - Healthy obsession with one's idea.

Sharma advised getting rid of unnecessary communications, building a strong operational team, regularly disconnecting from technology, and not turning work into a round-the-clock process. Even his advice about two phones—a smartphone for work and a simple phone for family and the core team—sounded like an attempt to give people back control over their own attention.

Physicist Carlo Rovelli spoke about another human advantage—the ability to doubt the obvious. As an example, he cited Anaximander, who, back in Ancient Greece, suggested that the Earth is not the center around which the sky revolves, but a stone suspended in space.

The idea came from a simple observation: the sun and moon disappear on one side and appear on the other. Similarly, Einstein arrived at the theory of relativity through contradictions between existing theories. And he got some ideas from a completely different field—working in a patent office and dealing with the tasks of synchronizing railway clocks.

French director Luc Besson joined the summit online—he spoke with Russian director and screenwriter Andrey Zolotov about AI in cinema. According to him, neural networks already allow for a significant reduction in the time and cost of film production, but he still writes scripts by hand. "A neural network feeds on all the information in the world, and I feed on 64 years of memories," Besson concluded.

Three Pitches on the Way to Coffee

Perhaps the most interesting things happened between the speeches: participants met, exchanged contacts, and discussed projects on the spot.

The organizers (Sber and the Moscow Government) had well-thought-out infrastructure for such meetings. In the app, you could find the right participant, exchange contacts, and arrange a meeting. Offline, there were Tech Lounges, a startup exhibition, cafes, and dozens of small zones with armchairs, poufs, and tables for this purpose. In the Tech Lounge, for example, speed dating took place: startup founders occupied a table with a specific number, and at the appointed time, an expert would approach them. After the meeting, you could move on to the next acquaintance.

You didn't even need to go anywhere for networking. On the first day, three times people approached me and pitched their startups: at the coffee point, outside by the fountains, and in the Tech Lounge. It wasn't hard to find me: participants had badges of different colors. Startup founders had bright green "Startup" badges, mine was "Corporation" in sea blue. Blue "Investor" and purple "Government" badges were also easily recognizable from afar.

As a result, the badge practically served as a business card: even before meeting, it was clear who you were talking to and why they came to the summit.

The startup exhibition became a separate point of attraction—it was almost constantly crowded. There were also Sber zones with their own projects: "School 21," SberUniversity, SberCity with a VR tour, and SberHealth with basic health parameter determination by face. Nearby were the stands of Sber's partners, some of which I never got to visit.

Demo Days: A Conveyor Belt from Idea to Market

There were several demo days at the summit where startups could get expertise, pilots, or investments. I'll start with Sber500.


In 2026, the program received over 1800 applications from 52 countries, and 25 projects from four areas reached the finals: robots and devices, agrotech and foodtech, buildtech and urbantech, medtech and biotech. Throughout the history of Sber500, finalists have attracted over five billion rubles and signed more than 11,000 pilots and contracts.

Among the projects were the robot agronomist Agronic, which detects plant diseases in greenhouses; MapLab, which reduces territory planning from three months to three minutes; the adaptive robotic manipulator SmartPicks; and the Indian XpressCBC—express blood analysis in 10 minutes. After the pitches, investors announced their readiness to discuss investments, Sber offered pilot platforms, and Cloud.ru provided GigaChat tokens to the finalists.

The Youth Demo Day showed the same mechanics at an earlier stage. Out of over 2000 applications, teams with projects ranging from the fire-fighting robot Freze to a platform for medical communication with foreign patients reached the selection. Here, the main focus was not only on the technology but also on the founder themselves. The youngest participant was only 13 years old.

At the "Know Our Own" investment demo day, projects were directly seeking funding. Companies had three minutes to explain to the expert council why they are worth investing in. Among them were PeptiGen with AI peptide design, Garpix Load System with AI for optimal cargo loading, Ice+ with immersion cooling for data centers, and HiveTrace for protecting AI agents from unauthorized actions.

After the presentations, investors announced specific amounts of potential investments: for example, 50, 60, 90, 100, and 150 million rubles were discussed for individual projects. In total, during the preparation and execution of the event, the presented projects were offered 763 million rubles.

Two Days Inside the Startup Ecosystem

When the summit ended, there was a feeling that in two days, I had only seen a part of what was happening. And perhaps this is the main indicator of its scale: something was constantly happening here—someone was pitching a startup, others were looking for an investor or discussing a pilot, and still others were deciding how to implement a new technology into their business.

I myself managed to communicate with several startup founders and look at the ecosystem not just as an observer. Therefore, I left with a very specific thought: next time, I want to return here with my own project—or at least with a different colored badge.
