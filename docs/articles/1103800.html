In my previous article, I talked about how I conducted 35+ in-depth interviews almost single-handedly (and with the help of AI). Today, I'll discuss a task that usually sits in the backlog for years for marketing departments without a budget (and ours was, by the way): website redesign. 22 pages, five new case studies, one working day of pure time, and not a single designer was harmed.

Caution: this is marketing again. There will be code, about 6,000 lines of it, but I didn't write it. There will be a process that can be repeated, and a list of places where everything falls apart without a human.

TL;DR

Three things worth reading on:

  1. AI is not a designer, but very fast hands. It writes code, does layout, checks pages on all screens itself, and suggests options. Taste, structure, and the final decision remain with the human. Without this, you get a neat but soulless website.

  2. The main saving is not on layout, but on iterations. An edit like "this line is overlapping that one" takes a minute, not a day of correspondence with a contractor. Therefore, you can afford many more edits – until the tokens run out :)

  3. AI is excellent at extracting facts, but doesn't know what can be published from them. Who did the project, what's under NDA, which figure in a case study cannot be left without confirmation – this is the boundary drawn by a human.

1. Where This Came From

Let me clarify upfront: our website doesn't sell anything and isn't supposed to. In our business segment, a website is more of a showcase and a place to direct clients to see case studies or for job seekers to check information about vacancies. Therefore, for several years, our website was dynamic: case studies, articles, good texts. But the service pages looked like technical documentation: long lists, identical blocks, nothing for the eye to latch onto. Internally, there were good projects and figures, but externally, it was "well, mediocre." For a company that sells expertise, this is frustrating.

The classic path is well-known: brief for the designer, mockups, revisions, more revisions, development. A month or two and a budget. The task conditions, to understand the resources involved:

  • An IT company with ~60 specialists, outstaffing, Russian market;

  • marketing – I'm the only one, and redesign is not my only task, but one of them;

  • budget for a designer and development – zero;

  • deadlines – "when you get it done.".

The second point explains the timeline. Pure time spent on design was one working day, but it stretched to about four – the redesign was done in pieces between all other tasks.

2. Step 0. Framework Before the First Line of Code

I started with one page – "Outstaffing Developers." I set the task in a human way: make it more pleasant and readable, and use our articles and case studies as a model – they are easy to read.

And immediately I set three rules that I didn't change until the end:

  • Brand style remains. Black background, green #80d207, Oswald and Roboto fonts. We are not rebranding.

  • Menu and footer – as is. Items, order, dropdown sub-sections. A person accustomed to the site should not get lost in it.

  • Texts are not touched. We change the presentation, not the content.

The third rule turned out to be the most useful. Texts are taken from the CMS as they are, the design is assembled according to the template – and one successful page immediately turns into ten.

Lesson: limitations are set before starting, not during. AI is eager to "improve" everything it can reach. The clearer the framework, the less time is spent on "revert it back.".

3. Step 1. Three Versions of One Page

The first version turned out neat and boring. The second – "modernized," but also without character. We made the third after I showed the AI a selection of web trends for 2026. Large kinetic headings, "glass" cards, a light spot following the cursor, and neat animation on scroll appeared.

I approved the third version, so from then on, the entire site was done in its style.

Lesson: don't try to describe the ideal in words right away. Personally, it's often easier for me to see three options and choose than to formulate my request in detail. With a contractor, each new mockup costs money and a week. With AI, the second and third options cost almost nothing – and this changes the logic of work itself: you can afford to choose.

4. Step 2. How It Looks in Practice: Nitpicking

Most of the work is not "make me a website," but short, nitpicky edits. Real phrases from the correspondence:

  • "Too flashy – make it more subdued"

  • "I didn't ask to change this block, revert it"

  • "No stairs" – when the cards lined up like steps

  • "Remove the turquoise, only corporate green"

  • "The letters in the heading are overlapping"

  • "Lower this part here so it doesn't cover the photo"

Some edits turned out to be more interesting than they looked.

Checklist that promised results. One blog post had an interactive checklist with checkboxes and a scale. It looked impressive. But such an interface implies that after the checkboxes, there will be a "Result" button and some output. There was no output. We replaced the interactive element with a memo that can be saved to PDF or copied.

Accreditation that cannot be verified. On the "Career" page, I wanted to link to our accreditation in the Ministry of Digital Development registry. It turned out that the registry on Gosuslugi (State Services portal) does not accept requests from a link, and searching by company name shows "no accreditation." Imagine the candidate's face who decided to check us. Solution: a button that copies the TIN before proceeding: the person just needs to paste it into the search.

Recommendation that should have been checked. GEO contractors advised supplementing the outstaffing page with banking case studies, industries, specialist deployment times, and replacement conditions. I asked AI to check what was already on the page. It turned out that almost nothing was. Incidentally, a contradiction was found that we hadn't noticed ourselves: in one place "1-3 days," in another "several days," but in reality, the selection takes one day.

And here I must admit: over time, you stop reading your own website. A contradiction in a key sales promise lived right before our eyes, and I didn't find it. Lesson: running your own website through AI for contradictions is cheaper than external auditing and finds what your eyes have long become accustomed to.

5. Step 3. Case Studies from Scans of Technical Specifications

A separate story is the case studies for the industry page on access control systems. I had technical specifications of 55 and 41 pages, some of them scans with stamps. AI read them from the page images and compiled draft case studies.

Then my work was needed again:

  • Merged duplicates. Two future case studies turned out to be one product in two generations – we combined them into one story.

  • Verified roles. Where we did the entire project, and where only the design. We naturally cannot write "developed" if we didn't do the development.

  • Anonymized clients. Removed names and details that would identify the client.

Lesson: AI is good at extracting facts from documents, but it doesn't know what can be published from them. This boundary is drawn by a human.

6. What I Delegated to the Neural Network – and Where the Boundary Lies

What I Gave to AI

Almost everything mechanical:

  • code and layout – about 6,000 lines, none of mine;

  • checking each edit: AI itself opened the page in a browser at widths from a phone to a large monitor and checked if anything was sticking out – it made over 120 screenshots in the process;

  • reading scans and compiling draft case studies;

  • cross-referencing the page with external recommendations and searching for contradictions.

Regarding checking on screens – separately. This is usually the most tedious part of the job, and it's what causes the most correspondence with the contractor. Here, it simply disappeared from my day.

The Boundary

But, of course, there are things that cannot be delegated:

  • Taste. AI will offer ten neat options, but only a human can choose the one that is "ours.".

  • Stop words. Without control, "honestly," and other marketing AI noise can creep into the texts.

  • Facts. AI doesn't seem to invent figures in case studies, but it also doesn't know which figures are missing. Yes, yes: metrics still have to be begged from project managers.

  • Politics. NDA, partner's permission for a logo, what can be published about a client – these are still human decisions.

Didn't give. The work is not finished – we always have something to edit, not to mention that we constantly publish new articles and case studies. It's too early to talk about how visitor behavior has changed – if there are non-trivial results, I will definitely write about them.

At the same time, it's important to maintain common sense: AI does not replace a designer when rebranding or new identity is needed; working within an existing style is much easier than creating a style from scratch. Conclusion

One working day, 22 pages, zero budget for design – and yet, the main thing for me is not the speed.
