Interviews are increasingly becoming a vector for cyberattacks. Attackers use deepfakes to impersonate real candidates and recruiters, or spread malware disguised as test assignments – sending "harmless" code or a dependency package that, when run on the victim's device, installs malicious software. Today, we at Beeline Cloud decided to discuss the methods used by fake applicants and recruiters, how to recognize an attacker "on the other side of the resume," and what AI has to do with it.

Interviews as an Attack Vector

Companies have always poached employees from each other to gain access to competitors' practices and even confidential data. For example, back in 2000, Intel sued Broadcom, claiming that the latter systematically recruited its engineers to extract trade secrets. Similar lawsuits against Broadcom were previously filed by industrial automation supplier Rockwell and research company Sarnoff. At the time, the disputes led nowhere, but generally, fake interviews, organized solely to gather information about a candidate's employer, are included in standard lists of industrial espionage methods – alongside wiretapping and bribery.

However, the rules are changing today, and an interview is becoming not just an intelligence tool, but a vector for a full-fledged cyberattack – primarily targeting job seekers themselves. Microsoft has detailed the mechanics of such attacks. First, attackers gain trust by posing as company representatives, and then they offer to complete a test assignment. Typically, this involves checking or fixing code in a repository on GitHub, GitLab, or Bitbucket. Infection occurs through npm packages or Visual Studio Code functions: backdoors are installed in the system that steal cryptographic keys, passwords, and other sensitive data. The malware can take screenshots, monitor clipboard content, and perform other checks.

In other words, attackers gain a foothold in the system and obtain remote control over it. In the past, the notorious Lazarus hacking group conducted similar campaigns. It sent fake job offers to targets in the chemical industry and IT companies, which hid Trojans. Moreover, there is a separate group specializing specifically in attacks through fake interviews. Its name speaks for itself – Contagious Interview.

Here's a private case example – this summer, a Python developer from the Smello project shared a story about how he almost fell for the attackers' trick. He was contacted by a small crypto startup with an offer for a lead engineer position. As a test assignment, he was asked to study the code in the repository and find a problem in it. To do this, he had to clone the repository to his machine and run the command npm install. The specialist studied the code and found that package.json contained a prepare script that npm automatically ran after installing dependencies. This, in turn, loaded a file disguised as test code with a backdoor. It pieced together the address of an external server, connected to it, and gave hackers remote access to the computer.

Fortunately, the attackers did not succeed in their plan that time, although the author of the material was likely not their only target. However, the cybersecurity threat can also be on the "other side of the resume" – not only IT specialists willing to consider a job offer suffer from hacker attacks, but also recruiters in companies.

And AI is to Blame Again

AI systems are very often used for attacks on recruiters – including for creating deepfakes during interviews. In some cases, these are relatively harmless experiments: for example, a more qualified specialist tries to impersonate a friend to pass an interview on their behalf and make a good impression. In this case, the company risks financial losses if it hires such a candidate and they cannot cope with their duties. But there are much more serious scenarios where a real identity is intentionally hidden for further industrial espionage – in this case, not only the payroll fund can suffer.

According to a Gartner report, by 2028, one in four job applicants at an interview may turn out to be fake. As Gartner's senior researcher notes, it is becoming increasingly difficult for employers to assess not only the real skills of candidates but, in some cases, their identity: "Fraudulent schemes create cybersecurity risks for companies that can be far more serious than the consequences of a typical bad hire.".

Stories with fake candidates are more common than they might seem at first glance. Information security specialist David Mochalov recounted that in just one month, he conducted interviews with developers twice, who turned out to be very convincing deepfakes. One of the candidates had an active LinkedIn profile, and even information about their higher education in Serbia could be verified.

However, the engineer was then alerted by a strong Asian accent (the company was looking for a specialist from Poland), and the deception was finally confirmed by visual artifacts during the video call. According to David Mochalov, if "candidates" almost passed the selection by a specialist who professionally deals with cybersecurity and knows what such deepfakes look like, then an ordinary hiring manager has even fewer chances to notice the substitution. And this means that somewhere this trick has already worked, it's just that no one has written a post about it.

Deepfake, I Know You!

Of course, you can protect yourself from interviews with hackers and fake candidates. A basic tip for job seekers is to use an isolated environment and run any code from test assignments only on a virtual machine. Microsoft recommends another simple but important habit: before running scripts, installing dependencies, and completing a test assignment, manually review the content of the repository sent by the "recruiter.".

As for companies, one of the first lines of defense can be to check the candidate's profile itself. For example, Socure, a company specializing in identity verification, identified several characteristic signs of fake applicants: a recently created email address, a phone number not corresponding to the stated location, and education that cannot be verified. The company also noticed an interesting effect: it is enough to warn the candidate about an upcoming document check – and fake applicants often disappear on their own.

In addition, recruiters should be trained to recognize deepfakes by visual and technical signs: desynchronization of image and sound, unnatural eye, face, or head movements. At New York University, a set of rules called GOTCHA was developed for this purpose. The idea is quite simple: during the interview, the candidate is asked to perform certain movements in front of the camera – for example, turn their head, cover part of their face with their hand, or put on sunglasses. In this case, it becomes more difficult for the AI system responsible for creating the "artificial face" to generate an image. By the way, it was these techniques that David Mochalov used when identifying "fake faces" among those applying for a job at his company. Tests show that GOTCHA helps detect deepfakes in almost 89% of cases.

There are also more exotic ways to detect deepfakes. Researchers at Clarkson University in New York have proposed a gaze detection method. During a conversation, a person constantly uses their gaze as a kind of non-verbal communication channel, and their eye movements are far from random. The authors suggested that modern deepfakes poorly reproduce these subtle patterns, so a neural network can be trained to distinguish a live interlocutor from a fake one. In experiments with 47 volunteers, this approach demonstrated an accuracy of over 80%.

Finally, specialists from the University at Buffalo have proposed using a "probe pattern" for deepfake detection. A bright geometric shape appears on the recruiter's screen for a short time. The human cornea acts as a mirror, so the image should be reflected in the eyes of a real person and captured by their camera. It is significantly more difficult for a deepfake to reproduce this effect: the generator usually does not model the surrounding lighting in real-time, so the reflection differs from what is expected.

The method was tested on Zoom call recordings of two volunteers (deepfakes were created using Avatarify and DeepFaceLive), and in all tested cases, the fake revealed itself: real people had a sufficiently confident reflection of the probe shape, while deepfakes did not. Although it should be noted here that a sample size of two people is still too small to talk about any statistical significance. This experiment is more of a demonstration of the principle than a ready-made system for production implementation.

The fact that information security specialists have a serious adversary – the rapid development of deepfakes themselves – adds fuel to the fire. The Unit 42 research group notes that current fakes still have enough limitations to be detected. But the technology is rapidly evolving, and detection methods will become less effective.

There will likely be no universal detector here. Organizations need "layered protection": enhanced identity verification procedures, technical control measures, document checks, and several independent stages of communication with the candidate. Not to mention the good old classic – returning to in-person interviews. Cisco, McKinsey, and Google, for example, are again using them as part of their selection process. Overall, it turns out to be an interesting situation: for many years, the "live meeting" aspect was diligently removed from hiring – and now it has to be brought back, and it turns out to be cheaper than hiring a person who does not exist.
