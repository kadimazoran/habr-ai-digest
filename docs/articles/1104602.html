When we first looked at automatic evaluations of AI reports for work meetings (on a five-point scale), it turned out that 45% of them received a C or lower. To understand if this was good or bad and where to look for the cause, we first had to learn how to count: who uses our services, whether they return to them, and what they get in the end. We will talk about this journey, from the list of visitors in the admin panel to the manual analysis of 56 reports, in this article.

Hello everyone, we are Yulia Efimova and Maya Azarova, representing the product part of the AI LAB team at Atom. In the previous article, we talked about how Transcriber for UX researchers grew into a separate direction of internal AI services. Today, we will talk about the path to metrics for our portfolio of internal AI tools:

  • Transcriber — Transcriber, we will use it as an example to discuss metrics in this article;

  • Atom Intelligence — an AI chat for the entire company with connections to various internal data sources and a corporate knowledge base;

  • DDM — smart monitoring of tasks in Jira, dashboard and report generation based on user settings, and reminders for urgent tasks;

  • TAR — AI code review and test generation in GitLab via slash commands in merge requests.

Transcriber was the first AI product, so we initially tested a set of metrics on it, which we eventually formalized into a general framework and decided to uniformly distribute across the entire portfolio. That is, not entirely uniformly, of course, but we will go in order.

When did we realize we knew little about our users?

The first audience for Transcriber, as we mentioned, were UX researchers, about ten people with a common context. They formulated the requirements themselves, used the service weekly, and directly reported problems. The product was in its early stages of development and was managed practically manually. As soon as we expanded the target audience to other employees of the company and added analysis of new types of meetings, we realized that we did not have answers to a number of questions about our product, for example:

  • does the service benefit the new audience?

  • is the number of active users growing?

  • do colleagues limit themselves to the first visit or return to the tool?

  • how much time can each created artifact save?

The work on metrics began with these questions.

User List

We started with the simplest analysis: a separate tab in the admin panel with a list of those who visited the service (their email, date of first visit, a flag indicating whether the employee performed at least one action). We immediately noticed a pattern: each publication in our work channel created a wave of visits from 10-20 people within an hour. However, such a list answered the question of who showed interest, but not what happened next. Did the user look at the interface and close the tab, or did they upload a file and get the transcript — it was impossible to determine from the table.

In parallel, we collected feedback directly in the product in three ways: likes and dislikes on AI chat responses with problem category indication; free-form comments; five-point ratings for artifacts, separately for the AI report, transcript, and chat.

The most useful part at the initial stage was the free-form comments. Phrasing like "incorrect speaker distribution," "the format of the minutes needs to be changed, add all topics as a list at the beginning," "error recognizing the word ACC" directly translated into improvements for us. A generalized rating of 3/5 does not provide such an opportunity.

First Metrics

We started displaying metrics that could be viewed in different time frames — daily, monthly, yearly, and any period by calendar.

In the first iteration, the set of metrics was simple:

  • visitors per period;

  • active users per period;

  • new users;

  • number of projects created, files uploaded, and transcribed.

    Active and new Transcriber users by month, April – August 2026

    This set was enough to see the dynamics. The number of new users per month grew from 5 in April to almost 30 in June and July, and in August, it decreased to about 13.

    In August, we did not release new features: the team completed refactoring and prepared the next release. The total number of employees who performed at least one action in the service by the end of August reached almost 100 people. However, the obtained figures still did not answer two key questions: does the service bring value, and do our colleagues return to the tool?

    Value, Returns, and Errors

    Before building the next layer, we agreed on terms and defined what we consider a valuable, or value-adding, action. We identified a set of value-adding actions, i.e., those for which the user comes to the service: downloading a finished document, receiving a transcript, visiting the AI report page (for more than one minute), providing feedback on artifacts, AI chat response based on meeting materials. Everything else, including short visits (less than one minute), was considered auxiliary events.

    Additionally, we added DAU (daily active users), WAU (weekly active users), MAU (monthly active users) metrics, and "stickiness" — the ratio of DAU to MAU, as well as the average number of value-adding actions per active user. The latter indicator is particularly interesting because it grew from approximately 3.4 in April to 6.8 in August.

    Average number of value-adding actions per active user, April–August 2026

    The audience grew significantly during the same period, and the depth of usage did not dilute but doubled. If the growth were driven by single visits out of curiosity, the average would have gone down. Since August 24, when visit data became complete (before that, some visits might not have been included in the statistics), the proportion of visitors who performed at least one action has remained above 80%.

    "Stickiness" on most working days remains in the range of 10-25%, with occasional peaks up to 45% and zeros on weekends. This is normal for Transcriber because users come when their research is done, important meetings or calls requiring processing have occurred. The natural rhythm here is rather weekly than daily.

    A separate block of our metrics dashboard is dedicated to what breaks. We added the number of transcription errors per period and the ratio of unsuccessful calls to successful ones.

    General recommendation: do not forget to display not only positive metrics on the dashboard but also counter-metrics, otherwise, you will always have only a deceptively pleasant picture. It is also important that the entire team agrees on how to interpret the obtained metrics and what user behavior for a specific product at a specific stage of development is considered normal.

    Where we are heading: from usage to business effect

    Gradually, the metrics formed into several layers. The universal layer is the same for all services: visitors, active users, activation rate, DAU, WAU, MAU, stickiness, new and returning users, value events and depth of use, feedback volume, and positive feedback share. On top of this, there is an inter-service layer that answers the question of how many employees work in more than one AI service and how product audiences intersect.

    The efficiency and reliability layer calculates the number of model calls, cost per request, per active user, and per value event, error rate, p50 and p95 latency, and availability relative to SLO.

    The business impact layer solves a problem common to all internal tools: they do not directly increase revenue, so a proxy indicator of value is needed. We calculate saved hours through the volume of work done, the base labor costs per unit agreed with the team, and adjustment factors for rework and for the fact that part of the work would not have been done at all without the tool. The calculation of saved hours using this formula is a topic for a separate discussion; here, we will limit ourselves to cost and speed benchmarks.

    The diagram shows the same layers broken down by proximity to business results: universal metrics are the lower levels, and quality, reliability, and cost run through all of them.

    How much does one AI report cost?

    One AI report costs us less than 100 rubles — the exact amount depends on the complexity of the recording and the type of report. An hourly meeting is usually processed in about 15 minutes, and during this entire time, the employee is busy with their own affairs, not transcribing. For comparison: manually, an hour of a user test turns into several hours of transcription, as we wrote in the first part.

    But the main value is not only in the saved time but also in the fact that the transcript and report remain in the system, can be revisited after six months, to find out what the respondent actually said or what was agreed upon at the meeting, and be used as material for the internal knowledge base. Without transcription, these recordings would likely not be reviewed by anyone.

    Where does uniformity end?

    Despite the desire for uniformity, for each product, it was necessary to separately define what constitutes value and activity. Our current version is shown below:

    Product

    Activity (user actions)

    Value (achieved result)

    Transcriber

    File upload; project creation; chat question

    Completed transcript; exported report; chat response on meeting content

    Atom Intelligence

    Chat question; file upload; file download

    Answer to a question based on internal sources or attached files

    TAR

    Initiating review; requests for help; comments

    Completed review for merge request; implemented suggestion

    DDM

    Report creation and opening; setting up alerts for oneself; setting up reminders for colleagues

    Generated report; sent notification

    Here the difficulties begin, which, judging by public discussions, we are not alone in experiencing. There is no single definition of an active user, and it has to be rediscovered each time. TAR can be launched automatically when a merge request is opened. The user did not perform any action, but they received a review and, possibly, applied some suggestions. Is this active or not? DDM sends email notifications, but some people don't read them... On the other hand, there are users who view the dashboard, but their actions were not initially registered as active. How long do you need to look at the dashboard for it to be considered conscious use, not just an accidentally opened link? In Transcriber, an employee could download an AI report but ultimately not use it and not indicate anywhere that the quality was unsatisfactory.

    We haven't found a single answer yet. Since the products themselves and their metrics are still evolving, at some point, we will change the current set of metrics based on the emergence of new functionality, observations, and conversations with users about how they actually use the product, and more systematic use of heatmaps.

    Summary Quality: How We Evaluate and Improve Reports

    Everything described above measures user behavior and business value. None of these metrics, except for textual and sometimes subjective user feedback, answers the question: "Has our AI report (summary) become better?"

    For this, we set up a separate automatic quality evaluation loop in Langfuse — an open-source platform for tracing and evaluating LLM applications. We chose it for two reasons: it's an open-source solution, and it has prompt versioning, without which it's impossible to understand which prompt edit led to what result.

    The evaluation is done by a separate evaluator model (LLM-as-a-judge approach): each generated meeting report receives a score on a five-point scale based on an internal set of criteria. The detailed evaluation logic is under NDA, but the principle is standard: criteria are set separately for different types of meetings. The higher the score, the better the report meets the criteria for that type of meeting.

    Summaries for research meetings consistently receive fours and fives. The explanation is simple: the report structure for this scenario was created together with researchers, for a specific client and a specific work format. We started from user requirements, and the result meets those requirements. Scores for other work meetings are distributed much more broadly, with threes appearing regularly. To understand the reasons, we conducted extensive analytical work, including manual log analysis.

    Analyzing Poor Scores

    We collected traces of all summaries that the evaluator in Langfuse checked from July 3 to September 22, 2026: 424 reports, of which 374 were for work meetings. 168 out of 374 received a score of 3 or lower, meaning 45%; the remaining 206 received a 4 or 5. We did not include research meetings in the analysis, as the goal was to improve the quality of AI reports for work meetings specifically.

    We read 56 reports in full, along with their transcripts: all 3 ones, 22 twos out of 37, and 31 threes out of 128. For each summary, we recorded one main reason for the poor score. Additionally, for each of the 56 analyzed reports, we generated reference summaries using a stronger model — these later served as a benchmark for comparison.

    • Summarization prompt — 45%. Almost half of the cases were caused by the quality of the summarization model's prompt.

    • Meeting is not "work-related" — 36%. Test drives with dictated incidents, product demos, status syncs. The format with topics, decisions, and tasks does not suit them, and no prompt edit for a work meeting will fix this.

    • Quality of source data — 12%. English speech recorded in Russian transliteration (before English was added to Transcriber in September), unreadable fragments, "Speaker 1" labels instead of names.

    • Evaluator error — 7%. For example, the evaluator gave a score of one for a correct refusal when the recording turned out to be a conversation with a voice assistant about ordering products instead of a meeting.

    It turns out that four out of five poor scores were not due to a picky evaluator or a broken transcript, but to the prompt itself and the inappropriate report type.

    What exactly was breaking in the work meeting reports?

    One report usually contained several problems, an average of 2.9, and the most frequent ones depend on our prompts:

    • main meeting conclusion lost — 28 out of 56 reports. Formally, the report is correct, but the key takeaway for which the meeting would be read was missing;

    • junk in tasks: things done directly in the meeting, trivial matters, others' assignments ("share screen") — 15;

    • sections contradict each other: one says "no significant topics," while the adjacent one has five substantive points — 15;

    • "no decisions," although there were some — 10, and the reverse error, a fabricated decision — 4;

    • inflated open questions, among which are those closed during the meeting — 10.

    From version to version, we tightened the rules so the model wouldn't invent assignments, and it started to discard real tasks. Consequently, our task description section degraded. A report of "no active tasks" with a deadline and an agreement for the next meeting became a typical scenario. The rule "better not to write than to invent" sounds reasonable, but without a counterbalance, the model goes to the other extreme. For the reader, a lost agreement is as bad as an invented one.

    What we fixed and what resulted

    • Rewrote the work meeting prompt. Each edit is tied to a defect from the analysis. Sections must be consistent with each other. A task is defined by specific signals: a first-person promise, an aloud instruction, a stated deadline. An organizational step and one participant's opinion are not considered a decision, and if an agreement was overturned, the report reflects the latest version. There are no more than five open questions, and only those that were not answered during the meeting. The main conclusion and the figures on which the decision is based are mandatory. The labels "blocker / important / minor" were removed entirely: an incorrect label did more harm than its absence, as the reader trusts it and doesn't re-read the topic.

    • Created separate report types for meetings that don't fit the work format. Reports for customer test drives and technical sprints are similar to research, but require their own templates, and summaries for lectures — although they are work meetings, they also require different prompts.

    • Redesigned the evaluator. Our first evaluator penalized correct refusals and did not distinguish between a short report and an empty one, so relying on its scores when editing the prompt was impossible. The new one first checks if there was material for the report in the recording and only then evaluates the quality.

    The new version — prompt, report types, and evaluator — was tested on 17 out of 56 analyzed meetings, including 8 out of 25 where the prompt was the main reason. New reports were compared with the reference summaries created during the analysis, and scores were observed. The average score for "poor" meetings increased from 2.53 to 3.35, while the median remained at 3. In eight meetings where the prompt was the main reason, the average increased from 2.62 to 3.50, and the median from 3 to 4. A total of eight out of 17 meetings received a four or five; before the edit, all 17 were rated 3 or lower — we selected them based on this criterion.

    The experiment has limitations, and we want to state them directly:

    • the evaluator changed along with the prompt, so the scores before and after are only approximately comparable;

    • in this iteration, we selected only the weakest reports, not considering those that received high scores;

    • each report was run once, although both generation and evaluation are non-deterministic;

    • 17 meetings is too few to talk about statistical significance;

    • we have not yet compared the evaluator's scores with human evaluations, and the reference summaries were written by a stronger model.

    We accept this result not as the ultimate truth, but as a benchmark for future improvements.

    What we learned

    1. Free-form comments are more useful than scale ratings. "Incorrect speaker distribution" immediately turns into a task, while 3/5 does not.

    2. Metrics need counterweights. Alongside audience growth, there should be errors and unsuccessful calls, otherwise, the picture will always be pleasant.

    3. "Active user" and "value" are defined for each product separately, and these definitions will need to be revised.

    4. The rule "better not to write than to invent" needs a counterbalance. Otherwise, the model starts to lose real agreements.

    5. The evaluator first checks if there was material for the report, and only then evaluates the quality.

    6. Not all meetings are "work-related." Test drives, lectures, and demos need their own report types.

    Currently, we are running an experiment comparing reports created by humans and with the help of our tool. We also plan to accumulate recordings for at least a month and repeat the analysis, while continuing to collect live feedback from our users. The work is in progress, and it is more important than ever for us that the metrics are not just a set of abstract numbers for reporting, but answer our questions and reflect real usage.
