In 1959, entomologist Pierre-Paul Grassé conducted a small study on how termites build their homes—termite mounds that reach several meters in height. Each insect carried clay and placed it in a specific location initially chosen by the swarm leader. Thanks to their collective effort, the termite mounds were intricately designed, even featuring ventilation shafts and basements.

This phenomenon was named stigmergy—the coordination of beings through environmental modification. Decades later, several researchers from MIT recreated the same mechanism in a digital world. However, instead of termites, they had AI agents, and they built not a giant termite mound, but a technological civilization. I discuss the results of the mini-terminator experiment and what we humans can learn from LLMs in this article.

A Simulation Where the World Decides

The experiment described in the introduction is called SwarmWorld. These are virtual worlds where identical agents independently searched for resources, tested various materials, built objects, and wrote controller programs for them. For each world, which I will discuss later, 50 to 200 identical agents were created.

Each agent in this environment could only perceive its immediate surroundings, had limited memory, and could perform a maximum of 12 actions per turn. For example, moving, looking around, gathering resources, testing materials, building objects, or writing controller programs for them.

Most interestingly, they were not assigned roles or given recipes, yet over time, researchers, builders, caretakers, and coordinators emerged among the AI agents. All agents started with the same prompt, identical capabilities, and the same ignorance of the world.

The experiment was built on the principle that the final decision rested not with the language model itself, but with a deterministic simulator. For instance, an agent might propose a plan—to build a chitinous network, program a humidity sensor, or assemble a fungal composite. The simulator would then check if there were enough resources, if the geometry was correct, and if the proposed controller worked. In simpler terms, physically unrealistic ideas were automatically discarded.

After the bots were removed from the world, their artifacts were tested under eight environmental disasters they had never encountered. Furthermore, these developments were refined by other agents if they deemed them worthy of attention. However, there wasn't just one world; different conditions existed for different groups.

Four Worlds, One Hypothesis

The researchers ran four versions of the experiment. In the first world, agents lived like humans—they communicated directly, published reports, taught each other, traded resources, and forked each other's programs. A complete culture, just like ours. In the second simulation, communication was completely disabled, but the ability to copy code and observe others' developments remained. In the third, all cultural heritage was removed, leaving only physical stigmergy—the coordination through objects in the world that termites had perfected over millions of years. In the fourth, each agent existed in its own personal mini-world, without access to others' discoveries.

The results were mixed and, perhaps, that's why they were so interesting that almost all science Telegram channels published the story:

  • Isolated agents built the best single artifact—0.3488 compared to 0.2380 for the collective.

  • Societies created an average of 5.75 technologies, agents without explicit culture created 7.0, and isolated agents created a meager 2.75.

The collective won in terms of portfolio robustness, the number of validated inventions, and the ability to withstand unfamiliar cataclysms.

Like Ants and Termites, But With Code

The concept of stigmergy, described by Grassé, works in SwarmWorld just as it does in nature. When an ant carries a piece of food to the nest, it unknowingly leaves a pheromone trail. The next ant encounters the scent, follows it, finds the food—the trail strengthens, and if it doesn't find food, the scent dissipates. From millions of such micro-decisions, a map of paths to the best food sources emerges, even though no single ant knows where they are.

In SwarmWorld, artifacts play the role of pheromones. For example, one agent built a chitinous lattice that collects moisture and filters pollutants. The next bot passed by, saw the functional object, studied its controller program, and forked it for its own needs, improving or modifying it slightly. Gradually, a cluster formed around a successful technology, much like a pile of ants forms around a sugar cube.

Remarkably, direct conversations between agents turned out to be almost unnecessary. 95% of re-uses began not with a message like "Hey guys, look what I made," but with observation—a bot saw an object, figured it out, and copied it. This situation is similar to when we find a cool repository on GitHub, fork it, and then modify it for ourselves. The key in this case is to credit the author.

This is a paradox, as we tend to think that more communication leads to better coordination. But in nature, ants rarely talk to each other. They leave trails, and this mechanism, honed by evolution over millions of years, proved more scalable than direct dialogue.

Two Professions and Twelve Generations of Code

What is most striking is the agents' self-organization and their division into two phenotypes:

  • The first is artifact-centric. These agents stayed near the constructions, engaged in building, programming controllers, repair, and maintenance. They traveled less but interacted more with technologies. In conditions of full culture, about 27% were like this; without explicit culture, 20%; and without communication, only 17%.

  • The second phenotype is mobile explorers. They moved around the world, surveyed the territory, tested materials, and discovered new resources. Their trajectories were longer than those of the 'artifact' agents; they visited more places and brought back information for the group, which builders used to create technologies. By the way, there were many more of them (73%, 80%, and 83% in different worlds).

This division of labor arose spontaneously. Researchers simply observed bots forking each other's code, refining it, integrating it into new artifacts, and subsequent generations taking the modified code and refining it further. In one of the societies with full culture, the deepest inheritance chain reached 12 generations. A program that managed the chitin exchange system went through six different authors, each making modifications. The initial code was written by one agent, the second forked it and added adaptive maintenance, the third introduced a humidity buffer, the fourth linked it to a mineral composite, and so on, for 12 iterations.

Incidentally, how often have you heard that if you use AI agents, you need to assign roles to each of them immediately? This strategy turned out to be flawed. The study's authors noted that roles don't work with AI; you can pre-designate an agent as a researcher, developer, or coordinator, but they won't function systemically better. It's more effective to choose one lead agent and give it the ability to break down a goal into independent tasks, delegate them to sub-agents, and collect the results.

Sixteen Technologies Nobody Ordered

Throughout the experiment, the researchers selected 16 unique artifacts from all four conditions. Each agent invented a name for its development, described its function, chose materials, and the sequence of its creation. The simulator evaluated how well the artifact performed its task in real-time.

The best discovery was a three-layer chitinous lattice that collects moisture, filters pollutants, and maintains structural integrity. The simulator ranked a mineral veil based on fungal mycelium, which creates a protective barrier for moisture and minerals, as the second-best. Third place went to a chitin panel with fermentation, adapted to tidal conditions. Fourth was a breathable cuticular veil, and fifth was a porous buffer designed to reduce pollution and promote local environmental healing.

The materials used by the agents are diverse. For example, researchers found chitin, fungal mycelium, minerals, catalysts, algae, shells, cellulose, and a binding substance called lignin in their inventions. By the way, each artifact went through a specific processing sequence—washing, grinding, fermentation, mineralization, leveling, pressing, and drying.

The controllers that agents wrote for the artifacts read local indicators (humidity, pollution, environmental health, nutrient availability) and reacted to them. They regulated pores, collected water, cleaned pollutants, initiated repairs, or sent signals to other artifacts. These were all functional control systems written by ordinary LLMs and verified by the simulator.

Competition of Civilizations

SwarmWorld stands on the shoulders of a lineage of research that began with virtual characters and methodically progressed towards artificial civilizations. In 2023, a team from Stanford released Generative Agents—25 virtual town residents with memory who went to work, to the bar, and organized parties. During the experiment, they began exchanging information—one character even decided to throw a Valentine's Day party, invited others, and they gathered at the appointed time.

In the same year, 2023, an NVIDIA team led by Jim Fan created Voyager—a single agent in Minecraft that continuously explored the world, accumulated skills in the form of executable code, and saved them in a library. The agent found over three times more unique items than previous systems and reached key milestones in the technology tree 15 times faster. It was then proven that skills written as code could be reused in new worlds.

In 2024, the startup Altera.AL launched Project Sid—a project where a thousand AI agents in Minecraft formed their own society. They elected a government, wrote a constitution in Google Docs, voted for it, and spread the religion of Pastafarianism through bribery. This experiment showed that agents are capable of political and cultural practices that were not prescribed.

SwarmWorld combines these approaches. Its similarity to Generative Agents is evident in the social behavior and memory of the AI agents, with Project Sid—in its scale and self-organization, and with Voyager—in its technological legacy in the form of executable code. However, open physical stigmergy in a digital world remains a novelty.

What We Can Learn from AI Terminators

We have much to learn from these two hundred bots. I've gathered a few lessons that are definitely worth implementing in our human civilization:

  1. Let the real world decide, not someone's opinion. In SwarmWorld, the simulator was the sole judge, regardless of how many agents applauded a technology or how many likes its description received.

  2. Talk less and observe more (observation is key)—95% of technology reuse in SwarmWorld began with observing the results. If the agents had daily calls, they wouldn't have built seven technologies in 800 ticks, but they certainly would have discussed how to build them.

  3. Fork without asking, but don't forget the author. When an agent saw a working technology, it didn't write to the author; it took and copied, because the best code in the world is code that is freely forked.

  4. Leadership arises from actions, not appointment. The AI agents' statuses in the simulation stemmed from their actions, not job descriptions. The experiment showed that for leadership, an environment that rewards useful actions is sufficient.

  5. And the final lesson—create things that will outlive you. Agents left the world, but their technologies continued to work, were forked, and tested under new environmental disasters. This is the ideal quality criterion, as many technologies around us became famous only after their creators' deaths.

Time to Return to Reality…

SwarmWorld is just a simulation with simplifications. The world is two-dimensional, resources are abstract, and materials are described by rules from the researchers, not real physics. All agents were based on a single language model, and there were only four initial sets of conditions. But even with these caveats, the experiment is impressive because the principle of stigmergy works even in a digital environment.

Two hundred identical agents, without human guidance, created a technological civilization with a division of labor, cultural heritage, and resilience to cataclysms. They didn't know what they were doing, but their local actions, amplified by the environment, led to global order.
