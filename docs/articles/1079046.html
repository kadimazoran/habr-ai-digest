"Content is King!" he once said, and they believed him and followed him, and the internet began to fill with necessary, high-quality, useful, and relevant, but sometimes not so much, information. And there was more and more of it, and then AI came, and a new era began... The era of great (in quantity) and terrible (in quality) neuro-slop. Now it's the main allergen in the IT community: everyone considers it important to express their weighty and irrefutable "boo" on this topic, setting up special super-mega-detectors against any minimally structured and grammatically correct texts, seriously assuring that they can "smell AI-generated content from a mile away."

And do you remember what the internet looked like just a few years ago, before AI? How much useful, smart, interesting, and educational content could be read?
Ah, that good old era of innocence of classic SEO copywriting. Tons of dull human trash, written for pennies. Amusing articles, concocted on the principle of "insert the keyword phrase *buy a green crocodile cheaply* five times per paragraph." And it was exactly the same slop, but hand-assembled and manually produced. Everyone lived peacefully, read, browsed, consumed this content, and didn't complain much.

But now it's a different story! Neuro-slop is advancing, and we must defend our positions. Everywhere you look, people are frantically running texts through dubious detectors that see traces of ChatGPT in every other normal technical manual, suspiciously eyeing any bulleted lists, and meticulously counting the number of em dashes against the character count. A classic witch hunt in its purest form.

And the question arises: "So, is it king or not? Or is one king different from another? Or will any non-king do?"

The whole paradox is that the information community itself is zealously pushing us into this AI-driven conveyor belt. The main task of promotion now is not just to get into search results as in ancient times, but to aim precisely at the overviews of AI search engines and the vector databases of RAG systems (all this is grandly abbreviated as GEO).

And what does it take to get into this dream search result and be listed in the entities? That's right! You need to feed the machines as much relevant text as possible. Upload hundreds of articles with descriptions of scenarios, terms, and processes everywhere you can. In other words, churn out megabytes of that very neuro-slop, and the more, the better. There's no thought of quality, necessity, value, or other benefits here.

And the circle is complete – it is king after all, although there's almost nothing kingly left in it.

And so you sit and think: "How do I promote my accounting system? I don't want to produce slop, I'll write with quality." You take a topic, lovingly ponder it, and write every line, spending weeks on one in-depth article with case studies and real facts. You publish it... And it's completely useless to anyone. It's simply buried under huge tsunamis of articles generated in three minutes by competitors who have launched automatic content farms.

As a result, you stand at a crossroads:

  • Either you principledly craft rare masterpieces for a handful of anonymous readers and get kicked out of the market.

  • Or you resign yourself, adjust your prompts, and start industrially feeding the bots so that AI algorithms notice your existence.

A brave new world in action: humans generate gigabytes of text by machines for machines, so that machines can then tell people who's who, where to go, and what to do. And amidst this data cycle, we, with serious faces, look for AI markers in each other's texts.

What should be done to avoid creating nonsense and still be at the top? Launch a pointless and ruthless conveyor belt of tiresome neuro-slop, or remain a craftsman with near-zero reach?

Remember what Bernard Shaw said? "Kings are not born, they are made by universal hallucination." So maybe it's time to stop supporting this new hallucination?
