If you browse job postings on Headhunter or LinkedIn, sooner or later you'll come across ads looking for someone with five years of experience who can read other people's code without comments, understands multithreading, and is ready to work with a large volume of code written without human involvement. Sometimes, these ads are posted by the same companies that, a year or two ago, boasted in press releases about how they replaced half of their junior developers and almost all manual QA with agents and generation pipelines. You'll pause at the last line of requirements with the same expression you get when reading a medicine's side effects, where the side effect is the illness itself.

A year ago, such an ad would have been a joke in the break room, but now similar vacancies are in every other feed, with various titles from the polite 'AI code reviewer' to 'vibe-code cleaner.' Behind them is almost always the same story: a company first paid to remove people from the process, and then paid again, and more expensively, to bring them back, but now as cleaners after a banquet they weren't invited to.

There have been several waves of technologies over the past decades that were supposed to eliminate programmers, from visual script editors to no-code builders, and each has rolled back along roughly the same trajectory. The current one is just noticeably faster because so much money was poured into it that losses started being counted earlier than usual.

Operators Came Back First

The rollback is most evident in support, because there the cost of error is measured in money and customer churn almost in real-time, and no beautiful report about saved positions can survive the customer outflow graph. In 2024, Klarna loudly announced that its chatbot was doing the work of seven hundred operators, but in the spring of 2025, its CEO admitted that the focus on savings had led to a decline in quality, and the company began hiring live people for support again, presenting it as a well-thought-out strategy, as if it had been planned from the start.

In the summer of the same year, Australia's Commonwealth Bank cut forty-five call center positions and replaced them with a voice bot. After this, the number of calls only increased, the remaining employees were forced into overtime, and team leads themselves had to take calls. About a month later, when the union got involved, the bank publicly apologized and offered the laid-off employees their jobs back.

The mechanics of both stories are the same, and it's familiar to anyone who has ever soft-launched a game: the metric they optimized improved as expected, but a neighboring metric, which no one was watching, quietly fell apart. By the time it was noticed, it turned out to be cheaper to bring people back than to explain to customers why the bot was offering to reset their password for the third time in a row.

Code That's Written Faster Than It's Read

The situation with code is more subtle because the rollback rarely makes the news and happens quietly within teams, in code reviews, in missed deadlines, and in who has to sit over a crash dump at two in the morning. In the summer of 2025, developers at METR had experienced open-source developers solve tasks in their own repositories. It turned out that with AI tools, tasks were completed about nineteen percent slower, even though the participants themselves were convinced after the experiment that they had sped up by about twenty percent. This gap between perception and the stopwatch seems to me to be the most important figure in this whole story.

Similar stories regularly surface in comments on Habr and threads on Reddit, where developers describe the same scenario: generation provides a very quick initial result, pull requests fly in batches, and a month later it turns out that the project contains several almost identical solutions to the same problem, written at different times with different prompts. Untangling this mess falls to the few people on the team who still remember how the system was originally conceived.

Generated code has an unpleasant property: it looks correct right up until you run it on real hardware with real data. Only the people who were supposed to be replaced by it can verify it, because a junior developer will likely miss a race condition in code that looks cleaner than their own. A senior developer, on the other hand, needs time to find it, time that was previously spent writing the code themselves, without the race condition.

Therefore, judging by job postings and what developers themselves write, no one has completely discarded agents from pipelines. Instead, entire layers of people have grown around them: reviewers and module owners responsible for every change in their area, and even specialists who clean up what has already made it into the master branch. If you add up their salaries, the savings from reducing junior staff start to suspiciously resemble a loan taken at a very unfavorable interest rate.

Gamers Have Also Learned to Count

In game development, the rollback is being pushed from below by players who, over a couple of years, have learned to recognize generated content faster than any detector. They spot it by the extra fingers on a poster, by signs with letters that blur as if drawn in a shaky bus, and by voice acting where a character shouts 'grenade' with the intonation of a weekend weather forecast.

Back in early 2024, Steam mandated that developers disclose the use of generative content. This tag on a game's page quickly turned from a formality into a filter, with a significant portion of the audience simply scrolling past the game. And at the end of 2025, if I remember correctly, Clair Obscur lost its Indie Game Awards after it emerged that generated placeholder textures, which the developers later cleaned up with a patch, had made it into the release build.

For a studio, this translates into very concrete arithmetic: the money saved on a concept artist must be weighed against the cost of a release scandal, refunds, and reviews that linger on the Steam page for years. After such a comparison, it's not surprising that more and more studios are explicitly emphasizing in game descriptions and interviews that the art, sound, and text are made by humans, and job postings are again featuring artists and voice actors who were recently the first to be laid off.

Amusingly, the industry itself continues to use generation, but it's been hidden deeper in the pipeline, for prototypes, research, and rough placement – places where the player will never see it. Everything the player sees and hears in the release is again made by a human, who had to be hired back for this purpose, sometimes by the same studio and sometimes for more money than they earned before being laid off.

Generation Is Cheap, Verification Is Expensive

If we reduce all these stories to a single formula, we get rather boring economics that, for some reason, few people calculated in advance: generating text, code, images, or a response to a client costs almost nothing. However, verifying the result costs as much as it used to cost to create it, and sometimes even more, because a person searches for their own mistake where they had doubts, while they doubt everything in someone else's work at once.

If we estimate for one typical feature: previously, a junior developer would write it in three days, and a senior developer would spend a couple of hours reviewing it. Now, an agent writes it in twenty minutes, and a senior developer spends a day and a half figuring out exactly where it's wrong, then another day rewriting what they found. As a result, the same feature costs the company more, and it's paid for by the most expensive person on the team, who already has a queue of tasks stretching to the next milestone.

And there's another expense that isn't visible at all in quarterly reports: the junior developers removed from the process were the very place where senior developers would grow in three to four years. Companies that were the first to stop hiring junior staff are now the first to discover that soon there will be no one to review the generated code, and a ready-made senior developer on the market costs so much that all of the first year's savings go towards one or two people.

Where AI Is Moving

Interestingly, the models themselves remain with the same companies and in the same pipelines. They are simply being moved from the positions where they replaced people back into tools, onto the same shelf as autocompletion, static analyzers, and profilers – that is, into the category of things that people use in their work and which have never been on the official staff list.

It turns out that the replacement really happened, just in the opposite direction from what was promised in presentations. In every position where a model was placed instead of a person and left unsupervised, a person sits again after a year or two, usually more experienced and more expensive than the one who was removed. This person does the same job plus cleaning up after the model that occupied their chair before them.

I think in a couple of years, the phrase 'AI will replace us' will sound about the same as 'outsourcing in another time zone will replace us' sounds now – a memory of a time when someone really wanted to save money on people and ended up paying those same people, but at triple the cost and with a delay of one release cycle.
