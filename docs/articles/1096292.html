At peak hour, the average speed in Bangalore is about 14 km/h, according to the TomTom traffic index. The city ranks second globally in congestion. To manage such traffic, data is needed: where the jams are now and what the roads will look like in five minutes. There are almost no road sensors in the city, but there are over five thousand security cameras.

From their video feeds, a traffic map and short-term forecasts can be generated. However, collecting the recordings in one place is not feasible: the network cannot handle such a flow, and the cameras belong to different departments, which are not obligated to share raw footage.

Researchers from the Indian Institute of Science (IISc) proposed a different approach: process the video near the camera and then transmit only numbers—how many vehicles of each type have passed. The system was named TrafficFab and tested on a setup with 400 video streams.

The system consists of three layers and three control loops

TrafficFab architecture with three layers. Source

The first layer is the edge network. Here, 42 Raspberry Pis (low-cost single-board computers) play back recorded videos as if they were live camera feeds. Video near the source is processed by Jetsons (compact Nvidia computers with built-in GPUs for AI) and workstations with GPUs.

The second layer is Amazon's public cloud: numerical data from the cameras arrives here, where the traffic situation is assessed and forecasts are built.

The third layer is the private cloud. It is not involved in the path from the camera to the forecast but maintains the quality of the first layer: GPU stations are used here to collect and average detector updates computed by individual devices. Additionally, in the testbed, ten standard servers in the private cloud play back video streams to test the load of up to a thousand cameras.

Above these layers are three control loops, each with its own task:

  1. An edge scheduler decides which device processes which camera.

  2. A cloud service allocates resources for forecasting when more cameras and applications, such as operator dashboards, request data simultaneously.

  3. A retraining loop selects when and on which hardware to fine-tune the vehicle detector.

Right by the road, a camera turns into a vehicle counter

Each video stream goes to a computing node near the camera. There, frames are decoded, batched, and sent to the YOLO26s detector. The model was specifically fine-tuned on annotated frames from Indian roads. Why? Because three-wheelers, trucks, and scooters often travel in the same lane without lane markings, causing standard detectors to make errors.

Example frames from Bangalore intersections. Source

A single detector is not enough. To avoid counting a vehicle twice, the stream passes through a tracker, which assigns a persistent ID to each vehicle across several frames. At the output, we get two sets of data. The first shows how many vehicles of each class appeared in the frame per second. The second stores pairs of "track ID, class" for each moment in time.

Short recordings, without frames or intermediate features, are sent to the cloud. The cloud service receives them in batches of 15 seconds (the interval can be changed) and aggregates them into time series per camera.

The amount of data processed by the system is illustrated by a one-hour run on 400 cameras: the detector produced up to 48,000 detections per second in total, with over 99% of the time having more than 40,000. The composition of the traffic flow was diverse: auto-rickshaws accounted for about 30% of detected vehicles, trucks around 25%, and hatchbacks about 12%.

The city network is not connected in the experiment; cameras are replaced by recordings. Morning videos are distributed as live streams by 42 Raspberry Pi single-board computers, totaling about a hundred video streams. For higher loads, ten servers were added, which together distribute up to a thousand streams. The rest of the system receives this video via the same protocol as a regular IP camera and cannot distinguish a recording from a live stream. Therefore, everything after the camera works in real-time. The only aspect not tested is operation with a real city camera network.

How energy is saved

The system can be assembled from various processing devices: there are 29 in the testbed, representing eight types, from compact Jetsons to GPU workstations. The scheduler decides how many cameras to assign to each device. This affects the energy consumption for the same task: an active device consumes power even under low load, and the power consumption with an increasing number of streams varies differently across models. At 50 streams, for example, one configuration requires 116 W, while another requires 235 W.

The limit of each device was determined by measurement: streams were added until it could no longer output 20 frames per second per stream. A Jetson Xavier AGX can handle 7 streams at about 38 W. A Jetson Orin AGX 32 GB handles 20 streams at 45 W, which is about three times more with only an 18% increase in power. The most streams, 74, are served by a station with an RTX 6000 Pro at 196 W, while an RTX 4090 at 55 streams consumes 236 W.

The task is as follows: given a certain number of streams and devices. Each stream must be placed exactly once, without exceeding the limit of any device, and maintaining the required frame rate on active devices. From the valid options, the one with the minimum total power consumption is chosen. The power consumption of each device is known only from measurements—formulas are not helpful. The best configuration is found through exact calculation (dynamic programming): it adds devices step by step, remembers the most economical option at each step, and finally outputs the configuration with minimum power. If there are more streams than all devices can handle combined, there is no solution: for the Jetson testbed, this limit is 286 streams, and with workstations—1000.

The difference in energy consumption between combinations is most noticeable when cameras are connected after startup. Here, it's not enough to find the best configuration by power: to achieve it, running streams must be migrated. And each migration involves a pause in processing and device reconfiguration. Therefore, not only power but also the cost of migrations is considered. There are three approaches:

  • The best configuration is recalculated for all streams, old and new, with some running streams migrating to other devices;

  • Running streams are left untouched, and new ones are placed in the remaining slots;

  • Migration is allowed, but a penalty is incurred for each migrated stream.

Four additional rules were used for comparison, where each new stream is immediately placed on a suitable device without recalculating the others. Such rules are called greedy: they take the best for today and do not look ahead.

The rules differ in two aspects. Devices can be iterated by capacity, starting with the smallest—or by energy consumption per frame, starting with the most economical. Streams can be packed into one device up to its limit, and only then the next one is taken—or each time placed on the one with more free space. Combinations of these aspects form the four rules.

The approaches can be compared in two modes. In static mode, all cameras are known in advance, and the configuration is calculated once before startup. In dynamic mode, streams are connected during operation, and the configuration must be revised. Let's start with static mode—it serves as a baseline.

In this mode, with 50 streams in a cluster of only Jetsons, the best possible configuration (optimum) consumes 116.2 W, the "smallest first" rule consumes 208.6 W, and the "most space" rule consumes 235.5 W. When workstations are added to the Jetsons, a poor decision becomes more costly: with the same 50 streams, the "most space" rule reaches 748.9 W. Rules that only consider capacity perform poorly.

Dynamic mode, where cameras are connected after startup, is a different story. Here, each approach has its own cost. The baseline is the power optimum, which recalculates the configuration at each step: it provides minimum power, but it is precisely because of the recalculation that migrations occur.

  • Recalculating the best configuration at each step yields the lowest power, but with 1306 migrations of running streams, and each migration disrupts operation;

  • The penalty option reduces migrations to 376, a 3.5-fold decrease, and costs an average of 13.8 W more than the optimum;

  • The no-migration option disrupts nothing but costs an average of 164 W more than the optimum.

For reference, with 500 streams, the entire cluster consumes about 1437 W according to calculations. The penalty option pays a small fraction of this power for stability, while the no-migration option pays a significantly larger portion.

Comparison of placement algorithms by power consumption and number of migrations. Source

The calculations were verified on a real cluster. The order of algorithms by power consumption matched the theory. All three dynamic programming algorithms sustained 20 frames per second. However, an economical configuration for 100 streams can differ significantly from the best for 90: a different set of devices is activated, and some running cameras must be migrated; in one step, as many as 90 streams are moved. If no streams are moved, new streams are placed in the remaining slots, which are not always the most economical, and power consumption increases. The migration penalty sets a price: streams are migrated only when energy savings outweigh it.

Cloud forecasting is limited by data reading speed

Numbers from the cameras arrive at the Amazon cloud. Three services receive them, display the traffic situation, and build forecasts for the next few minutes. The forecast is made by a graph neural network TrendGCN. Cameras are not at every intersection, so the network works with a simplified graph of intersections with cameras, and the traffic load on the streets between them is calculated using counters.

Scalability was tested on virtual machines of different sizes: from two cores and 8 GB of RAM to 16 cores and 64 GB. In the most demanding scenario, eight applications requested forecasts simultaneously, and there were 2400 video streams (obtained by duplicating 400 recorded videos). On the smallest machine, the response took 113 seconds, and on the largest—58 seconds. Resources increased eightfold, but the system only became twice as fast.

The reason lies in data reading from disk: it hits a ceiling of about 660 operations per second, and the processor is loaded at approximately 13%—just over two out of sixteen cores are occupied. The preprint suggests splitting the graph across multiple machines to remove this limitation, but this has not yet been tried.

The detector retrains on hints from a large model, while video stays put

A detector trained once becomes outdated over time: vehicle types, camera angles, and weather change. The easiest approach would be to collect video from all cameras in one place and retrain the model on it, but transmitting such a stream is difficult, and cameras may belong to different departments (e.g., police, metro, and public transport), which are not obligated to share recordings. Therefore, the detector is retrained near the cameras, and only model updates are sent to the central server.

Once per second, the device near the camera takes a random frame. It then checks how confident the detector is about the detected vehicles. If the confidence is above 0.7, the frame is added to the training set as is. If it's below 0.4, it's discarded as noise. Ambiguous results between these values are sent for annotation by a large model SAM 3. It distinguishes vehicle types more accurately than the base detector but only runs on GPU stations and operates in the background. Jetsons are too slow for it—about 0.8 frames per second. An RTX 5090 outputs 6.7 frames but consumes about 479 W.

Each device then retrains its copy of the detector; one round takes 30–108 seconds. Only weight updates are sent to the center, where they are averaged using the FedAvg scheme.

Loops are connected by streams and shared hardware

And here we come to the main point. Each loop was measured separately: the scheduler by device profiles, cloud forecasting on machines of different sizes, training by round time and load. How they work together is determined by the common unit: the stream.

The number of active streams determines everything: how many devices the scheduler will launch, how many recordings will go to the cloud, and how many frames will be included in the retraining dataset. Models are retrained in the same place where cameras are processed—on Jetsons and workstations. However, their loads differ. During training, the GPU on an Orin is loaded on average by 92%, on a Xavier—by 83.5%, and on stations with RTX 4090 and 5090—only by 57–60%. This is why heavy "teachers" are kept on workstations: there is enough spare capacity for both annotation and training simultaneously.

The scheduler determines the device limit based on measurements without training, at 20 frames per second per stream. How this limit changes while a retraining round is ongoing on the same Jetson is not shown in the preprint. The complete cycle was demonstrated at the IEEE TCSC SCALE Challenge competition, where they took first place: 400 streams, about 90 devices near cameras, 21 computing nodes, four cloud machines, and 454 containers. The preprint lacks figures on how the loops influence each other during simultaneous operation (processing speed, energy consumption, latency).

What else is being done on this topic

TrafficFab is not the only system that turns city cameras into a data source without sending all video to the cloud. Here are three similar projects:

  • EdgeCloudAI was presented at the ACM MobiCom 2024 conference. The testbed was located at the NSF COSMOS urban testbed in New York: city cameras and one edge server with an Nvidia A100 GPU. At the edge, a small neural network detects scene changes and checks if the frame contains the required objects. In the example from the publication, it's construction equipment. The system crops out unnecessary parts and sends a video fragment with reduced resolution and frame rate to the cloud. There, it is checked by GPT-4o. The user specifies what to search for. According to the publication, the edge GPU is loaded only by 5–7%, and cloud requests cost no more than $10 per week per camera for round-the-clock operation. The system differs from TrafficFab in its task and the fact that video, albeit reduced, is sent to the cloud. It searches for specified incidents and does not build a congestion map.

  • FilterForward conserves bandwidth. A camera has limited bandwidth, so high-quality video cannot be sent to the data center. Near the camera, each frame first passes through a common neural network that extracts key features: this is the most computationally intensive part and is done once for all applications. Then, each application has its own micro-classifier—a small network that answers one question based on these features: does the frame contain the required event, such as a pedestrian at a crosswalk? Only matching frames are sent to the data center for full analysis. In experiments on two sets of real footage, the volume of transmitted data was reduced by 6–13 times compared to sending the entire video in a highly compressed format.

  • CIRCLES. In November 2022, a consortium of universities conducted the MegaVanderTest experiment on Interstate 24 near Nashville. For five days during the morning rush hour, 100 vehicles with adaptive cruise control driven by AI traveled on it. The goal was to dampen stop-and-go waves that cause traffic jams without apparent reason. A 4.2-mile (about 6.8 km) stretch of highway was equipped with the I-24 MOTION system: 276 cameras on 40 poles. Their video was used to reconstruct the movement of each vehicle. However, the cameras there measured the effect of control, whereas TrafficFab estimates and forecasts road congestion and does not control vehicles.

What are the results and prospects of the experiment

So far, this is a testbed for verifying the idea; it has not yet become a city-wide system. There are no live cameras in it: video is played back from recordings made between 7 and 8 AM from late January to late February 2025.

The main run was on 400 streams. The edge scheduler was scaled up to 1000 streams, and the cloud forecast to 2400. In the cloud, extra streams were obtained by duplicating the same 400 videos, so there was no new traffic. The impact of retraining is still unknown: the final quality of the detector is not stated in the preprint.

There are no new models in operation: the detector, tracker, graph network, and the large annotation model were taken off-the-shelf. The contribution of this work lies in: stream placement algorithms that consider energy and migrations; measurements of cloud forecast limits and detector retraining without a central video storage. The video itself does not disappear: it goes from the camera to the nearest device, and questionable frame fragments are sent to GPU stations for annotation. Only numbers and model updates go to the central storage for analysis.

Real city life is much more chaotic, and some particularly unpredictable movements cannot be accounted for in advance. Testing how all this behaves in the real world will be done outside the laboratory.
