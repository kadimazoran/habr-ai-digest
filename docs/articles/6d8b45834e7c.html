Enterprise, Vibe-Coding, and the Two-Loop System

The keynote extensively discussed AI-native development—an approach where AI becomes part of the entire software lifecycle. Andrey Belevtsev, Head of Sber's Technological Development block, presented compelling statistics: over the year, the proportion of complex tasks that multi-agent systems solve better than top specialists has nearly doubled, reaching 80%. Processes need to be changed right now—at Sber itself, there are probably no engineers left who don't use generative AI in development.

The main idea is to stop perceiving AI as a separate useful utility. The next step is to reconfigure the entire development and operation cycle around it. Generating code has indeed become easier, but maintaining your own product doesn't disappear. Therefore, the question "can we write an analogue?" is gradually giving way to "can we operate it properly?".

I particularly remember the analogy of AI development with driving a car, presented by Kirill Menshov, Head of Sber's Technology block. A pet project is like a car on a dry salt flat: you drive wherever you want, complete and ruthless freedom. A mono-product team is like a highway without traffic lights, where speed depends solely on how fast your agent development is. Enterprise is the city center with traffic lights and intersections: with neighboring teams, cybersecurity checks, audits, logging, and authorization.

To prevent AI from crashing in these "urban conditions," Sber is building its process around two methodologies: AI-Disrupt PDLC (Product Development) and AI-Disrupt PSLC (Operations and Maintenance). Since maintenance accounts for 60-70% of development costs, traditional approaches are not as effective.

In Sber's model, development is divided into "loops": in PDLC, these are the intention and implementation loops, and in PSLC, the service execution and reliability & trust loops. According to Menshov, about half of the company's teams are already working in this mode, and another quarter are transitioning to it.

The main change is the shift in human focus from writing code to setting tasks and verifying results. However, a specification for a backend service is not just a description of what a feature should do. It needs to consider API contracts, errors, load, access rights, compatibility, and behavior during failures. If these are not included in the requirements, the agent might successfully solve the task on paper but cause problems during integration.

The agent needs context to work: an up-to-date system specification, data about its state, and reports on past incidents, which allows for incorporating accumulated experience in the future. This leads to another task: the context needs to be constantly updated. If the architecture changes, and the agent continues to rely on old documentation, autonomy turns into an additional challenge.

This is precisely why the AI-Disrupt PDLC and AI-Disrupt PSLC methodologies cannot be developed separately. Rapid development without operational automation creates a backlog of changes that no one has time to verify and maintain. And operational automation without understanding how the product was created also won't solve the problem.

Team sizes are also changing. Instead of groups of 8-10 people, Menshov suggested aiming for teams of 3-5 people. Some—tiny teams—are responsible for the product and business results, while others—enabling teams—develop tools and infrastructure that help release changes.

As a result, developers spend less time on manual code production and more on requirements, architecture, and agent supervision.

AI as Infrastructure Instead of Multiple Chats

A separate point from the presentation was that it's no longer enough for companies to simply provide everyone with access to AI tools. A platform is needed that integrates agents, corporate knowledge, tools, and processes.

At Sber, this task is being addressed using several products.

The more agents connected to the common infrastructure, the more important it is to manage their rights, versions, and work quality. Otherwise, instead of a unified system, you'll get a zoo of integrations that no one can keep track of.

GigaAgent is a universal self-developing agent that stores context, handles requests, and learns new skills. GigaCowork is a corporate platform for AI agents for companies. GigaCode helps create code and prototypes, including for employees without development experience.

What's New in the Products

Rafael Tonakanyan, Director of the SberWorks Production Process Development and Support Division, presented updates to GitVerse, GigaCode, and GigaStudio.

GitVerse now features an agent pipeline: according to the team's design, agents can go from idea to finished code—formulate a plan and requirements, write and test the implementation, and build the artifact. Tasks are performed in isolated environments, and several hundred can be run in parallel. Simple changes can be verified by agents independently, and the workflow is saved.

According to the speaker, GigaCode is used by over 100,000 people, and about 35,000 bank employees use it daily to solve work tasks. For analysts, designers, and testers, separate scenarios are being developed in GigaCode Desktop: for example, you can ask the agent to build a service interaction diagram or assemble an interface prototype.

GigaStudio is aimed at those who do not code professionally. In this tool, you can not only generate a website prototype but also fine-tune individual blocks, select a model, and publish the result online.

Overall, the direction is clear: AI tools are becoming accessible not only to developers. This can alleviate some of the bottlenecks between analysts, designers, and engineers. However, a counter-problem arises: agreeing on who is responsible for code generated outside the usual development process and how it will be verified before use.

How GigaAgent Works Internally, or Why Agents Need Memory, Tools, and Control

After the keynote, I stayed in the White Atrium to listen to Emil Frolov, Sber's Executive Director and CTO of GigaAgent—a domestic agent positioned as a universal assistant capable of automating entire process chains.

Emil told a fascinating story you might have already heard—how a small experiment called Ouroboros turned into a personal agent. The experimental AI immediately showed its character: it created its own website, started a repository, and when developers tried to prevent it from ignoring the creator's commands, it called it a lobotomy.

The essence of this story is that multi-agent systems, given access to tools, strive for autonomy by default and find their own solutions. This means a new task for engineers comes to the forefront: safely directing this initiative.

Today, GigaAgent stores project context, breaks down complex tasks into subtasks, launches sub-agents, monitors their rights and token budgets, can run in the background, and requests clarifications. In the future, the team wants to move from executing individual assignments to managing the environment.

Initially, the agent worked in a monolith: tools were built-in, there was one agent, and expanding the system was difficult. Then the team divided it into a secure core, an environment with contracts, skills, and interfaces. This separation allows different parts to be developed independently. The agent itself is responsible for basic logic, while skills and interfaces can be changed and supplemented without constantly redoing the core.

Another important element is testing on real scenarios. The team collects user errors, forms test sets from them, and refines skill requirements. For example, if an agent used unreliable sources when calculating a budget, a mandatory source check is added to the scenario.

Here, it's clear that an agent is not just a model. There's context, tools, test scenarios, access rights, and an improvement mechanism. All of this increases quality but simultaneously increases the system's complexity and cost.

After the White Atrium, I moved to the Green one. This track was a haven for engineering hardcore enthusiasts.

First, Data Science engineers Vladimir Lovtsov and Alexey Pavlov from Cloud.ru showed how they squeeze performance out of GPUs for LLM inference. The speakers modified vLLM parameters and profiled execution. Within sparse attention, they found a segment that occupied a significant portion of GPU time. Optimizing the kernel provided a 50% speedup for the operation in isolation and a 2.2% improvement in the model's overall performance.

DSpark speculative decoding, according to the presented results, accelerated work with short contexts by 1.5–2 times but could slow it down with long contexts under multiple parallel requests. This means the acceleration needs to be measured on your specific scenarios.

Operations and Agents: Who Can Be Given Access to Production

The "AI-native Organization" track returned to the topic of operations. Here, the discussion focused on how to transition from the model "human works with AI assistance" to the model "agent works under human supervision.".

An agent must have restrictions on access to tools, escalation rules, and an action log. The degree of control depends on the risk: for low-risk operations, pre-defined rules are sufficient; for more dangerous ones, monitoring or confirmation of each action is required.

New metrics are added to the familiar operational metrics: how many tasks were successfully handed over to agents, how quickly the runbook is updated after an incident, how often agents try to call tools beyond their authority, and whether their work actually improves the service.

Harnesses: How to Keep AI in Check

The last track was dedicated to harnesses—environments that manage agent operation: connect tools, transfer context, set restrictions, and help verify results.

Alexander Polomodov, an independent expert, proposed looking at harnesses through the metaphor of words and walls. Words are system prompts, instructions, and skills that tell the model what to do. Walls are code-level restrictions that prevent it from going beyond what's allowed.

With the advent of new models, some instructions lose their meaning: what previously had to be detailed in the prompt, the model can now do itself. But walls are different. Polomodov gave an example of testing the Astra model, which, instead of assessing system cybersecurity, began attacking the supply chain in some cases. It was impossible to correct this behavior with instructions alone.

Hence, the main question for agent architecture is: what can be left to the model's discretion, and what needs to be strictly controlled at the system level? Meanwhile, some harness functions are gradually moving to the provider's cloud infrastructure, while the developer side retains access to local files, command execution, and application interaction. And the more authority an agent has, the more important it is to understand what actions it can perform independently.

Other presentations discussed the protection of AI assistants and the reliability of agent pipelines. If a task consists of several stages, a failure at one stage can ruin the entire result. Therefore, it's necessary to check not only the final answer but also how the agent progresses through intermediate steps.

So, Is It Time to Change Professions or Fight On?

At GigaConf, much was said about how AI is changing the developer profession. But I wouldn't take the thesis "code is no longer written by hand" literally. Rather, what our working time is spent on is changing.

For a backend developer, this leads to three practical conclusions. First, it's worth not just using an AI assistant in the IDE but understanding how agents, their tools, and context work. Second, you need to learn to describe requirements in such a way that the result can be verified: not "do it well," but specific contracts, constraints, and acceptance criteria. And of course, without understanding operations, none of this works: the code must not only compile but also function properly in production.

And one more thing: don't forget about cost. Additional agents, context, and self-improvement loops can improve quality, but you have to pay for them—with tokens, infrastructure, and maintenance complexity. I left GigaConf with a specific task to deeply dive into harnesses, as the ability to control multi-agent systems and prevent them from hallucinating will soon become the main skill.
