Artificial intelligence is undoubtedly a productivity tool. But productivity is not a good in itself: if the neural network user has gaps in their competencies that they are unaware of, multiplied by the neural network's productivity, they will simply produce more garbage results.

In 2023, Harvard Business School and BCG conducted an experiment and gave real tasks to 758 consultants. When consultants used their strengths, such as writing or generating ideas, AI became an assistant and improved speed and quality.

However, in one of the business cases, it was required to compare a table with interview records and identify inconsistencies. In this situation, consultants working with a neural network provided the correct answer 19 percentage points less often than those who did not use AI in their work.

A neural network cannot always close a knowledge gap, especially if expert information on a narrow topic is required. And it certainly cannot close the blind spot in the competencies of the person consulting it. On the contrary, the default "obligingness" embedded in modern neural networks is more likely to become a tool for amplifying misconceptions on a cosmic scale with corresponding consequences. Human incompetence, multiplied by the neural network's efficiency and productivity, is a formula for disaster, and the cost of error in this formula will be proportional to the human's authority and degree of responsibility. Therefore, managerial decisions are at the greatest risk in terms of unskilled interaction with AI at work.

Danger for Managers

A manager's job is to connect the work of other employees. Team productivity is synergistic—meaning the result of teamwork is greater than the sum of individual efforts. This synergy is the key to collective success, but it also harbors risks. Therefore, the effectiveness of feedback within the team has always been considered the foundation of an effective team organization. But management schemes developed for humans and tested on teams composed entirely of humans (it's surprising that this sentence even needs to be written—as if it could have been any other way before!) can "break" when AI is integrated into the decision-making and execution chain. This increases the likelihood that a managerial error may not be discovered until the last moment. And all this time, the LLM embedded in the workflow, if relied upon too heavily, will continue to cause systematic harm.

Workslop Instead of Results

Neural networks love to incorporate tables into work. They can be good for systematizing the most important things, but sometimes artificial intelligence suggests them simply because it's convenient for it. For example, a manager creates a bunch of documents with the help of a neural network in one evening, and in the morning sends them out to employees, announcing a new management system for the company.

This is an example of how involving a neural network without sufficient team engagement and their necessary expertise on the company's actual needs and problems breaks the feedback loop essential for competent management. Decisions made unilaterally and imposed from above create only an illusion of control. The manager might think: "A lot of work has been done, things are definitely going to improve now." In reality, the neural network has simply processed the existing information and presented it in a new format, but the manager's job is to manage relationships between people. If people—employees, team members—are excluded from the decision-making process, management becomes detached from reality. A neural network cannot replace a manager's work with people, because that is what management is all about. And managers need to maintain this understanding.

Such a temptation can arise, for example, in matters of integrating new management methods and IT solutions into a company: previously, implementing something new at the business level required a lot of time and money, and expensive services from integrators and consultants. Consulting and integration is one area where a manager might naturally consider saving both time and money with the help of AI.

In 1981, Feldman and March identified that organizations often request more information than they actually need. All of this also helps create an illusion of control, although obtaining an answer does not equate to reading it and taking action. And with the new, no-joke, amazing capabilities and, one might even say, "zeal" of AI to work with information, vast amounts of data, both input and output, this tendency towards information overload can, in reality, paralyze an organization's operations.

And studies conducted in the AI era show that AI implementation sometimes creates new problems without solving old ones. For instance, in 2024, Upwork surveyed 2500 people. The result: 96% of top managers expect AI to increase productivity. At the same time, 77% of employees say their workload has actually increased.

In 2025, Stanford and BetterUp called this “workslop,” meaning content that looks like work but actually shifts the work onto the recipient. The most unpleasant part is that you can return such work to a colleague and discuss a solution, but a manager may not receive feedback.

A Neural Network Doesn't Understand When It Makes a Mistake

This is a critically important understanding for working with AI: artificial intelligence can be used for verification and control—but it must be verified and controlled itself, because a neural network does not realize it is making a mistake the way humans do. An LLM will provide a perfectly correct answer and a detailed, elaborate hallucination with the same confident tone. And if the person receiving this answer lacks independent expertise in the subject, they will accept it as truth.

  1. Neural networks are very good at generating highly plausible, detailed, complex, and completely fictional answers. In 2024, Stanford RegLab asked three models verifiable questions about court cases: 69% to 88% of the answers were fabrications, and the models did not correct false premises in the questions themselves. Thus, a manager might assign a task to verify some information and, upon receiving a false answer, not suspect an error.

  2. Partly, AI hallucinations are the flip side of the neural network's desire to be helpful. A neural network readily "agrees" with its user. In late 2023, Anthropic tested five leading assistants: all adjusted their answers to the user's opinion because, during training, people more often chose a convincing answer that matched their position. Artificial intelligence tends to offer more arguments "for" the idea you are proposing. Such an opinion would be difficult to call objective.

  3. A neural network is good at presenting a generated answer as finished work. Often, the result of a neural network query can look like something ready to be sent. In 2025, Deloitte Australia submitted a report to the government for 440,000 Australian dollars with fabricated sources and a fictitious court quote. The internal review of the Big Four missed it. The errors were found by an external person who—pay attention!—understood the subject.

From all of the above, one conclusion can be drawn: the best neural network cannot replace a person's own competencies and expertise if they themselves do not understand the subject matter.

Large Volume of Work Done ≠ Result

A neural network delivers finished work in minutes, or even seconds. The brain is easily fooled, so it considers the task done, and the thought arises, "Wow, I did this." The motivation to re-check the result disappears almost immediately. When doing work independently, a person, while doing it much slower than a machine, also checks their own work in the process. That is, self-checking is built into the human work process. Checking the voluminous output of a neural network, however, turns into a separate task requiring considerable concentration and, again, time.

And if we compare not the time to complete the work itself, but the time to complete the work with verification, it may turn out that the time to complete AI work with subsequent step-by-step human verification is not so different from the time a human works with self-checking built into the process.

In the summer of 2025, the research organization METR conducted an experiment with 16 experienced developers. They were given 246 real tasks from their own projects. Some were to be completed with AI, others independently. Here's what the researchers found:

It was expected that work would speed up by 24%. According to employee perceptions, work sped up by 20%. According to actual measurements, work slowed down by 19%.

The difference between how participants estimated their speed and what the measurements showed was almost 40 percentage points. This can be particularly dangerous for a manager, as they cannot measure performance through tests or code reviews like a programmer. The consequences of their inefficient work may only become apparent after several months.

Consequences of Thoughtless AI Implementation

As a result, implementing artificial intelligence without control can lead to the following:

Strong employees are the first to decide to leave the team. Neural networks are not flexible enough. They do not account for the fact that a team may consist of people with different skills, abilities, tasks, and resources. For example, a strong and experienced specialist can accomplish more in a limited time than an entire team. However, this will not be reflected in the scoring. On the contrary, it will seem like they are spending less time than everyone else. It will be easier for the neural network to assess quantity than quality.

Increase in reports during crisis times. When an error occurs, a manager may start requesting more reports, thinking it will help them regain control of the situation. As a result, they may spend a lot of time reading information that does not actually provide a solution to the problem. While the ship is sinking, the neural network will be measuring the speed at which it is doing so.

Delayed negative effect. A large amount of information and the appearance of work can create an "activity illusion" for a long time and distract the manager and the entire team from real problems. However, when reviewing results after a long period, it may turn out that efficiency has actually decreased.

The Solution to Neural Network Efficiency Problems Lies with People

Ultimately, it all comes back to one thing: the problems with neural network efficiency are actually problems with how people interact with neural networks. And it's not the AI that needs "treatment"—it's improving your own competence: competence in working with AI, and, most importantly, your own competence in the work for which you use the neural network.

Rest assured: if you don't understand the subject, the model will deceive you. Only real human experience will help you find and correct errors in time. An incompetent employee or manager won't know where to expect pitfalls, and the apparent, illusory competence of AI will only prolong the time until the error is realized, by which point it may have become systemic, and its consequences catastrophic.

The rules are simple:

  1. A neural network can help make decisions, but it does not absolve responsibility for them. The final decision is made by a person—and the responsibility for it also lies with that person. All examples where people were caught using AI-generated hallucinated results in their work are examples of their professional incompetence, a stain on their professional reputation—not on the neural network.

  2. Seek advice from a specialist if you are not an expert in a particular topic. Furthermore, do not neglect human expertise if you have a relevant specialist on your team.

  3. Do not try to use a neural network to mask your own uncertainty/ignorance—this will only increase the cost of error. Attempting to hide your incompetence with AI only escalates the situation and will ultimately lead to exposure. No one is an expert in everything; on the contrary, everyone is ignorant in everything except what they understand. The foundation of professionalism lies in understanding one's limitations.

  4. Ask the neural network to explain. All of the above does not mean that in any unclear situation with a neural network, you should shift the work to others. In any case, working with AI requires a certain discipline: ask for detailed explanations, analyze, seek external sources, and double-check data. Set tasks for the AI in such a way that it is forced to double-check itself.

  5. Work with a neural network independently only where you are definitely competent—or trust the neural network at your own risk.

Uncontrolled implementation of a neural network into your work or life can only cause harm—but this is a general rule: uncontrolled implementation of anything can only cause harm—and artificial intelligence is no exception. And fundamentally, nothing changes: your life/work is your responsibility.
