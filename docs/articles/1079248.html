How the Idea Came About

KORUS is already actively working with young specialists: business units launch internships for students based on their needs, and agreements are in place with specialized universities — this is an important requirement in the context of maintaining IT accreditation. The summer internship was conceived as a month-long sprint during which students go through a real product development journey, learn to brief a client, and work with new AI tools.

👉Why this format was chosen:

1. It maximally approximates real work conditions. Interns don't just complete isolated academic tasks; they work in groups on a specific project and go through the entire process: from gathering client needs to presenting the finished product.

2. It provides an opportunity to try different roles. Students get to understand how product development works, practice teamwork, and, of course, improve their AI skills.

3. Neural networks have opened up new possibilities in the last year or two. Now, there's no need to invent academic tasks — interns can be immersed in working with business cases that have real client needs. AI helps bridge the gap of inexperience, allows for faster learning, and enables the team to create a fully functional solution in four weeks.

Maxim Sumin

Head of the Corporate University at KORUS

“For us, this was a great opportunity to make the internship much more practical and bring it as close as possible to what people encounter in real work.”

Project clients were found within the company — business units formulated tasks themselves that could be conveniently solved with AI.

How the Selection Process Went

For the KORUS Team

Daniil Anuprienko

HR Manager

Daniil shares how they looked for students and by what criteria they selected them: “The target profile is second to fourth-year university students, full-time IT majors. After applying, we offered a short interview with an AI at their convenience, lasting no more than ten minutes. The candidates answered questions day and night, whenever they felt comfortable. We wanted to understand their motivation and expectations from the internship.”

The next stage was a test assignment, which required:

  1. Creating an HTML page with research on a chosen topic using AI.

  2. Recording a short video introduction — talking about themselves or on a free topic.

Daniil notes that the technical aspect was less important in the test than the conscientiousness of the approach: “Critical thinking is perhaps one of the most important skills when working with AI. We encountered submissions that looked good at first glance but, upon closer inspection, were clearly done quickly and without verification — broken links, contradictions in the text. Nobody needs the output of a hallucinating language model. Working with AI begins precisely after you get a result from it.”

A key criterion was that all participants should have a similar level of understanding of working with AI. Based on the selection from about fifty applicants, four teams were formed. Students from three of these teams participated in preparing the article.

Students Recall the Selection Process

Ilya initially applied for Sales Camp — an internship in KORUS sales. But during the breaks between selection stages, he learned about the new internship program, the AI Lab, from Daniil. “Knowing that I was applying for sales with a technical background, I thought this format would be more interesting,” Ilya explains.

“The first test assignment, which required conducting research, was more engaging due to the creative approach. But the video introduction was harder. I thought I could do it without cuts or editing. I was wrong. I ended up making about ten cuts. The questions were easy, but answering them on camera for recording turned out to be not so simple,” recalls the intern.

Artyom was purposefully looking for internships — through hh.ru, Telegram channels, and company websites. “I was looking for programs with real business projects. I wanted to go through the entire process from initial idea, client communication, and development to presentation and defense of the final product,” he explains.

Recalling the selection process, he says: “I used AI for 10% of the page, and did 90% myself. Of course, I double-checked all sources. But in the end, I honestly wrote that I didn't have time to check the last part of the site, so I just inserted the code. I received feedback that my approach to completing the task was appreciated. They invited me for an internship.”

In addition to creating the website and video introduction, Ksyusha specifically mentioned the resume-building stage: “It was my first time ever doing it. I was worried because I had no work experience, only academic and pet projects. I had previously done vibecoding: created congratulatory websites and mini-games for friends. I think this experience helped me move forward.”

Moreover, Ksyusha was already familiar with KORUS's culture. Her mother has worked at the company for a long time, and Ksyusha herself had visited the KORUS office before. According to her, there was always a friendly and interesting atmosphere there, so the internship itself didn't seem intimidating.

Impressions of Teamwork

In Ilya's team, roles were clearly defined — some worked on the technical part of the service, others on the logic and calculations in Excel.

“I like to identify people's strengths — to understand what they can do, what they enjoy more. I asked the guys about their interests and aspirations. I assigned roles mostly based on their answers. The project is extensive, so it wasn't difficult to assign everyone their part.”

Ilya himself took on the role of distributing tasks among the team, developing the overall project logic, and creating Excel files — he shaped the overall structure of the tables, figured out which data was needed and which wasn't, and managed communication with the client.

In Artyom's team, all four members were developers, each assigned a part of the code for the assistant's functionality. Roles were further distributed by competencies: business analyst — the one with client communication experience, interviewer — the one with interview experience, product manager — the one who worked with the product. “I considered myself a developer and tester. I wrote my part of the code and checked how everything worked,” says Artyom.

Ksyusha's team consisted of four girls. “I was the youngest in the cohort. When everyone was added to the group chat and asked to introduce themselves, the others wrote that they were finishing their bachelor's degrees or studying for master's degrees, and I had just finished my first year. It was a bit awkward. The team was good, but establishing joint work turned out to be difficult: everyone had different ideas about how the result should look. We discussed controversial decisions and tried to justify our positions. If someone suggested something, it was important to explain why that particular option should be chosen,” recalls the intern.

First Leadership Experience

For some reason, it's commonly thought that internships are a time to put your leadership potential aside. Yes, formally, no one appoints you as a leader during internships or tells you that you specifically should take responsibility for the project. You build collaborative work yourself and decide whether to wait for someone else to make a move or make it yourself. Artyom and Ilya chose the latter.

Artyom Dvornikov

AI Lab Intern

“Contacting the client was initially scary for the whole team. We were afraid, unsure how to communicate. No one took the initiative to reach out. Everyone thought, ‘Well, I hope someone will do it.’ One of the colleagues contacted the client, but was in a different time zone, which made prompt communication difficult. I saw this and decided to take the initiative myself and wrote to them,” says Artyom.

After that, he became the one who communicated with the client and synchronized the team: “For the most part, I initiated ideas in development, distributed tasks, we completed them, then we all reviewed. I was responsible for the final check that everything was working well, after which I sent the intermediate result to the client.”

The last three days before the defense, the team finished coding and testing. “The atmosphere in our team is great, we all became friends. I was lucky with the guys,” adds Artyom.

Ilya Malyshev

AI Lab Intern

Ilya describes his approach to leadership: “Usually, I try to be not just an executor, but also someone who distributes tasks, finds out if a certain feature is needed. A lot depends on my opinion. But I don't dictate terms — I like to listen to the guys, understand their strengths. Based on their interests, opinions, and aspirations, we chose who would do what on the project.”

Internship Is Not Just About Code

In parallel with working on projects, the Corporate University conducted soft skills development trainings for all teams.

The goal of the trainings was to support and develop soft skills during the implementation of team projects. Therefore, the selection was based on their stages. A total of four activities were held:

  1. Mini-training “Building Effective Communication with Clients. Preparing for Client Interviews.”

  2. TEAM CANVAS session: setting up team work (Goal — Roles — Interaction — Improvement).

  3. Mini-training “Public Speaking. How to Present Your Project Clearly and Confidently.”

  4. Mini-retrospective session on project implementation outcomes.

Each training included techniques and tools that students could apply immediately to enhance their product.

Anna Usikova

HR Development and Training Manager

“I was delighted to see the enthusiasm of the guys, their willingness to make mistakes and experiment, to ask for feedback and consider it. Most teams performed exceptionally well — both in client interviews and presentations. And the discussion of teamwork in two sessions helped the guys draw realistic lessons about working in a team and reallocate tasks. A plus of working with such an audience is their flexibility and ability to absorb knowledge on the fly,” says Anna Usikova, HR Development and Training Manager.

Although the students did not have personal mentors, they could always ask Maxim or Daniil questions if something didn't work out or they were unsure how to approach a solution. On one hand, it was a space for independence; on the other, this format is not comfortable for everyone at the start of their careers.

Ilya, Artyom, and Ksyusha noted that the lack of step-by-step control provided freedom. But with freedom came responsibility: they had to manage their own time and resources. During the general interim meeting, it was evident that all teams were progressing at their own pace and coping. This meant that strict control was not necessary.

A Look from the Curators' Perspective

Daniil sees the value of the AI Lab in several aspects: “For students, this is real experience. They stumbled in the same places as experienced colleagues: the client doesn't respond immediately, there are different opinions within the team, there's never enough time. The main thing is that they learned to find solutions not in theory, but in practice. For many, the internship was their first introduction to the corporate world. I remember receiving messages at night and on weekends at the beginning, and closer to the end, everything happened strictly during working hours. Such small things — not being afraid to write to reception, asking colleagues for help, contacting Service Desk — form a sense of a safe corporate environment. When a new employee quickly feels comfortable working, that is perhaps one of the most important values.”

Maxim admits that before the start, he thought students were not fully prepared for real business tasks. But after the first meeting with the guys, he changed his mind: “I was surprised by their readiness to get involved and the experience many came with. Some had already developed their own products, others worked while studying. At some point, I even had to think about how to complicate the task — the overall level of the groups turned out to be higher than I expected.”

Nevertheless, the short internship format has its peculiarities. It's quite difficult to simultaneously work on a real project and seriously focus on learning.

“I would have liked to give the guys more practical tools, to analyze what didn't work out in more detail. There were mistakes in both the work and team interaction. This is also an important part of the experience. But the AI Lab is a very fast format. There simply wasn't enough time for deep reflection. The first thing I want to reconfigure for the future is the start, make it shorter and more intensive to move to development faster. Teamwork should also be shifted to the very beginning: set up roles and agreements earlier, before difficulties arise,” adds Maxim.
