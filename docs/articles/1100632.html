In Brief

In this essay, I introduce the concept of decoherence, which is the accumulation of models in a system that are individually workable but incompatible with each other or redundant. In such a system, the main cost of changes falls on reconciling these models and preventing unintended side effects.

Two directions of development for a single system. Decoherence grows on the left, coherence is maintained on the right. Static composition of the author's original diagrams.

In other words, it's what happens when a low-quality mess drowns out a useful signal in a vast sea of noise.

Decoherence grows faster the faster the system changes, and the less coherence it has, the more this process accelerates. This creates uncontrolled exponential growth. This phenomenon is not new. Humans have always resisted it, thoughtfully seeking ways to achieve more with less and integrating disparate approaches and models into a cohesive whole. However, the widespread use of AI is now generating decoherence at such a speed that it cannot continue for long.

The good news is that this eliminates the frightening scenario of uncontrolled recursive self-improvement, the very intelligence explosion where the growth curve shoots up before anyone can react. Decoherence prevents AI from improving in an isolated "bubble" and limits its development speed outside this bubble. The bad news is that we are hurtling towards another catastrophe, decoherence collapse.

Imagine a building, say a small office center. When it was built, pipes were used that wear out over time and eventually require replacement. This is ordinary infrastructure debt, or, as it's commonly called, natural wear and tear.

Now, suppose that by the time you need to replace the pipes, you have to switch to a new standard. Perhaps building codes have changed, old pipes are no longer manufactured, or new ones are much better and easier to maintain. This is ordinary technology development debt, which arises from the need to update and switch to new solutions as technology progresses.

There is a third type of debt that can arise depending on how exactly you transition from old pipes to new ones. Consider the following options.

  1. Close the building and replace all pipes at once. This is an expensive method that seriously disrupts normal operations, as the building cannot be used during repairs, and the entire replacement must be paid for upfront. However, by the end of the repairs, the transition will be fully completed.

  2. Close floors one by one and replace pipes on each. This is generally less disruptive to operations but costs slightly more, as payment is still required in advance, and maintaining normal building operations during repairs incurs additional labor costs.

  3. Replace pipes as needed, when they start to fail. This option is the least disruptive and allows costs to be spread over time, replacing the entire system gradually, one small repair at a time.

Most people find the third option very attractive. It allows normal operations to be maintained and spreads maintenance costs over time. And if you can do it like the Ship of Theseus [1] and replace pipes one by one, why not do so?

Well, if only because it creates decoherence.

And decoherence is, by a wide margin, the worst type of technical debt.

Evolution

The Ship of Theseus helps to understand the true scale of decoherence. We know how to build wooden ships and how to build steel ones. If you repair a vessel piece by piece, using the same material as in the original thought experiment, it will remain functional, and all the design decisions embedded in it will be preserved.

But do we know how to build all the intermediate variants between a wooden ship and a steel one? Is it possible, for example, to transform a sailing warship into a modern destroyer by gradually replacing its parts? And do so in such a way that it remains functional at every step, at least in some sense of the word?

The original Ship of Theseus, intermediate hybrids, and the final design. Four illustrations from the original are combined into one sequence.

I won't say the answer is an unconditional "no." If you use enough imagination and involve the best minds, some path will surely be found. But it will require ingenuity, skills, and mastery. It will require attention to detail. And, most importantly, you will likely want to complete the transition, because a vessel that is half a sailing warship and half a destroyer will probably not perform very well in modern combat.

Let's go back to the pipes. This strategy is constantly encountered in construction. When a pipe bursts, a plumber is called, the wall is opened, a solution is found for the specific section, and then everything is sealed up again. If you're lucky, the plumber will leave detailed notes. If you're unlucky, a few years later, some poor soul will open that wall and ask themselves, "What the hell is going on here?"

Now imagine this repeating year after year as we gradually replace pipes throughout the building. The result will be a patchwork mix of old and new pipes, significantly more complex than either of the two systems individually. Each joint, each patch fitted in place, will have its own unique, poorly understood combination of stresses and properties. To fully understand and work with it requires a specialist who knows both systems.

At first glance, everything seems fine. The pipes work, so what difference does it make how exactly? If the patch is good, who cares?

But what happens if we want to, say, build an extension?

As part of a thought experiment, let's assume that water needs to be supplied to the extension through a part of the existing building, using the existing pipes. And, importantly, this will require increasing the water pressure throughout the system.

The question arises: is it safe to increase the pressure?

If all the pipes were of type A or type B, even after multiple repairs and replacements of individual sections, the answer would be straightforward. Both systems are well-studied, and for both, there are tools and methods for calculating permissible loads, and people constantly perform such calculations.

But if we have an unknown mixture of two systems, the answer suddenly requires a much deeper analysis. Any patch can alter the outcome, even on the opposite side of the building, because everything is interconnected.

Increasing pressure affects the entire system. Non-standard joints between different types of pipes become independent sources of risk.

This is precisely where the true complexity of decoherence becomes apparent. To modify a decoherent system requires much more knowledge and skill than working with each of the original systems separately. The engineer has to trace connections much more carefully and understand its structure, because changes can have unintended consequences. (And yes, I consider plumbers engineers, certainly no less than developers. Have you seen how they figure out pressure forces in drain pipes on the fly? Impressive.)

This is not a simple task, and it's best not to rush it. But an outside observer, who doesn't grasp the depth of the problem, might find such slowness irritating. Usually, everything is easy at this stage. Why is it taking so long this time?

In such a situation, management often starts pressuring the specialist, demanding faster completion. But under pressure, they don't conduct the full analysis faster; they simply stop earlier and do what they believe should work. A less-than-perfect joint, a non-standard part, perhaps some extra safety measures just in case.

However, unfinished work triggers a chain reaction. A specific change turns out to be less качественным and makes the system more fragile and complex. This means the next one will require even more attention and thought, and if the pressure on the engineer remains the same, they will do the job even worse.

The picture is already quite unpleasant, and you can discern a kind of exponential growth in complexity. But wait, it gets worse! Consider the next level of decoherence, when the second transition is started before the first is finished.

The Labyrinth

With software, unlike a building or other physical systems, there's one difficulty. It's sort of invisible.

Programmers walk through the corridors of codebases every day. We navigate this labyrinth so often successfully that we know every corner and every turn. And when our routes lengthen, when they contain more corridors, dead ends, and secret hatches, we feel it immediately, almost intuitively.

The labyrinth that a developer navigates every day. The animation of the original diagram has been replaced by a static frame.

But explaining this to people who don't even see the labyrinth and don't walk through it with you every day is difficult. When it's time to pay off some technical debt, to make the routes shorter and less winding, convincing management of the necessity of such work can be challenging, even if the managers themselves once wandered through labyrinths. After all, none of them know your labyrinth. They don't know what challenges you face every time you enter it.

But our familiar plumber can show the building owner the absurdity of what's happening with the pipes much more graphically.

Photo from r/Plumbing: "Am I being too picky with the plumbing work in my new house?" [2]

When we start mixing more than two systems, chaotically adding more patches over time, the complexity of the labyrinth explodes. It becomes combinatorial, as we now have to consider A → B, A → C, B → C, A → B → C, C → B → A, and so on.

Of course, we can shield engineers from some of this complexity. We can develop best practices, use tools and frameworks to isolate one part of the system from another (e.g., install a pressure reducing valve in the pipes). But inevitably, a point comes when even the best engineers, even the smartest people, can no longer navigate the labyrinth safely.

Perhaps they succeed in 80 or 90 percent of cases. But one day they will take a wrong turn,

forget to leave a guiding thread behind and get stuck
in an immense labyrinth,
that grows endlessly,
outpacing their every step,
forever.

The decoherence trap: the system becomes too large and complex to grasp entirely.

This is the decoherence trap. The labyrinth grows and grows until it consumes you whole and leaves you wandering in the dark. Until it becomes so vast that it takes entire lifetimes to navigate.

At least, human lifetimes. But what about AI?

Simulation

Humans get tired and hit physical limitations, while swarms of autonomous agents can work around the clock, collaboratively and tirelessly mapping the labyrinth, like obedient little robots. If the problem is scale, can't we just add computing power and be done with it?

It all depends on the complexity we face. How does it grow?

  1. Does it grow linearly with the overall size of the codebase?

  2. Or does it grow exponentially, so that each change increases the probability that the next one will add even more complexity?

In the first case, yes, as the task grows, we can add agents and data centers and solve it at roughly the same pace. But in the second case, additional power will only allow us to advance a little further before the labyrinth grows so large that it threatens to consume all the computing resources on Earth (and in space, if SpaceX's plans [3] come to fruition).

In my experience, the second case is true. When a plumber opens a wall and sees a mess, they don't understand what they're looking at. They might take a type A pipe, a type B pipe, or, without understanding, add a completely new type. This is even more true with code.

This can be seen in the simple simulation below. Here, the graph represents a codebase (or other complex system), where:

  • Nodes represent components.

  • The size of a node corresponds to the size of the component.

  • The color of a node indicates the predominant paradigm within it, i.e., the "type" of solution, be it pipe material, code structure, or system variety.

  • Edges represent connections between components.

A participant in the simulation, representing a developer, implements a "feature" by traversing a random section of the graph and then modifying it. The basic behavior of this developer defines the coherence factor.

  • Low factor means the developer quickly grasps the task and makes decisions on the fly. They prefer simple solutions, don't consider the surrounding context, and generally increase the graph's complexity.

  • High factor means the developer spends more time and carefully considers each task, thus being more likely to make a less complex change or even reduce complexity.

Initially, a developer with a low factor progresses very quickly compared to a more thoughtful colleague. But changes to the graph also affect their traversal. Three factors slow down progress.

  1. The greater the number of connections a node has (its degree). To understand the influence of neighboring nodes on the current one, one must at least partially consider the context of each, which adds extra load.

  2. Transitions between colors. Switching between paradigms when implementing a feature requires extra effort, as one needs to re-understand each paradigm and how they are connected.

  3. Path length. The more connections and transitions, the higher the probability that the route will need to be extended. The developer needs to read more code and revisit previously viewed sections more often to gather all necessary context. The path can grow until it encompasses the entire system. In other words, to change anything, one needs to understand everything.

If you plot the number of implemented features against time and coherence factor, you get a picture with the golden mean familiar to any engineer.

In this golden mean, the system is relatively coherent, and we deliberately invest in keeping it that way. It has several predominant paradigms, but enough variability for things to change and adapt over time. At the same time, we don't try to perfect every little detail.

Above the golden mean, we put enormous effort into ensuring each new node fits into existing paradigms and eliminate complexity wherever possible. This takes much more time and slows down feature development, but even in the worst case, it leaves us with a highly coherent system that can be developed further relatively quickly.

Below the golden mean, a collapse begins. The codebase becomes exponentially more complex, and we rapidly lose speed. If the coherence factor is negative and no effort is made to maintain coherence, the feature release time grows, seemingly without any limits.

You can also see how different strategies affect the system over time, depending on its initial state. If you start with a very coherent system and choose a strategy that primarily destroys coherence (a factor of +0.2, where some effort is still made to maintain it, but short paths are also used), the graph will initially degrade slowly, then pass through several tipping points and fall into the trap.

Conversely, if you take an initially incoherent system and consistently invest in restoring its coherence, you can reverse the trend and accelerate.

Critics might point out that the weights in this simulation are arbitrary and it doesn't prove that real complex systems will evolve this way. I am not claiming that.

The simulation shows that such a curve can arise from simple rules and local decisions. Those who change the system today can make it easier or harder for those who change it tomorrow. This is an obvious truth, but, in my opinion, its consequences are easily missed if one cannot literally see the complexity, as in the physical world with its pipes, wires, and tangible equipment.

So, back to the question of agents. Can they work with such systems and maintain coherence? Or do they, over time, still introduce decoherence, even if little by little? Here, we need to consider another fundamental risk. Decoherence grows not with the passage of time, but with changes.

If you build a decoherent system today, don't change it for ten years, and then return to it, it will be exactly as decoherent as you left it. Other types of technical debt may arise, but they are unrelated to decoherence. Decoherence increases or decreases only when changes are made to the system.

And if you make many changes very quickly, decoherence can also grow very rapidly. Even a very small increase at each step can get out of control before we notice it.

We have encountered this phenomenon for much, much longer than agent-based development has existed. In all the companies I've ever worked for, there has been a huge amount of decoherence. Less experienced engineers and engineers under pressure also tend to increase it over time, so people are far from perfect here.

Moreover, I venture to suggest that most companies are noticeably below the golden mean and live in a constant cycle:

  1. Decoherence grows to the point where no feature can be completed in a reasonable time.

  2. Significant resources are allocated to reduce it.

  3. The efforts yield results, and features start coming out at a slow but acceptable pace.

  4. The work is not completed. Or, if it is, it still doesn't reach the golden mean. Resources and attention are shifted elsewhere, and the system begins to lose coherence again.

And so it goes in a circle. Some companies restore coherence faster than others and sustain these efforts longer. Very few manage to stay consistently in the golden mean, but this requires an engineering culture consistently focused on high quality (Apple comes to mind; whatever you say about the current state of macOS, I'd prefer it over Windows any day).

The question is whether agents are already handling this better than a regular engineering team with its established culture. And significantly better?

So far, we haven't seen confirmation of this. Rather, the first studies point in the opposite direction.

A Harvard study [4] covering 718 companies showed that the introduction of programming agents increased the volume of code written by 30%, but did not yield a measurable increase in completed work. Instead, the additional code accumulated in reviews. Checking pull requests took 49% longer, and the number of returned items for rework nearly doubled.

A Carnegie Mellon University study [5] on open-source projects that adopted Cursor found a sharp increase in development speed that proved short-lived, and an increase in code complexity that, conversely, persisted. Moreover, this additional complexity itself became one of the main reasons for the subsequent slowdown.

Finally, an analysis of over 300,000 AI-generated commits in more than 6,000 repositories [6] showed that approximately every eleventh commit, and for some assistants up to every seventh, introduced at least one quality issue. Almost a quarter of these issues remain in the code to this day.

More code, the same amount of completed work, and more complexity after each change.

Progress

Now let's address the main objection, the argument about technological progress. It sounds something like this:

The first generation of agents will write lower-quality code, but as long as it's "good enough," agents will still be able to replace humans in most programming tasks. And we can build processes around them, expecting that more capable agents will emerge in the future.

Essentially, this is the story of automation during the Industrial Revolution, when the first textile machines indeed worked much worse than humans, but eventually caught up. As Cory Doctorow noted [7], industrialists rushed to implement automation because it allowed them to subject workers to stricter discipline and concentrate power in their hands at the expense of workers and society. Several generations had to endure much worse working conditions and lower-quality goods. [8] [9]

"Engels' Pause": output per worker and real wages did not grow simultaneously. Original author's graph based on Robert Allen's work [9]; captions translated.

But I want to expand on this idea because, for the textile industry, this approach worked for one fundamental reason. The machines produced inferior products, but they met the minimum acceptable quality standard.

The machines themselves were very well designed for their tasks. I recently spoke with a mechanical engineer who spoke with enthusiasm about the quality of these generations of machine tools, how well they worked and how long they lasted. Yes, they might have produced low-grade goods, but they did so quickly, tirelessly, and for many, many years, until they were replaced by the next, slightly improved generation. And, crucially, while the machine was running, decoherence did not increase. Wear and tear – yes. Technological generation changes – yes. But not decoherence.

And here's the catch. This argument assumes that code is analogous to fabric, i.e., it's the final product that users are willing to pay for. But no one wants to buy code. People want to buy applications.

In other words, code is not fabric, but a loom. Every time a screen is rendered, a form is submitted, or a record is updated, the very "product" that the code produces is made. This is precisely why software has become so profitable, as each additional customer does not require additional overhead. The raw material is bits and bytes, represented by electrical signals in microcircuits, and the product is the information and processes encoded in these bits.

A more accurate analogy would be if we somehow went back in time and created not just a loom, but a machine that designs looms. One that develops the next generations of textile equipment itself, semi-autonomously or perhaps fully autonomously. Nothing like this has existed before, and there is no precedent for it in the history of automation.

This is the dream of recursive self-improvement. Of an intelligence explosion, of a technological singularity [10]. This is the dream that originally stood behind AI.

And how are things progressing on this path?

Singularity

Well, the bar for success is slowly shifting. In May 2026, Tom Reed, a skeptic of the intelligence explosion theory, proposed the term "Goodhart Singularity" [11]. He used this to describe the limitations of AI that improves within an isolated bubble, without receiving new data about the real world. It is based on Goodhart's Law [12]:

When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.

Marilyn Strathern, paraphrasing Charles Goodhart.

In a September publication, Tom Davidson, Senior Research Scientist at Forethought [13], acknowledged that data scarcity will slow progress [14] when AI reaches what he calls the "human-quality ceiling." At this point, there will no longer be enough of the best examples of human work for training, and AI will have to create data itself that surpasses existing quality. Davidson has written extensively about AI takeoff speed models, and later forecasts, particularly the AI 2027 scenario [15], were based on his work.

Therefore, even a slight shift in Davidson's position deserves attention. But he argues that it's a one-time slowdown, not an insurmountable wall. Overall, proponents of rapid takeoff still believe that AI will be able to recursively improve itself with a relatively small amount of data, and that this process could spiral out of control in an isolated data center, leading to the birth of superintelligence.

They are counting on the so-called Software Intelligence Explosion (SIE) [16]. There is one subtle point in Davidson's text on data scarcity that this argument largely relies on:

Instead of reinforcement learning environments where AI learns to create software products for the real world, it should build environments that train it to create software better than any existing in the real world.

I'll say right away, I think this is possible! Coming up with complex tasks and checking answers to them is easier than solving these tasks. Even today, human experts create reinforcement learning environments that provide data above the human-quality ceiling. The quality of data will not be limited by this ceiling.

Let's break this down.

Davidson says that AI must be able to create software better than anything existing in the real world while remaining largely isolated from it. To do this, AI will need to build a reinforcement learning environment [17] that allows it to produce such software. In general terms, the process looks like this:

  1. Define the goal.

  2. Define the set of allowed actions.

  3. Use an algorithm to try many ways of achieving the goal.

  4. Measure how close it came to the goal and how good the chosen method is.

  5. Find the best method or several best methods.

For such an intelligence explosion to occur, AI must perform this entire cycle independently, discovering ever more sophisticated learning algorithms. This is precisely what is important. It's not about higher-quality software itself, but about higher-quality learning and improvement algorithms.

The Goodhart Singularity argument essentially states that for most meaningful real-world tasks, AI, isolated in a data center, will be unable to complete this cycle due to the measurement stage. To design a better car, rocket, or microchip, you can build a simulation and test ideas within it, but the results will still need to be verified in the real world. Otherwise, as designs become more extreme, you will drift further from reality. The need for verification limits speed and significantly slows down the process.

Without calibration to reality, the model gradually drifts away from it. Periodic checks allow for deviation correction.

But for some areas, this is not true, which is what Davidson discusses in the second part of the quote. In games like Go or chess [18], the rules of the game define the entire world, and the victory conditions are clearly defined. Therefore, one can work in isolation, find ever stronger strategies, and also improve the algorithms for searching them. The same applies to more complex games, such as StarCraft [19] or Dota [20].

But these are games, and this approach is possible because the victory conditions are clear and simple, and the rules or environment are highly constrained. To achieve an intelligence explosion, these methods must be applied to something truly significant, and the result must be verified within the data center bubble, without reference to the outside world.

In a game, all stages of the loop, including result evaluation, are available within the environment. For real-world tasks, the verification stage extends beyond it.

And here we come to software. It seems to me that SIE proponents have latched onto it precisely because they see it as a way to bridge this gap. Software can operate in isolation, and testing it in isolation is easy and fast. These are ideal conditions for creating a reinforcement learning environment, and they believe that over time we will be able to build such an environment that does not require reference to the real world.

After all, it's software. It can do almost anything, right?

It's not hard to see why people in Silicon Valley find this idea so natural. We pride ourselves on our intelligence and ability to solve big problems. A significant part of computer science education is dedicated to logically proving properties of programs, algorithms, and the like. Will the program terminate? Will this sorting algorithm be fast? How fast? Will the system be secure and not crash in any conceivable case?

We are accustomed to thinking about problems this way and to the idea that software has no limits. Technologies have conquered the world, so it seems natural to assume that if we teach a machine to write code, it will eventually learn to improve itself in programming.

And then, eventually, the machine will conquer the world too.

What Makes Software Better

But this view completely misses coherence, although it is precisely what makes software good.

Let me explain. Software serves as a tool to achieve a goal in the real world. There may be many intermediate steps and levels of abstraction to reach this goal, but ultimately, success is determined by how well the software helps achieve it.

You might think: okay, but if the program already exists and solves the problem, can't we take its result as a benchmark? And then let AI find ever more efficient ways to achieve the same result?

If "better" is understood as something simple and easily measurable, such as lines of code, speed, memory consumption, or efficiency, then yes. All these metrics are important, but if we make them the goal, we get small, fast, and completely incoherent software.

Code becomes faster when it does less work, and it can do without some work only by making assumptions about the rest of the system. For example, that this list is already sorted, or that this value will never be zero. Each such assumption creates a hidden dependency that no one has documented. Optimized native code is not hidden behind abstractions by accident. It's needed for performance, so we lock it in a "box," preventing its assumptions from leaking out. If all code is written this way, it becomes a tangle. (In JavaScript, for example, several logically different classes are combined into one to maintain monomorphism in hot code paths [21]. It's always a hassle, but it pays off as long as nothing needs to be touched.)

Optimized code can be isolated behind an abstraction boundary. If the entire system is structured like such a segment, hidden dependencies become intertwined everywhere.

Everything would be fine if programs only needed to be written once and never revisited. But in practice, this is not the case, because software is like the Ship of Theseus, constantly being rebuilt. Applications, libraries, and frameworks must evolve with the changing conditions of the real world. These conditions change as we reorganize society, learn more about nature, and push the boundaries of what is possible.

Therefore, good software is adaptable over time. You can take the accumulated technological capital (all the software written before) and quickly assemble new combinations from it to solve new problems on the fly.

What makes it adaptive is coherence. Most major achievements in programming and software quality have been related to increasing it, and you can only verify if a system has truly become more coherent through feedback from the real world.

Let's consider this with a specific example: futures [22].

Futures significantly simplify asynchronous programming. Before them, there were many ways to handle asynchronicity, and all of it predictably turned into chaos. More precisely, into a decoherent mess of many locally sensible ways of doing the same thing that didn't fit together at all.

Developers had to live in this mess and carefully, thoughtfully switch between all the varieties of asynchronicity coexisting in one application. Missing a step or forgetting something would lead to elusive errors. As callbacks multiplied, the application grew more complex, and the number of asynchronous operations increased, reading the code itself turned into a horror movie.

The idea of futures emerged in the 1970s and consisted of temporarily representing a value not yet received by a special object. The first concrete implementation appeared in MultiLisp in 1985. But futures didn't become widespread in development until the 2000s, when they were added to Java in 2004. They truly became popular in the 2010s, with the introduction of promises in JavaScript, which can be chained, and Task in C#. [22]

The path from the idea of a future value to widespread adoption took decades and went through MultiLisp, Java, and then JavaScript and C#.

There were two main reasons.

  1. Early future APIs worked but were clumsy. A future could be passed between parts of a program, but attempting to read its value blocked execution, so one had to carefully choose when to read it. Furthermore, different chains could not be independently built from one future, and branching further computations was difficult.

  2. Futures incur noticeable overhead. Each time, a wrapper object tracking state has to be created, instead of, for example, simply storing a pointer to a function.

But over time, we found convenient APIs, smoothed out the rough edges, and realized that the additional overhead was justified because futures make it easier to reason about large systems and connect their parts (reduced computation and memory costs also helped).

Let's return to AI.

To create higher-quality software for AI, one must be able to verify if it is indeed better. And, as we've discussed, this verification must happen within the bubble. Verification outside it is not allowed, otherwise we'll hit the speed limitations imposed by the world again.

What can be verified internally?

We've narrowed it down to three areas:

  • Games.

  • Software.

  • Mathematics.

Games won't get us very far because they are not tied to the real-world tasks that are important to us. So let's move on to software.

As we've established, good software is coherent. But how do you measure coherence?

You need to take an abstraction and see how well it works in different contexts and how much the code has to be refactored to adapt it to each. Since we are forbidden to leave the data center, there are only two ways to do this.

Apply it to all existing software. This will only help up to a certain point, because even good performance with all existing programs doesn't tell you if the abstraction will be as good for all future ones. Callbacks and event loops weren't even widespread until graphical interfaces became the norm, and early web servers still used threads and traditional parallelism. Therefore, the value of futures wasn't obvious until we created and explored the full diversity of programs that make up the web. We will retrain on already known programs. Today, the result may look perfectly coherent, but the very first new task that doesn't fit will require a workaround.

Create new software. In theory, you can try this, but in practice, you can't just generate programs that seem useful to expand the training set. Again, software serves as a tool to achieve a goal in the real world, and if you don't check its usefulness from time to time, you can drift far from reality.

Besides this main problem, there's another important criterion for coherence. You need to understand how well an abstraction or concept holds up over time, across different contexts, and under changing requirements. A standard might look great on paper and even work well in one environment, like desktops, but prove completely unsuitable for mobile devices or large-scale cloud systems.

It would be necessary to generate not only software that seems useful but also plausible scenarios of its evolution, to see how it adapts, what happens when not everyone updates, when people (or agents) fall behind or decide to go a different way, when the entire ecosystem of systems constantly changes like a moving target.

You can become very good at improving the coherence of existing software. But to learn to work better with software we don't have yet, you either need to wait for the real world to create new programs or create them yourself, test them in the real world, and wait for them to go through a certain evolutionary path. Both options require leaving the bubble and returning.

Now let's put it all together.

  1. We can create AI that, while remaining within the bubble, makes software faster, more compact, and more efficient than humans can.

  2. But we cannot create AI that improves software coherence beyond human level without leaving the bubble, for the reasons outlined above.

  3. Since AI cannot verify if the software has become better in a general sense without going outside, it cannot determine if it is truly improving its learning algorithms or simply drifting away from reality.

  4. We arrive again at the Goodhart Singularity.

This alone hammers the nail in the coffin of rapid isolated SIE. But we can approach the issue from another angle, and this argument also applies to other types of intelligence explosions.

  1. Any form of intelligence explosion requires AI to research and improve its own software or hardware, with each new generation building on the improvements of the previous one.

  2. In each generation, there is a certain probability of introducing decoherence, as AI builds or expands systems beyond its current understanding.

  3. If AI moves too fast, decoherence accumulates faster than it can be eliminated. As a result, its overall capability level stops growing or even decreases.

  4. If, however, AI verifies and confirms all its results, its speed is limited by its ability to gather data about the surrounding world.

  5. It is impossible to fix this with more sophisticated learning algorithms created in complete isolation, because AI has nothing to compare their quality against (see the first proof above).

If this is true and AI cannot bypass decoherence, then ultimately its speed is limited by reality. It's not that it will never surpass us, but it's still a long way from truly significant superiority. And even when it achieves it, AI will still have to do all the work that reality demands, subject to the same physical and informational constraints as we are.

Davidson claims that gathering enough data is easy, simply by recording the execution of real tasks:

If the whole world unites to do this quickly, it won't take long. For each profession, a thousand people could record their screens and wear video cameras on their heads, and in four months, AI would gain experience equivalent to three hundred years of work.

This approach fundamentally underestimates the complexity of the world. If we expect a machine to improve itself indefinitely, it's not enough for it to move a cursor on a screen or recognize objects in a video. It needs to deeply understand the world itself. It doesn't necessarily need to experience it the same way a human does, but it needs to obtain the same kind of information, look from the same perspective, and measure elusive properties and nuances that cannot be captured by just a screen recording and video stream.

It must be able to find connections between things that initially seem completely unrelated. Which brings us to the final topic.

Mathematics

There is also mathematics, where, in theory, results can be verified within the bubble, ensuring the correctness of proofs. If AI can improve in mathematics without leaving, it means it can develop a more sophisticated learning algorithm overall. Then, perhaps, the argument for SIE still holds.

But mathematics is, in a sense, arbitrary. We ourselves make many decisions about how it is structured and what is useful within it. For example, zero was not always recognized as a full number. It took centuries to accept imaginary numbers, and their four-dimensional relatives, quaternions, seemed so absurd to Lewis Carroll that it's suggested he mocked them in the Mad Hatter's tea party scene [23].

Today, we take such things for granted, although they are not as obvious or natural as they seem. They gained widespread acceptance only after finding practical applications and yielding results that showed a meaningful correspondence to the real world.

A similar story happened with Boolean algebra, the fundamental mathematics on which computation is based. For almost a century, it was also considered an exotic branch of mathematics. George Boole first published it [24] in 1847, but it had no practical applications and was studied as a curiosity of formal logic, not as a tool for serious engineering.

Its significance only became clear in the 1930s when Claude Shannon was working at MIT on the relay control of Vannevar Bush's differential analyzer. He had encountered Boolean algebra earlier, during his university studies, in philosophy classes [25]. While working with circuits, Shannon realized that it described them perfectly. In his master's thesis, "A Symbolic Analysis of Relay and Switching Circuits" [26], he established this connection in 1937. The rest, as they say, is history.

From George Boole's algebra of logic (1847) to its application to electric switching circuits by Shannon (1937), about 90 years passed.

Boole wasn't just inventing mathematics for its own sake. He intuitively felt that the properties he discovered and the system he created would one day be important. He believed he was discovering the laws of human thought. Perhaps he was somewhat mistaken, but the idea was interesting enough for logicians and philosophers to study and pass on until Shannon encountered it and continued the work.

Could Shannon have discovered this connection in the age of AI? If he hadn't stood in a room full of relays, facing a real problem that suggested where to look, but instead sifted through endless stacks of proofs, individually correct but collectively meaningless?

Producing correct statements and finding meaningful connections among them are not the same thing.

In isolation from reality, one can define an infinite number of logical and mathematical systems and become extremely good at building and exploring these hypothetical constructs. But to determine if they have meaning requires a different skill that cannot be improved within a bubble.

Mathematicians are already concerned about this divergence. In September 2026, twenty-five Fields Medalists, including Terence Tao, signed a declaration [27]. In it, they argue that problem-solving is only an indirect measure of mathematics' true goal, understanding, and that mass production of correct statements can deplete the soil from which new ideas grow.

What will happen when this soil is completely depleted?

Collapse

To create coherence requires long and tireless work. To explore every crack, to look under every stone. To wade into the mud of reality and let it seep into your boots while blindly feeling for all the ways the world around you can be shaped and reshaped, endlessly, but in such a way that it doesn't consume you whole.

It's not just about solving problems. It's also about understanding what problems we are trying to solve.

To understand all these problems and find a unified solution that handles each one without turning into a rapidly growing labyrinth of complexity.

And this brings me to my final thought.

Banner text: "Beautiful, engaging Linux by DHH with agents. A flexible OS for the agent era. Vibe-code any changes, settings, and fixes."

There has been a lot of talk about Omarchy [28] lately. Many like it, while many others, let's say, are skeptical of a Linux distribution that places agents at its core. The project has moved in this direction very quickly and seems to be evolving from an initially conceived neat set of default settings into a full-fledged distribution with its own kernel build [29].

Let me preface this by saying that experiments on the periphery of the ecosystem are important. The ability to fork, explore ideas, freely figure out what works, and then return the results to the community is largely what makes open-source software so successful.

But here, I'm starting to worry.

I worry that somewhere along the way, we might have forgotten that we are all climbing the same mountain together. That it was precisely the creation of coherence that made our community so successful and resilient, allowing computing technology to advance so far and so fast.

In the 1980s, 1990s, and throughout the 2000s, every company maintained its own stack. Each server had its own set of dependencies, scripts, OS versions, and so on. In a typical company, an entire team was needed just to keep everything running smoothly.

During the Unix wars [30], fragmentation was even more severe. Unix had no obvious successor, and Windows was regaining ground by offering a completely different paradigm. Where would we be today if Linux hadn't emerged then and united the aging *nix ecosystem around itself? (Yes, I know FreeBSD existed and could have taken that place. In reality, I'm asking what would have happened if the free software movement hadn't emerged at all.)

Would we be paying licensing fees for every container launched in the cloud?

How many startups would never have appeared simply due to the cost of operating their own stack?

Because of this fragmentation, each system eventually began to seem outdated and cumbersome. The entire ecosystem was incoherent, and a significant part of the work of the last two decades has been to increase its coherence (and to capture some of the created value through SaaS products, but I'm getting ahead of myself).

I fear we may now be engaging in something akin to slash-and-burn agriculture, discarding accumulated coherence to recreate each dependency, tailor each program, and expand in all directions for short-term tasks.

And failing to notice that all this time we are digging ourselves deeper into the trap.

The inner area is accessible to humans. A larger system can be maintained by agents, but this doesn't mean they can lead it back to lower complexity. The outer area is beyond available computing resources. Static version of the original diagram.

If we move forward for too long and too far without maintaining or restoring coherence, then with current trends, we will reach a point where agents will have to be used to maintain systems. Systems will become so vast and unmanageable that we ourselves will no longer cope. But if we get stuck there and it turns out that agents are unable to do the work of fixing systems and lead us back, the situation will be quite unpleasant:

We will be locked into a development model that slowly destroys our systems, no matter how much effort we spend fixing them.

This doesn't mean systems can never be fixed. But restoration might take years or even decades. And all this time, we won't be able to release new features or make progress on many other pressing societal problems.

This is what I call decoherence collapse. And I fear it much more than uncontrolled superintelligence, because it is already happening right now, before our eyes.

I want to clarify once again that AI has a certain utility. It's just that, in my opinion, it's more in auxiliary tasks than in replacing core workflows or fully autonomous operation. I strongly resonate with Cory Doctorow's "reverse centaur" analysis [31]. It's not the machine that should control us, but we who should control the machine. You can read more about my approach to this in work and life in my "AI Usage Policy and Guide."

As I have repeatedly stated in this essay, decoherence is not a new problem. It has existed since the dawn of human civilization, and possibly longer. It is a natural form of decay or degradation, a kind of entropy that we have always dealt with and always will. With this in mind, I want to leave you with two final thoughts.

First. Decoherence only changes when the systems themselves change. Therefore, no matter how bad things get, we can always stop moving forward and start working our way back. If AI has led us deep into a trap, full recovery may take years or even decades, but the beginning will be the hardest. Then improvements will start to snowball if we continue to increase coherence and finish the job. If we only improve the system enough to return to old habits, we will simply restart the cycle.

Second. We already have processes that are known to increase coherence over time. Humans have found ways to create more coherent systems. In many ways, it is these systems that have enabled the scope and scale of technological progress of the last few centuries.

In particular, the open-source software development process, to which we owe a significant portion of coherence in programming, creates it so successfully that in some truly remarkable aspects, it has surpassed corporate development. But more on this in the next essay.
