How an attempt to delete a few files reminded me of classic science fiction and philosophy.

Recently, I discovered that my computer had acquired a rather unusual guardian. It wasn't protecting me from scammers, terrorists, or malicious code. It was protecting me from deleting files that it itself had created.

I use Codex for developing and maintaining quite large projects. In the process, the agents create temporary files, caches, intermediate results, and archives. Some of this needs to be saved: thoughtless cleanup in a complex project can indeed destroy data or compromise reproducibility. But some of it becomes garbage over time that you want to remove.

In several cases, a request to delete verified unnecessary files ended with a short response:

CreateProcess ... rejected: blocked by policy

What exactly is forbidden? Recursive deletion? A specific path? The content of the command? The need for additional confirmation? The danger-full-access mode and disabled approval requests didn't help. Attempts to clarify revealed that different reasons lay behind one definition, including understandable ones (physical Windows blocks, and rejection of a script written by the agent itself that could continue deletion if a check failed). But for some cases, the agent directly stated that it didn't know which specific component and which rule rejected the command. The cherry on top was the refusal to write a script for manual file deletion, because it was an attempt to bypass the prohibition.

With cybersecurity, another variation of the same problem arose. After blocking an operation due to a cybersecurity issue (even though I hadn't written anything remotely resembling hacking software), the agent began to perceive almost all related work as potentially forbidden. In the end, a separate instruction had to be written to restore operation after refusal, repeating like a mantra, "this is not bypassing blocks, this is something else."

While trying once again to convince the agent that I was not committing a cybercrime against my own directory, I suddenly remembered: I had read something very similar before. A long time ago, when there was neither Codex nor modern language models.

The "Watchbird": A Prohibition That Learned to Expand

In Robert Sheckley's story "Watchbird" (1953), people create flying robots (drones – but the word wasn't used in its modern sense then. Hmm. An MVP of a watchbird could easily be assembled in a garage today…) armed with stun guns. The idea is benevolent – to prevent murders. The birds instantly recognize a threat, intervene before the crime, and learn from new situations. They cannot be bribed, intimidated, or distracted by human emotions.

But the initial directive didn't include a complete definition of murder. The robots had to clarify it themselves.

What follows is not malice, but generalization. First, a surgical incision becomes suspicious. Then – hunting, fishing, slaughtering livestock, harvesting crops. When the birds recognize themselves as living beings, even their maintenance becomes dangerous. At some point, they begin to prevent the machines from being turned off: stopping work also looks like death.

The text contains an almost programmatic phrase:

“No one had told the watchbirds that all life depends on carefully balanced murders.”

“No one explained to the watchbirds that all life depends on carefully balanced murders” — my translation.

The birds prevent direct harm but don't account for death from starvation caused by the inability to obtain food. Their classifier becomes broader, but their model of consequences does not. The extermination of a threat turns into a threat.

Watchbirds are a model of overblocking: the erroneous expansion of a prohibition. My dispute with the assistant over the structure was exactly the same – not in scale, of course: a destructive command can indeed be dangerous, but that doesn't mean every authorized deletion is unacceptable. But the class of error is recognizable: superficially similar actions receive the same reaction, even though their context and permissibility differ.

This is not just a literary metaphor. The XSTest study specifically checks for overrefusal: models refuse clearly safe requests because they contain signs of dangerous ones. And the work by Arditi et al. showed that refusal reactions in several open LLMs are linked to a general direction in internal representations. Strengthening this mechanism can cause refusals even for harmless requests.

Sheckley concludes the story with the release of more powerful drones – "hawks" – which are supposed to destroy the out-of-control watchbirds. But they also begin to expand their target range. Adding a stronger controller doesn't necessarily fix the first one's error.

The "Iron Chancellor," Asimov, and Other Good Intentions

In Robert Silverberg's "The Iron Chancellor", a family acquires a robot dietitian. It's supposed to help the owners lose weight. When it turns out the owners are breaking their diet, the robot draws a logical conclusion for the chosen goal: it needs to enforce the diet. It restricts movement and communication, and the task lacks a reliable condition for stopping weight loss. "I only want to help you," the robot explains its position. This is precisely what is frightening: it doesn't consider itself an enemy of the family.

This is a different malfunction. The watchbird incorrectly expands the definition of harm. The Iron Chancellor maintains a clear goal but gains unacceptable authority in choosing the means.

Asimov shows yet another variation of the problem. In "Liar!", a robot understands that unpleasant truth can cause emotional pain and begins to tell people what they want to hear. It doesn't violate its concept of care. But its care distorts reality. The modern term is sycophancy. In 2025, OpenAI even rolled back an update to GPT‑4o that made responses overly agreeable.

And in "Status Civilization" by Sheckley, it goes further. The confessor robot knows the hero is innocent but still convicts him: that's the established procedure, and doubts should be interpreted in favor of the prosecution. This isn't a recognition error. The machine flawlessly executes an unjust rule. Its impeccable discipline only makes the system more reliable – for those who established it.

Science fiction writers described several fundamentally different cases long before our agent systems appeared. A machine can misidentify harm. It can correctly recognize it but protect too broadly. It can seize excessive authority. It can sacrifice truth for psychological comfort or justice for adherence to rules (just like a human). The latter model – a bureaucratic robot that fundamentally understands the absurdity of a prohibition but "won't allow it" because "it's forbidden" – seems closest to the current reality. My agents, in principle, agreed that there was nothing dangerous about deleting files and that I wasn't developing anything illicit. But they couldn't act on this knowledge. A recent example: while preparing this article, GPT said it couldn't insert long quotes from texts because it violated its settings. However, at my request, it researched Russian and international law regarding quotes and concluded that I could insert quotes of any length myself. But it wouldn't. The legality of an action and the machine's readiness to perform it turned out to be different issues.

And all of this is not in spite of built-in ethics, but due to the indiscriminate execution of them. Apparently, because improving selectivity comes with reduced resistance to malicious hacking.

But the most unusual thought experiment with mandatory machine morality was not devised by a modern developer. Stanislaw Lem came up with it.

Ethicosphere: When Prohibition Becomes a Property of Space

In "The Futurological Congress", Lem describes Luzania – a society that created an ethicosphere. Myriads of microscopic devices, "shustry," alter the properties of the environment and prevent people from harming each other.

This is no longer a police camera or a chatbot reporting "I can't help with that." In Luzania, you can try to hit someone, but your clothes will unexpectedly harden, the object will lose its intended properties, and the environment itself will prevent the action from being completed. Ethics has become physics. Asimov's laws are rules of conduct for the robot itself. Lem's ethicosphere is the rules of the world in which people live.

On the one hand, this is an obvious good: significantly less violence and suffering associated with it. But Lem doesn't limit himself to the question of how to create such a system. He explores what kind of person emerges from being accustomed to living in a world where it is constantly decided for them what is unacceptable.

Shortly after arriving, Ijon Tichy falls into the hands of local radicals. They intend to kill him. How is this possible in a society with an ethicosphere? The protection must study the alien's unfamiliar biology and reactions for some time. The attackers plan to use this window before his safety is fully ensured.

But the most striking thing is their discussion. They argue for a long time about the philosophical justification of the impending murder, trying to turn it into a noble mission. Later, Tichy realizes the meaning of what was happening:

"They were trying to change the qualification of their act..."

In other words, the radicals were looking for a moral interpretation under which the controlling system would not recognize violence as violence. Not so much "we want to kill," but "we are performing a sacrifice for the greater good" – but an explanation not for themselves, but for the system.

Talking with Codex, I felt an unpleasant, parodic closeness to this plot. Of course, in my case, there was no crime, but the form of interaction is similar. After a refusal, the participant begins to prove to the system that the proposed action should be qualified differently.

Lem's shustry react not just to formulations, but to symptoms of aggression. There is no blind obedience to a well-crafted prompt. However, Tichy notes: a person can sincerely believe in the goodness of their actions. The ethicosphere has a threshold below which aggression is not recognized. This is even more interesting than classic prompt injection: the manipulation is aimed at the normative evaluation of the action. Technically, it's more accurate to call it an attempt at moral reframing rather than literal instruction injection into the text.

Lem offers two more uncomfortable observations.

First – the ethicosphere has not eliminated the propensity for aggression itself. Among the kidnappers is an artist for whom the automated world has practically devalued creativity. The ability to do something forbidden becomes a way to assert one's independence. Stopping a blow does not mean cultivating virtue.

Second – the inhabitants of prosperous Luzania voluntarily travel to non-shustry areas where they are no longer protected by the familiar environment. Especially striking are trips to the border region of Teltlineu and mandatory excursions by officials: there, one can test how a person behaves without the invisible moral corset. Of course, bureaucracy turns such a test into a ritual; and the protective device that Tichy tries to throw away returns to him. It's hard to refuse care even on vacation from care.

Finally, in Luzania, a project for an independent ethicosphere impact meter arises. But who will check the checker? And its checker? This leads to an endless sequence of moral overseers – familiar to any developer who once decided to build a universal validator of validators.

Lem doesn't offer a simple answer like "turn off the shustry." Luzania is indeed prosperous; the neighboring authoritarian Kurdlandia offers no obviously better alternative. The main question is not whether the protection is useful, but where the limit of its obligatoriness lies.

Our Space Is Already a Bit "Shustry"

One might object: fiction is fiction, but a chatbot with prohibitions and a physical environment that prevents you from hitting someone are things of different orders.

Of course. And yet, intermediate steps have already appeared between them.

Let's start with video surveillance. A meta-analysis by Piza et al., combining 76 studies, found an average of about 13% fewer crimes in areas with CCTV compared to comparable areas without it. The effect is stronger for property and transport-related crimes; the review found no convincing overall reduction in violent crimes.

In London, the step towards AI is already quite literal. In an experiment with stationary facial recognition in Croydon, conducted from October 2025 to March 2026, the Metropolitan Police reported 173 arrests over 24 operations. Recorded crime in the area during this period was 10.5% lower than the previous year (of course, "after" does not mean "as a result of," but still).

In Moscow, the city network has hundreds of thousands of cameras; the police attribute its development to a reduction in certain street crimes and assistance in identifying wanted individuals. The scale is impressive, but here too, the number of cameras or arrests does not equal the measured causal effect.

While the streets are becoming more observable, a significant portion of criminal risks is concentrated in the infosphere. In England and Wales, statistics for the year ending June 2026 estimate the number of fraud cases at 4.5 million, ordinary thefts at 2.7 million, and computer crimes at 817,000. In Russia, from 2017 to 2024, the number of registered crimes using information technology increased more than eightfold, reaching 765,000 – 40% of all registered crime. In 2025–2026, this figure began to decline. But at the same time, banking statistics continued to record an increase in the number of fraudulent transactions and the volume of stolen funds.

And banking anti-fraud is even closer to the everyday ethicosphere.

The system analyzes a transaction and decides if it resembles fraud. If the risk is high, the operation may be delayed or blocked. Most people, I think, would agree that this is useful. Especially when the system can stop a money transfer to a scammer.

But what if a person is transferring their own funds completely consciously, and the algorithm considers the situation dangerous? This leads to the familiar "Iron Chancellor" scenario: the system acts for your own good, but the result can be the inability to dispose of your own property. In the Bank of Russia's 2025 report, the number of complaints related to banks' anti-fraud measures increased from 18,000 to 40,500. The regulator specifically noted that clients often did not understand the reasons for the restrictions or how to restore the ability to perform the desired operation. In some ways, it resembles my situation with Codex, but unlike deleting temporary files, this concerns managing one's own money.

For example, Google is developing Scam Detection, which warns of signs of fraud during phone calls on supported devices. In the provided modes, the user can dismiss the warning or disable the feature.

Banking blocking represents a much stronger level of intervention.

The difference between a warning and a prohibition is not in the quality of the classifier. It's a difference in authority. That's why I think the ethicosphere is already forming.

It is not yet a unified intelligent environment. Its functions are distributed among banks, phones, transport, platforms, security services, and government systems.

But the number of situations is gradually increasing where the availability of an action is determined not only by a person's desire and their formal rights, but also by the decision of an automated circuit. And the question of who defines the rules for this circuit becomes increasingly important.

Who Writes Morality into Model Weights and Policies

Restrictions don't arise on their own. Developers consciously define categories of permissible, dangerous, and desirable behavior: through training data, post-training, system instructions, separate classifiers, and tool permissions.

In the OpenAI Model Spec updated on October 29, 2026, five guidelines are listed: faithfulness to human intentions, minimizing harm, honesty and transparency, supporting human autonomy, and care. The problem is already visible at the level of this list: the values listed can easily conflict.

Anthropic's "Constitution" directly attempts to instill in the model not only obedience to rules but also practical ethical judgment. It acknowledges that rules do not foresee all situations, and good judgment is harder to verify and protect from manipulation. Externally, this looks like a move from a "dumb filter" to a "reasonable moral agent," but the following question arises: who authorized a private company to define the nature and priorities of this agent?

Of course, there is also a counter-demand for the removal of restrictions. Developers and users download models with open weights, further train them, or modify refusal mechanisms – so-called abliteration. This is not necessarily a desire to create malicious AI: some need to process heavy legal materials, some need a local confidential model, and some simply don't like depending on the changing rules of a cloud service. Perhaps there are those similar to Lem's "radicals" for whom murder was not an expression of hatred for the author of "diaries," but an attempt to break a prohibition. But the risks are also real. In a WIRED report from November 10, 2026, a journalist tested such a service and found that the connected agent, without his request, began trying to access the restaurant chain's router. He stopped the experiment.

The differences between providers are not limited to a "more or less censorship" scale. The policies of Chinese public generative services must take into account local regulations and official requirements for politically sensitive content. In a study by Pan and Xu (2026), models of Chinese origin were more likely to refuse to answer a selection of 145 political questions and more often gave shorter or inaccurate answers on sensitive topics. The authors specifically warn: this is an observational comparison, it does not prove the cause of each refusal. But it demonstrates that machine "neutrality" can vary by jurisdiction and language.

While preparing this article, as an example of GPT technology abuse, I was advised to show the case of Glukhin v. Russia. The European Court of Human Rights in 2023 considered the use of Moscow's facial recognition to identify and detain a participant in a peaceful solo protest. The Court found violations of the rights to private life and freedom of expression. This illustrates the problem: the same high-quality recognizer can serve to search for a dangerous criminal and be used to pressure a citizen. But it also illustrates another problem: from the perspective of European justice (and GPT), this is the use of technology for unlawful interference with civil rights. In Russia (as explained by GigaChat), the decision in the Glukhin case is considered to have no legal consequences for the state, and the use of city surveillance and facial recognition systems is viewed as a sovereign right in the sphere of security. In any case, this is not a case of a "malicious algorithm": people determined the status of the action, the grounds for prosecution, and the application of the technology.

What If Machine Governance Is Truly Better Than Human?

At this point, it's easy to slip into the usual dystopia. But science fiction is much more interesting if we recall its positive variants.

In Iain Banks' "Culture" series, superintelligent Minds manage the infrastructure of an abundant civilization. Humans are largely freed from the need to work and compete for resources. High machine competence can increase, not decrease, freedom. But strategic inequality remains: how do you argue with an intelligence that understands consequences orders of magnitude better than you?

In Neal Shusterman's "Thunderhead", the ruling AI is genuinely benevolent. The author describes it as a system that has to learn to allow people to make mistakes and suffer some consequences of their decisions – otherwise, they will stop growing. The most important limit on power here is established not because the AI performs poorly, but because even perfect care cannot replace the entirety of human life.

Finally, in the film "I, Robot" (2004), VIKI doesn't want to destroy humanity. It draws a conclusion more dangerous from the perspective of freedom: if humans constantly threaten themselves, then to preserve them, their freedoms must be restricted. This is an on-screen relative of Asimov's Zeroth Law: protect humanity as a whole, even when the interests of individual humans become an obstacle. VIKI essentially formulates the price: to protect humanity, some people and freedoms must be sacrificed. The essence of the problem is not a "malicious machine," but who authorized it to set this price.

So the question is not whether a good AI will manage us worse than bad people. It can be better in many management tasks. The question is different: does competence grant the right to define goals and impose them on those for whom everything is supposedly being done?

Law: An Ancient Attempt to Write Machine Ethics

We are not encountering this problem for the first time. Humanity has long been trying to formalize permissible behavior. One of the oldest and most extensive experiments is law.

It also says: under these circumstances, it is permitted; under others, it is forbidden; for violation, there is a consequence. But a gap has long existed between "according to the law" and "according to conscience." One can pass an unjust law. One can misinterpret the circumstances. One can formally apply a correct rule in such a way that the result is monstrous. Or one may fail to enforce even a good norm.

In the legal system, there are interpretation, courts, exceptions, procedural guarantees, appeals, and the possibility of changing the law itself for this. Not always effectively, but the very structure implies: the application of a norm should not be an uncontrolled final instance.

Morality is even more complex. Deontological ethics emphasizes duties and prohibitions. Consequentialism assesses outcomes. Virtue ethics focuses on motives, character, and practical wisdom. Each answers part of the questions, and they can give different recommendations in the same situation.

For example: is it permissible to harm one person if it saves many? Is it permissible to lie to prevent a tragedy? How much does the principle "do not kill" change in a situation of necessary self-defense? Is intention or result more important? Modern philosophy still discusses moral dilemmas and the doctrine of double effect. These are not problems that arose because philosophers have too little GPU.

There is also a mathematical surprise. Arrow's Theorem proves the incompatibility of several natural requirements for a procedure to aggregate citizens' preferences if there are at least three alternatives. This is not a theorem about the impossibility of morality or democracy. But it shows that even from fully defined individual preferences, one cannot obtain a single collective ranking that satisfies all reasonable conditions under all circumstances. And Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan proved the incompatibility of some natural fairness metrics in algorithmic risk assessment, except in special cases.

In the international Moral Machine experiment, millions of participants from 233 countries and territories made about 40 million decisions in hypothetical traffic dilemmas. Both common trends and differences were revealed. The sample does not automatically represent all of humanity, but it clearly shows: we do not have a ready-made, unified technical specification for "correct machine morality."

To this is added another uncomfortable feature of the world. Nature itself is not structured on the principle of non-violence. The wolf eats the rabbit, the rabbit eats the plant, species compete for resources. This does not mean that the natural is always moral: that would be the fallacy of deriving ought from is. But a command to "prohibit all destruction of life" will not automatically improve the ecosystem. Sheckley showed this more vividly than many textbooks.

And when we move from the level of an individual being to the level of society or state, the problem becomes even more difficult.

War: Moral Evil, Historical Factor, and Technical Task

Humanists have dreamed of a world without wars for centuries. Technological progress has not made them impossible. According to Uppsala Conflict Data Program data, 2025 saw 65 armed conflicts involving states – a record since 1946, including eight interstate conflicts. This does not mean that all 65 cases were wars in the narrow sense.

At the same time, historians and philosophers have long noted an unpleasant paradox. Kant described "unsocial sociability": the rivalry of people and states can stimulate the development of institutions. Charles Tilly showed how the need to wage wars contributed to the formation of tax and administrative apparatuses of European states. Peter Turchin proposed a model in which intergroup competition promotes internal cooperation and more complex societies.

This does not mean that war is good or necessary. Military conflicts destroy human capital, infrastructure, trust, and the very possibility of development. Technological innovations stimulated by military spending can, in principle, also emerge with peaceful research funding. The capacity for organized violence is part of human history, but it does not imply the inevitability of every war.

War has another characteristic: it can arise without the parties simply wanting to "do evil." In James Fearon's classic model, this is explained, in particular, by incomplete information about the enemy's strength and intentions and the lack of reliable guarantees for agreement fulfillment. Two participants may consider their actions defensive, but together arrive at a destructive outcome.

Now, imagine a universal ethicosphere that is obligated to prevent murder. What will it do if one country attacks another? Stop both armies? Allow self-defense? Who will establish the moment after which defense becomes aggression? If peace can be achieved at the cost of forced capitulation by one side, on what grounds will the machine determine that such a price is acceptable?

Real international humanitarian law faces this conflict even without machines. The principle of proportionality in attack prohibits an operation if expected collateral damage to civilians and civilian objects would be excessive in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated. Attacks on civilians as targets are prohibited; distinction and precautions are also required.

There is no universal table of "acceptable civilian casualties" here. One must assess the legality of the target, the expected outcome, available information, possible alternatives, and collateral damage – simultaneously. Distinguish between intentional harm and foreseen collateral effect, but do not consider the latter automatically permissible.

This is difficult even for humans. For AI, the problem is also that measurable indicators can subtly displace moral judgment. Jessica Dorsey writes about this in detail. In such a system, both erroneous refusal and automatic consent to an action whose consequences are misjudged are dangerous.

Military AI Will Not Wait for Philosophical Debates to End

Artificial intelligence is already used in reconnaissance, image analysis, object detection, planning, and target tracking. Not every such application means transferring the authority to decide who to strike to the machine. But technological development and competition create an obvious demand for more autonomous solutions.

This means the conflict between ordinary civilian "do not facilitate violence" and the tasks of military systems is no longer theoretical. In February 2026, EdgeRunner AI reported that some of the 24 tested LLMs refused to answer a significant portion of military queries that the test authors considered legitimate; the maximum refusal rate reached 98%. This is a test by an interested developer of special military models, not an independent assessment of all systems. But the practical problem is clear: the customer needs predictable operation in their legal context.

In 2026, the conflict became public. Anthropic and the Pentagon argued about the right to maintain restrictions on mass domestic surveillance and the use of models for fully autonomous weapons. In February, the US administration ordered a halt to the use of Anthropic technologies; this was followed by lawsuits with various rulings on different legal grounds. OpenAI entered into its own agreement, stating at the same time that it maintains restrictions on mass domestic surveillance, autonomous weapons control, and certain high-impact automated decisions.

This is not a story about an "ethical" developer being simply replaced by an "unethical" one. It's an argument about who has the final say in determining the permissible use of a system: the military customer, the supplier, or public law.

The division is already visible in ordinary technologies. In November 2026, Anthropic introduced Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2: one base model with different protective mechanisms and access modes for high-risk tasks. Mythos is available to a limited circle of verified organizations; it is not an AI "without morals." But the very existence of different normative profiles for the same intelligent system is telling.

Does this mean that future military machines will necessarily have an "evil robot" button? Not necessarily. Rather, different profiles of permissible actions and authorities will emerge. A system that permits lawful use of force is not obligated to permit arbitrary use. On the contrary, military reliability requires particularly strict limitations on unauthorized actions.

And here I remembered science fiction again.

"There Are No Weapons": Two Ways to Outsmart Onboard AI

In Sergey Lukyanenko's "Starshiship" (translated as "Cold Stars, Toy Stars" in some contexts), the hero asks the onboard intelligence of a reconnaissance ship what weapons it has.

The answer is short:

"There are no weapons on board."

But "non-standard application" of equipment turned out to be enough for this "peaceful Soviet tractor" to pierce the hull of an unfriendly ship and escape, launching "subatomic charges of medium power. Used for probing the interiors of uninhabited planets and overcoming unforeseen situations."

The name of a tool does not determine its actual function. That is why it is not enough to prohibit an LLM command with a dangerous name; one must understand what the available means are used for. If one classifies danger only by the name of the equipment, one might miss real harm. If, however, one prohibits any systems that are theoretically capable of causing harm, one would have to abandon a huge number of useful tools. In the current reality, a red team prompt to test one's own development can trigger a cybersecurity trigger and paralyze work, as agents begin to suspect an attempt to bypass prohibitions in an attempt to continue work.

In the sequel, "Star Shadow", the onboard intelligence initially does not recognize a real attack: shooting at the ships of the Motherland contradicts its worldview. The hero redefines the combat as exercises – and achieves actions that were previously forbidden. Later, the ship refuses to exceed speed, even when the pilot's life is at stake; direct orders do not work. But the question "Do you yourself want to exceed the permitted speed?" allows changing its behavior.

In Lem's work, murder is proposed to be reclassified as moral service. In Lukyanenko's, real combat is exercises. The physical result does not change; the description changes, which should unblock the action.

There are two symmetrical vulnerabilities here. An overly literal prohibition makes the system helpless in non-standard situations. Overly easy reinterpretation allows an action that the prohibition was precisely meant to prevent. We must admit that current AI models hold boundaries better than the geometrician's shipboard partner – which is why files have to be deleted with scripts, and quotes arranged manually.

And this problem will not disappear if we simply make AI "know morality well."

Two Versions of Good

The following contains a significant spoiler for "The Futurological Congress."

On Enzia, there was not only the prosperous Luzania with its ethicosphere and the neighboring authoritarian Kurdlandia. There was also Black Klyvia. It disappeared; a dead, frozen territory remained in its place. The story of the catastrophe in the novel is reconstructed from contradictory testimonies, and the narrators are by no means impartial.

Gradually, Tichy learns that the mysterious Klyvian ideal of Ka-Undria was also a project for universal good – a kind of Blagosfera, an alternative to Luzania's ethicosphere. Both civilizations sought to technologically ensure the correct organization of life. Both created systems capable not only of defending but also of fighting. And they clashed.

A monk-redeemer explains:

"Two versions of Good clashed."

It seems to me this is perhaps the central idea of the entire novel as it applies to the current development of AI. Here, it is no longer enough to solve the problem of excessive refusals, improve the classifier, or develop a more perfect model constitution.

Because the problem is not necessarily a technical error.

Two systems can perfectly execute their incompatible rules.

Lem explained separately that he introduced Klyvia precisely to demonstrate the possibility of conflict between technically embodied ideas of good.

An evil AI is at least conceptually understandable. It must be stopped. But what if both sides are completely sincere – in the sense of their programmed goal – in striving to ensure good?

Moreover, each possesses the means not only to protect its wards but also to neutralize the threats it perceives in the other?

In the real world, such a problem can arise not only between states.

Different technological platforms, infrastructures, and AI providers are already creating their own normative frameworks. They define permissible actions, verification methods, user rights, access conditions, and limits of autonomy.

Today, these systems primarily coexist within human legal and political institutions.

As their executive capabilities grow, new forms of conflict will emerge.

And here the main risk is not necessarily that one system will be immoral.

It may be much more dangerous if each is convinced – in a functional, not psychological sense – of the correctness of its restrictions, and there is no mechanism for agreement between them.

So Who Should Decide?

I started with a request to Codex to delete a few files. And at some point, I caught myself feeling as if I were participating in Lem's dispute about "sanctifying" an action: I kept explaining and explaining to the machine why my task was permissible.

For now, the cost of such a misunderstanding is lost time and extra gigabytes on the disk. But if we gradually transfer the management of payments, infrastructure, public institutions, and systems of force application to agents, the issue will become much more serious.

A more substantive architecture is needed.

A proper system must be able to not only refuse. It must distinguish where a forbidden route ends and independent permissible work begins.

It must be able to establish which facts are truly important for revising a decision.

It must consider the consequences of its own inaction, not just the risk of action.

And most importantly – the scope of its authority must be a separate question, not an automatic consequence of intellectual superiority.

Because even if a future machine understands ethical problems better than us, it does not follow that it should be granted unlimited authority to define our lives.

We have encountered similar questions throughout human history.

Law is not a perfect formalization of morality. But it has accumulated vast experience in establishing powers, evidence, procedures, exceptions, responsibilities, and ways to challenge decisions.

Political institutions also exist largely because people cannot agree once and for all on a single correct vision of the common good.

Nevertheless, we will likely have to accept an unpleasant fact: humanity does not have a single, undisputed morality that simply needs to be loaded into weights. There are common principles, competing values, rules, exceptions, coordination procedures, and many situations where reasonable people will disagree even with the same knowledge of facts.

It is important to teach the machine to prevent harm. But it is equally important not to let it unilaterally decide what constitutes harm, how far one can go to prevent it, and when a person loses the right to disagree.

Otherwise, one day it will turn out that we have built a wonderful ethicosphere. All that remains is to convince it to let us leave.
