Sound familiar? It has a name and nearly sixty years of research behind it, though no psychologist in 1968, of course, was thinking about Zoom meetings.

Meetings That Don't Lead to Results

According to the RBC School of Management, 22% of managers consider their own meetings unproductive – second wave survey, May 2026, 2243 respondents. These are current managers who sit in their own meetings and simultaneously think – what's the point?

Familiar symptoms, if you look closely:

  • a decision was made in the meeting, but a week later no one remembers who was responsible for it

  • the task was voiced but didn't end up in any system – not in a tracker, not in a chat

  • at the next meeting, the same thing is discussed for the second, and sometimes third, time

Usually, this is attributed to poor discipline. A more accurate and far less flattering answer for the manager is this: the more people hear a decision, the less each of them feels obligated to carry it out.

The Client No One Called

It happened to us exactly as described at the beginning of the article. During a meeting, we agreed to call one client back to clarify contract details. We discussed it aloud, everyone nodded, and dispersed. A few days later, the client themselves wrote in the general chat: you promised to call back, I'm waiting. We started investigating who was supposed to do it – and it turned out that everyone present thought someone else was going to call. Formally, the task was set. In reality, it wasn't assigned to anyone.

Later, I stumbled upon an experiment that explains this almost literally.

In 1968, psychologists John Darley and Bibb Latané conducted an experiment that became a classic in social psychology. 72 students participated in a discussion via intercom and heard one of the participants – actually a pre-recorded voice – begin to simulate an epileptic seizure. The participants couldn't see each other and only knew the supposed number of listeners.

The result showed a direct correlation with the group size:

Each participant psychologically divided the responsibility by the number of presumed witnesses and ended up with such a small share that it ceased to be felt as a personal duty.

It was exactly the same with the client. Five people heard the task, and each of them mentally performed the same unspoken calculation: there are five of us, my share is one-fifth, and one-fifth is easily mistaken for zero.

There's a detail in the experiment that explains why written documentation actually helped in our case. The study participants couldn't see each other and didn't know for sure who had already reacted – all the uncertainty was built on anonymity and lack of knowledge.

It was the same in our meeting: the decision was announced to the group, but who exactly took ownership of it remained unknown to everyone, including the person who, at the moment, wasn't sure it was them. An individual meeting protocol eliminates precisely this uncertainty: it makes impossible the lack of knowledge on which the entire effect in the Darley and Latané experiment was based.

Worse When Everyone Works

Diffusion of responsibility explains why a specific task gets lost. There's another story – when everyone seems to be working on a task, but there's still no result.

We've had that too. We were preparing a quarterly report for management, breaking it down into parts – each person wrote their section. Individually, the sections were fine. When combined, they were loose, as if three different documents were glued together with tape, and no one took responsibility for the final assembly. Everyone thought that since everyone was writing, someone else would tidy up the whole thing.

A different mechanism is at play here – also old, but not from psychology, rather from engineering.

In 1913, French engineer Max Ringelmann studied tug-of-war and discovered a pattern in physical effort: the more people pull together, the less force each person applies. He identified two reasons – loss of coordination (people synchronize worse in a group) and loss of motivation (each person expects their neighbor to pull for them).

Both reasons applied to the report. Loss of coordination – three sections written without a cohesive sense of the whole. Loss of motivation – everyone expected someone else to do the final proofreading, since three people were writing it. Ringelmann, of course, knew nothing about quarterly reports – he worked with ropes his whole life. But the conclusion is the same: the more hands involved, the less output from each individual hand.

What's Needed for a Decision to Become a Task

Putting both cases together, we understood a simple thing about conducting meetings in general. A decision needs one clear owner and one written record that everyone sees, not the memory of a few people with different versions of what happened. This led to a short list of what a meeting should cover on its own, without additional effort afterward:

  • the responsible person is named aloud and recorded in writing in the meeting minutes;

  • the record is unified for everyone, not each person having their own version;

  • the deadline is set immediately, not appearing later as a separate chat message.

This sounds like three lines. Implementing it turned out to be harder than it seemed on paper.

Meeting Transcription and Minutes in Four Steps

Essentially, it's an AI secretary that sits in on every meeting and forgets nothing. The logic is simple, four steps:

1. Record the meeting – in the service, by connecting to an online call, or by uploading a ready audio file, even from a dictaphone.

2. Get the meeting transcript and minutes – the system separates speakers and highlights the essence.

3. Refine the minutes manually if details need clarification.

4. Assign tasks to colleagues.

In this format, the aforementioned client call would look different: a task with a name and date would go to the tracker immediately after the meeting, instead of remaining in the memory of five people, each thinking their neighbor would make the call.

What Really Works

The tool solves the problem of a single clear owner and a shared meeting record for everyone. But there's a third case that we struggled with even with minutes. The same issue regarding the distribution of incoming leads among managers was discussed in three consecutive meetings. The responsible person was named each time, minutes were kept meticulously, but in reality, nothing changed – the fourth meeting started from the same point.

The responsible person was named, and minutes were kept. But the minutes stated something like, "we agreed to improve lead distribution." This is a decision. There's no task in it – no specific action, no deadline, because it's unclear what exactly needs to be done on Monday morning.

How We Started Keeping Meeting Minutes Differently

A real shift occurred when we started recording the goal, not the decision, and at the end of the meeting, broke it down into steps. Recording the goal during the meeting and breaking it down into specific steps at the end – this is the rule that truly changed the situation.

Here's the mechanism using the lead distribution issue as an example. Instead of "we agreed to improve lead distribution," we record the goal at the meeting: "leads should go to an available manager, not the one who saw the notification first."

Then, at the end of the meeting, we break the goal down into steps – set up queuing in the CRM, agree with the sales department on a new procedure, inform regular clients about a possible manager change. Each step already has a natural performer and deadline because the step is specific, not general – you don't need to invent them separately, they emerge from the breakdown itself.

From this rule, smaller habits also grew:

  • naming the responsible person aloud in the moment, rather than implying it;

  • not postponing documentation – what isn't recorded immediately after the meeting usually isn't recorded at all;

  • reviewing the list of open tasks once a week to see what's lagging in time.

The first point sounds simple on paper, but in practice, naming someone responsible in front of the whole team feels like pointing a finger at them – especially if the task is unpleasant. We solve this with a question. We ask, "Who will take it?" and pause, even if it's awkward.

In nine out of ten cases, someone names themselves – the candidate is usually already clear to everyone in the room, it's just that no one had voiced the name aloud before. A person who says, "Let me," takes on the task differently than someone who was pointed at: they have publicly committed themselves, and it's no longer possible to back out unnoticed.

Diffusion of responsibility doesn't disappear completely because of this – it just moves down a level. If the goal at the meeting is formulated as vaguely as the decision was previously, the effect will return, but now in the formulation of the steps.

The rule and the tool reliably remove the symptom. Effective meetings begin with a specific person articulating the goal aloud at the meeting – this is still manual work that no tool can replace.
