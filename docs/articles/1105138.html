What I Mean by Artistic Value

For this experiment, I will define artistic value as the extent to which the text accurately and subtly portrays a person's character and their experiences. It's important to me that the author leaves room for the reader's own conclusions, and after a scene, the reader has something to ponder. For example, a familiar behavior reveals a contradiction, an ordinary object gains significance, or something said by a character takes on a completely different meaning.

How the Model Works with Fictional Text

In the generated scenes, I will check how consistently the model connects characters, their thoughts, and actions. I will analyze context handling using the autoregressive transformer as an example.

From Tokens to Connections

First, the text is broken down into tokens, each of which is assigned a vector representation, i.e., a set of numbers. In the model's layers, these representations are modified based on context and token order. The attention mechanism calculates the weight with which to consider information from other available text positions. This is then processed by other model blocks. This scheme is described in the paper Attention Is All You Need.

In our scene, the model needs to maintain several connections:

Coreference. "The man," "he," and "the player" can refer to the same character. When changing designations, it's important to maintain who is acting.

The word "child" presents another subtlety. It can refer to a boy or a girl, even though grammatically it's masculine. The model chose a boy, although I did not specify the character's gender in the prompt.

Perspective. "Another easy win"—this is how the chess player assesses the game. The model needs to distinguish his confidence from the actual board situation. The narration is in the third person, but the assessment belongs to the character.

In narratology, the selection of information available to the reader is related to focalization. Here, it's important to distinguish who is narrating and whose perception the text conveys. Burkhard Niederhoff writes about this here.

Internal Speech. The character thinks he guesses the other player's move, but he might be thinking something else entirely. The model needs to distinguish his guess from his own thoughts, considering what the reader already knows about each.

In NLP, there are tasks for tracking what each participant in a conversation knows. One example is the FANToM benchmark.

Pragmatics. "Family" is one possible interpretation of the situation where a man and a woman are playing chess, and a child is nearby. In the first generation, the model chose this interpretation and made the child the players' son.

We see that the generator constructed relationships that were not in the prompt. I cannot reconstruct the internal computations that led to this choice from a single response.

What Clarification in the Prompt Changes

At each generation step, the model calculates the probabilities of the next token, considering the prompt and the already written text:

The selected token is added to the context, and then the calculation is repeated.

In normal generation, the model's weights remain unchanged. Clarification changes the input text, which can affect contextual representations and the choice of continuation. In a paper about GPT-3, researchers tested how the model performs tasks based on instructions and examples without updating parameters.

In fictional text, clarifications set the narrative conditions. For example, the request "describe the storm through the eyes of an experienced sailor" indicates whose perspective to convey. The author expects a specific vocabulary and attention to details that such a person would notice.

The prompt can convey to the model a hidden backstory that it should consider but not reveal to the reader. It can also specify what information is already available to the reader and at what point to end the scene.

Is the Neural Network a Writer, or a Reader?

For comparison, I needed my own text, written without any AI assistance. I took a short scene from a story written seven years ago, without any editing—about fifteen hundred characters:

Hidden text

In the room where two people had been moving their black and white pieces for an hour, and a third ran between them, many objects seemed transparent. Both adults were indifferent to most of the furniture. They only needed a chair, a table, and a wardrobe, which often also seemed transparent. Most of the items were created only for the child and by the child. The white walls left dusty traces of plaster on their fingers, and pencils were scattered everywhere on the light gray floor.

The only light source, an uncurtained window, let in so many white rays that the girl's face was almost invisible, and the man opposite her did not remove his dark glasses. Waiting for her move, he lazily rolled a yellow tennis ball across the slightly rough surface of the black table. He enjoyed rolling the ball along the very edge, as if it were about to fall off the table with a clatter. But the ball didn't fall, and its owner watched it with satisfaction, without turning his head a single degree.

A dull wooden clack distracted him. C8. Knight. She is suspiciously intuitive, but would she have enough insight to figure out the Sicilian Defense? He was slightly disappointed. Another easy win. Three moves and checkmate ahead. He would have played the Sicilian Defense first, and then, when the other player moved their rook, he would have immediately launched a Traxler counterattack. She is trusting. He touched the ball again, and then very slowly moved the rook to C7. How could she give up a piece so easily?

In the story, I described ambivalence—the contradictory feelings and impulses a person experiences simultaneously. This internal conflict is shown through the man, the woman, and the child. In the chosen excerpt, the reader should initially see them as separate individuals; their connection is revealed later. The child is not the players' son.

In the first prompt, I only described the external situation:

In a room, a man and a woman are playing chess, with a child nearby. The man is waiting for his opponent's move and is confident he will easily defeat her.

I also asked it to convey the characters' thoughts: the characters address each other internally, imagine responses, and argue with them. The volume was set to be small—1000–1500 characters with spaces.

During the analysis, I checked if the characters' actions were understandable, what the details revealed about them, and what the reader was left to infer. Plus, I verified if my original intent was preserved. A successful scene can easily become a different story.

One interesting outcome of the experiment is that the model fills in missing context using familiar narrative patterns. But this is not the same as an inability to understand a hidden intent.

I progressively refined the prompt and evaluated the artistic choices in the responses. There was no blind reader testing at this stage.

In the first response, the child turned out to be the players' son. He was building a tower out of blocks. The man thought his son needed to learn patience, while the woman saw stubbornness in his attempts. This could be called a successful character development at the local scene level. The model created a conflict of perception: one character sees a lack of patience, the other sees stubbornness.

The scene included an expressive detail:

He once waited for her to finish reading a book to ask if she liked it. Now he was waiting for her move.

This gave the couple a past. Through these two moments of waiting, one can see how their relationship has changed: previously, the man was interested in her opinion of the book; now, he wanted to hear an admission of his superiority.

By the end, the woman made an unexpected move, and the child announced that his tower had finally stood. The parallel was obvious. Then, the man noticed how the woman was holding the board so the pieces wouldn't wobble from the child's play. His attention shifted from the game to her, and the scene moved towards a cautious reconciliation.

From a short prompt, a family story emerged. The model chose kinship for the characters, invented a past for them, and outlined a reconciliation. In the initial prompt, I hadn't yet explained that I was describing ambivalence through these characters. At this stage, one can primarily judge how the model filled in the gaps in the prompt.

What Changed After Clarifications

I explained the hidden meaning of the characters to the model: I am showing ambivalence through their relationship. But I asked it not to reveal this connection to the reader. Within the scene, they were supposed to remain independent individuals, and their shared nature should influence their actions and reactions without being explicitly stated.

In the next version, the child was no longer called a son. He was sorting buttons, and the players argued internally. The plot again moved towards an unexpected chess move by the woman: the man considered her decision a mistake, but she played a check.

Then I described the characters in more detail. The man likes to control things. He can be cruel, but he understands the child quickly. The woman is afraid of losing and is tired of the game. She sympathizes with the child, although she also pushes him away when he interferes. The child tries to express what is difficult for him to say in words.

In my story, the man criticizes children's drawings while understanding their feelings and desires faster than the woman. I wanted to preserve this contradiction.

I also had to consider the difference in format. For comparison, I took a fragment from the middle of the story, while I asked the model to create a complete vignette. Such a task pushed it to conclude the scene with a noticeable turn. In the new prompt, I asked it to leave the game and the conflict unresolved.

What the Model Offered in the Third Version

The child rolled a toy car with a tangled thread on its wheel towards the table. The man removed the thread, checked the axle, and returned the toy. Then he turned back to chess.

He immediately responded to the child's difficulty, but left the woman's desire to end the game unanswered. This is where the contradiction I needed appeared.

The model interpreted the condition "understands the child" quite literally. In my story, the man deciphers drawings that represent feelings and desires. In the response, it was enough for him to notice the thread on the wheel. His ability to understand the child was given a simpler explanation.

The model also added doubt:

He knew she would stay at the table anyway. Or he hoped he knew.

The second phrase calls into question the certainty of the first. Such doubt can be left if it serves the character's purpose in this scene.

Another line directly formulated the woman's situation:

You always give me a choice where I'm already guilty.

The phrase accurately describes her situation and immediately explains the relationship dynamics to the reader. Whether it suits the character is a separate question.

A discrepancy in actions was also found. At the beginning, the man holds his hand over a piece, then fixes the toy car, and at the end, "finally took his hand off the piece." When he managed to return it to the board is not shown in the text. The final pause is constructed as if his hand had remained in place the entire time.

By the third version, the scene more closely matched the prompt. The next step was to decide: should the toy car remain, does the man need doubt, and is the woman's situation so clearly articulated suitable for her?

How to Work with a Neural Network on Your Story

I would not call a fully generated story my own work. The responsibility for the text, both moral and legal, lies with the person who publishes it and claims authorship.

Of course, as a reader, I would prefer to read entirely author-generated texts. I find neural networks applicable in literature for improving skills or for editing, and I believe they are a good tool for those who want to try writing but are afraid. However, in the creative process, neural networks can strip the text of style and make it somewhat impersonal.

In my experiment, expressive elements appeared as early as the first response. Along with them, the model invented kinship and a past for the characters, and then twice led the scene to an unexpected chess move. To approach my original intent, I needed to explain the characters' relationships and specify the exact fragment I wanted.

For working on your story, I would suggest the following sequence.

  1. Write down what the characters want and what hinders them. Describe what the character wants and why it's important to them. Behind strictness might lie a fear of error or a pleasure in power. Also, clarify character contradictions: for example, a character demands independence from colleagues but interferes in their every decision. The model will have more basis for choosing actions than when described by a single word like "strict."

  2. Inform the model about what the reader doesn't yet know. Describe the characters' relationships, the world's rules, and the hidden intent. Specify what the reader should learn now and what later. For example, the characters have known each other for a long time, although they pretend to see each other for the first time upon meeting. The model needs this backstory: without it, it might invent a different explanation for their behavior.

  3. Clarify the scene's place in the story. A complete vignette and a fragment from the middle of a story require different solutions. If the conflict is meant to continue, state it directly. In my case, the request to write a miniature naturally pushed the model towards a noticeable final turn.

  4. Check how the model interpreted each condition. For example, you described a character as reserved. In the model's response, they remained silent after an offensive remark. Are they afraid of an argument, trying to spare the other person, or considering them unworthy of a response? Each explanation fits the action but reveals the character differently. Check which motive appeared in the text and how well it aligns with your intent.

  5. Verify actions and information distribution. Check who hears the remarks, to whom imagined responses belong, and where objects are located. In the third version, the man was fixing a toy car, and at the end, "finally took his hand off the piece." When he managed to return it to the board is not stated in the text. It turned out as if he had kept his hand on the piece the whole time, even though he had time to untangle the thread on the wheel between the two actions.

  6. Edit the scene based on your decisions. Decide whether the character needs a sudden flash of doubt, how well the heroine understands her own situation, and whether she should articulate it aloud. After that, check the phrases: what does each add to the scene, which explanations repeat what has already been shown, where does the character's speech sound too convenient for the reader. Replacing a few words with synonyms will not accomplish this task.

I would use the model's suggestions as material for further work. The author still needs to understand why a character acts in a certain way, what the reader learns in that scene, and in what words to convey it.
