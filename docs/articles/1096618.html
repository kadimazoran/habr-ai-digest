Technological singularity is usually discussed as a point after which artificial intelligence will discover new laws, create technologies, and improve its own models at such a speed that humans will be left to use the results of its work.

Today, most of the devices around us seem almost magical to those unfamiliar with their workings. But within this "magic" are principles, research, calculations, and engineering solutions understandable to humans. At the same time, no single person holds all knowledge in their head. It exists as a common heritage of many scientists and specialists, which is transmitted, verified, and developed. Science, long before the advent of AI, became a collective way of understanding the world.

What if the main result of AI development also enters this common pool of knowledge?

Imagine that artificial intelligence helps discover a mathematical theory that is understandable to humans but also profound. One that makes previously separate areas of mathematics visible as parts of a single structure. Prepared specialists will be able to master its principles and apply them independently. And if the new theory simultaneously provides a significant advantage in already known calculations, the distribution of influence among people in society will change first and foremost.

This is the only condition set by the article.

Let's call this scenario Intelligible Singularity — in contrast to the usual "black box singularity." Its essence is that fundamental knowledge discovered with the help of AI is returned to humans in a compact, clear, and self-developable form.

The Gray Sphere: Knowledge Always Comes with Consequences

In 2019, philosopher Nick Bostrom proposed the "Vulnerable World Hypothesis." In his thought experiment, humanity draws discoveries from a huge urn. White balls bring mostly benefits, gray balls have mixed consequences, and a black ball, with the current societal structure, makes civilizational catastrophe extremely likely. Bostrom also notes that technologies from the urn can be linked: one discovery brings a whole group of others closer.

The history of science has given us many gray balls, especially among the most powerful technologies. Nuclear physics is a striking example. It opened the way to atomic weapons (placing the world under the threat of self-destruction) and also provided nuclear energy, radiation therapy, medical diagnostics, and many research methods. Outside the scientific laboratory, the fate of technology has been determined more by the political decisions of individuals and the structure of civilization as a whole.

A fundamental mathematical method in this picture is a special kind of gray ball: it increases the speed and reduces the cost of obtaining new results, so its consequences can manifest in many industries in a relatively short period.

How New Ideas Changed Scientific Capabilities

In the mid-19th century, James Clerk Maxwell unified research on electricity and magnetism into a general theory of the electromagnetic field. His equations linked light with electromagnetic waves and predicted phenomena later confirmed experimentally. A holistic physical picture emerged from previously separate fields, upon which radio communication and many other technologies eventually grew. The History of Maxwell's Equations.

In the early 20th century, the mathematical apparatus of infinite-dimensional structures, laid down by David Hilbert, turned out to be an ideal candidate 15 years later for constructing a rigorous language for quantum mechanics. Then, in 1927, John von Neumann gave it its modern abstract form and applied it to quantum theory. Thus, mathematics, developed for one set of problems, became the foundation for understanding another branch of practical science. Klaas Landsman's historical study.

In 2017, Ashish Vaswani's group introduced the Transformer architecture in the paper Attention Is All You Need. In this example, the attention mechanism itself already existed, but the new model organization significantly improved machine learning results with considerably less time expenditure.

In all three stories, the new knowledge structure remained accessible to human understanding and significantly changed the scientific landscape. The next leap may combine these effects in several areas simultaneously.

Metamathematics of Singularity, Accessible to Humans

Algebra, geometry, analysis, combinatorics, information theory, and dynamical systems have long been exchanging methods. As science develops, new correspondences are discovered between them, and the search for a common language for these connections is already moving into the practical realm.

As early as in the paper How Intelligible Is Intelligence? (2010), Anna Salamon, Steven Reick, and Janos Kramar considered the possibility of a compact theory of intelligence, the discovery of which could lead to a sharp conceptual leap. In 2026, the authors of Compression Is All You Need: Modeling Mathematics explore the compression of mathematical derivations, and Artificial Intelligence and the Structure of Mathematics raises the question of global connections between mathematical proofs that AI can help discover.

Such compression of mathematical structures is quite likely to lead to new algorithms that significantly reduce the computational costs for current technological tasks.

The Gray Wall: Capabilities Grow Faster Than Self-Defense

With the advent of an effective mathematical core, the world's accumulated computing power becomes more than sufficient for a fully general AI on various devices. And the entire existing infrastructure, without years of waiting for new chips, begins to transition to a qualitatively new architecture in a short period, accelerating by orders of magnitude.

The gray wall is an image of a critical time gap between the speed of new technological capabilities emerging and the speed of deploying defenses against them. In this window, colossal intellectual power (including its destructive side) can fall into the hands of many independent groups long before society can react. After all, a pure mathematical method spreads worldwide in a matter of weeks, while rebuilding digital infrastructure, international agreements, regulations, and security institutions takes incomparably more time.

This is where the main nerve of Intelligible Singularity lies: it cannot be locked away in a corporate data center. A black box requires gigawatts of energy and complex infrastructure that can be regulated and guarded. But human-understandable mathematics spreads by itself at the speed of reading text. Intelligible Singularity democratizes the power of new knowledge faster than society can build defenses against the consequences of technologies made accessible by this knowledge.

It is quite plausible that as a result of such a singularity, even a relatively small group of strong interdisciplinary mathematicians and engineers on just a few server racks could deploy a Mathematical General Intellect (MGI). Of course, some research will continue to rely on colossal industrial capacities. But even such a scenario can increase the number of independent advanced research centers by orders of magnitude.

Naturally, states and major corporations will retain colossal advantages in infrastructure, computational scale, and resources. However, access to a compact mathematical method provides the ability to independently acquire knowledge and create technologies that are today available only to a select few.

Let's take one specific example: MGI discovers a classical algorithm that allows for the rapid factorization of large numbers on relatively modest computational resources. The accessible computations undermine the security of RSA—a fundamental family of encryption and digital signature systems. Organizations are forced to urgently re-verify trust in some electronic documents, connections, and keys. Banks, cloud services, and government agencies introduce emergency measures, international communications become more cautious, and infrastructure updates take months. At the same time, other cryptographic systems have their own mathematical foundations, so they are not affected (e.g., quantum ones). But even a local blow to a basic trust mechanism can cause a global systemic crisis.

The same intellectual power will accelerate the creation of defenses and provide new opportunities for those willing to cause harm. We already know this duality from the nuclear age. The difference will primarily be in the number of participants and the speed of new technology dissemination. Michael Nielsen, in his essay ASI Existential Risk: Reconsidering Alignment as a Goal (2025), links some of the risks of superintelligence to the power it can provide to humans. When the carrier of this power is mathematical knowledge that humans can reproduce, develop, and understand independently, its dissemination speed becomes maximal.

Humanity Has Already Become One — Now We Need to Learn to Live as a Whole

Look at the screen you are reading this text on. Behind the ability to see a few lines are generations of mathematicians, physicists, engineers, and workers. Ultimately, billions of people, through their labor, have created the civilization that allows you to read these lines now. Technologically, we already live as a single planetary organism.

However, culturally and socially, we often continue to live as competing and sometimes hostile groups. The power of technology grows thanks to the collective labor of humanity, while decisions about its application are made by individuals within many conflicting interests.

Therefore, the gray wall, under such a hypothetical development, appears to be a possible civilizational filter. The preservation of civilization will then depend on how quickly we can develop common rules of safety, trust, and responsibility without destroying the diversity of human life.

Cultural Unity and Turning Points

As long as our knowledge was fragmented and technologies imperfect, physical limitations served as a natural barrier to our disunity. But when knowledge becomes open and the lever of action approaches the absolute, the only real factor for survival becomes how people treat each other. This is precisely what becomes a strict engineering condition for the existence of civilization.

History has already known eras when rapid technological and social crises demanded a complete rethinking of morality. Karl Jaspers described the "Axial Age" (800–200 BCE) as a turning point when traditions synchronously turned to human dignity and self-awareness. Columbia University overview of the Axial Age.

As early as the 20th century, Jacque Fresco pointed out the structural contradiction of our civilization: humanity already possesses technologies capable of providing for everyone's needs, but this alone has not led to universal well-being. Singularity may repeat the same story: people will continue to work, and their dependence on technologies and the companies that own them will only increase.

Today, the issue of civilizational unity becomes a practical condition for technological safety. By unity, I mean primarily the understanding and experience of oneness: whether in a spiritual, cultural, or scientific form—it doesn't matter. The main condition is the ability to perceive another person as a participant in common life, while preserving their independence and dignity.

And here, Intelligible Singularity inevitably sheds its technical shell and reveals its root cause—the singularity of human relations.

For a civilization with greatly increased technical power, this could practically become a variant of Robin Hanson's Great Filter (The Great Filter). And it is not impossible that any developed civilization goes through a path close to ours in one way or another (alas, we are not unique again) and all civilizations find the same philosophical grail of knowledge. At the same time, the condition for passing the filter is not this knowledge itself, but primarily the balance of human relationships, in the presence of an almost universally accessible absolute ("divine") source of power.

One condition—the gift of this power is viable only in conjunction with the immortality of civilization as a whole, not of an individual. Any personal dreams of radical life extension or technological salvation are illusory if the environment they inhabit is destroyed: individual longevity cannot be separated from the collective safety of the species.

We have approached the possibility of possessing almost divine technologies. The question is whether we will become the unified whole from Plato's "Parmenides," where no part can survive apart from the whole, before the flash of this knowledge blinds us.
