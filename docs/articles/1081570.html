Why a dispute over two former employees could be a dispute about what comes after the smartphone.

On July 10, 2026, Apple filed a lawsuit in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California against OpenAI and two of its former employees, Chen Liu and Tan Yu Tan. The company alleges that OpenAI gained access to confidential information about Apple's yet-to-be-released products, their design, manufacturing processes, and supplier relationships to accelerate the development of its own AI-powered consumer device. The defendants deny the accusations, and OpenAI has already asked the court to dismiss the lawsuit. Therefore, everything related to the alleged theft of trade secrets remains an assertion by one party, not an established fact. But even at this early stage, the case is interesting not just as another Silicon Valley scandal. Behind the dispute over files, prototypes, and the boundaries of permissible employee transitions lies a conflict between two visions of the future—and two technological paradigms.

Apple has created one of the most successful models of modern technological civilization. At its core is a perfectly designed physical object—a computer, phone, watch, headphones—and the company's strength has always lain not in any single invention, but in its ability to integrate a device, operating system, design, and human daily habits into a unified environment. This philosophy is most fully embodied in the iPhone, which became not just Apple's most successful product, but the central artifact of the digital age. Through a small screen, people talk, work, read, take photos, buy, sell, meet, argue, navigate, and access almost all of humanity's accumulated knowledge. For two decades, the phone has been the center of gravity of our technological lives.

OpenAI belongs to a different logic. Its main product is not an object that a person holds in their hand, but an intelligence with which they engage in dialogue. In this system, the device becomes not the center, but an intermediary—a temporary shell through which artificial intelligence sees, hears, speaks, and acts. The difference may seem minor, but in reality, it is fundamental: in Apple's world, a person opens the desired application and performs a specific sequence of actions, whereas in the world that AI companies are trying to build, a person formulates an intention, and the system itself chooses the necessary applications, sources, and tools.

We are accustomed to thinking that we use a phone. The next stage of technological development may be structured differently: we will use not a device, but an intelligence capable of interacting with devices on our behalf. This, of course, does not mean the immediate disappearance of the iPhone. Technological eras rarely end overnight: the automobile did not destroy the railway, television did not destroy radio, the internet did not destroy the book. Old forms continue to exist but cease to define the direction of movement. Therefore, the main question is not whether the smartphone will disappear, but whether it will remain the primary intermediary between humans and the digital world. If the answer is no, Apple will face not just a new competitor, but a change in the very principle on which its power is built.

For decades, the power of major tech companies has been based on controlling access to the digital environment. Microsoft controlled the personal computer operating system, Google controlled search and information flow on the internet, and Apple controlled the device and ecosystem through which hundreds of millions of people access applications and services. Artificial intelligence can change this architecture. A person conversing with an intelligent system no longer needs to know where a particular function is located, choose between dozens of applications, navigate menus, or manually construct a chain of operations. They explain what they want, and what becomes primary is no longer the screen, or even the operating system, but the entity that best understands human intent.

This is precisely why the appearance of individuals who played a crucial role in creating the previous era alongside OpenAI has almost historical symbolism. Jony Ive and his colleagues once shaped the visual and physical language of the smartphone world. In 2025, OpenAI acquired io Products, a company founded by Ive, to develop a new family of AI devices together with its team. Now, specialists associated with Apple's design tradition are seeking a form for the post-smartphone world—or at least a world where the smartphone loses its former exclusivity. For Apple, this is not ordinary competition. It is almost a rebellion within its own aesthetic school: the people who created the material form of the digital age are now trying to create the form for the next age.

Of course, the philosophical scale of the conflict does not negate the specific legal issues. An employee's freedom to move to a competitor does not grant them the right to take confidential developments with them, and a belief in innovation does not exempt a company from the law. If Apple's accusations are proven, it will not be about a romantic confrontation between the old and the new, but about a very specific infringement of rights. However, the reverse is also true: even a potential infringement does not explain the full intensity of what is happening. Large corporations defend their secrets particularly fiercely when they feel that a rival is approaching the very source of their power. A lawsuit becomes not only a way to seek compensation or stop a specific project but also a defensive line drawn around an entire economic model.

The history of technology has repeatedly changed its main protagonist. The world centered around IBM's large mainframe computers gave way to the personal computer and Microsoft's software; then the internet became crucial, along with it, Google; finally, the computer ended up in the palm of the hand, and the iPhone became the symbol of the new era. Each of these companies rose to prominence not because they produced a better version of an existing product, but because they managed to define the central element of the next system. Today, artificial intelligence may become that element.

This is why it is incorrect to view what is happening solely as a war between Apple and OpenAI. Two economic and philosophical models are clashing. One is built around the perfect device, the other around a constantly present intelligence. In the former, a person touches a screen, opens a program, and operates a tool; in the latter, they converse with a system that interprets their intent and acts with them. However, there is an even more radical possibility: the future may abandon the idea of a single primary device altogether.
