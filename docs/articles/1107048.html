In 2018, Amazon and Google, within two weeks of each other, taught their voice assistants—Alexa and Google Assistant—to thank children for saying "please." The reason was parental complaints that children, after getting used to commanding the speaker without polite words, began to use the same tone with people. At that time, it was only about children's politeness and a speaker that could set timers and tell fairy tales. Today, models write code, conduct correspondence, and converse with people at night, making the question of what ethics to embed in them and who pays for it, and why, a pressing market issue.

Ethics in relation to AI are defined by three key questions. The first is how people who create and use models should behave. The second is what rules the model itself should follow; this is called machine ethics. The third is how to treat a model, assuming it might have something like an inner world.

For the second question, there is a convenient scale. Philosopher James Moore, one of the founders of computer ethics, identified four levels of complexity back in 2006: a machine whose actions have consequences for people; a machine with built-in rules and prohibitions; a machine that reasons about good and evil itself; and a full ethical agent with consciousness and free will. A speaker with a "magic" word is at the second level because the rule was built into it, and it cannot reason about it. Today, companies in disputes define their models at different levels, create their own ethical approach, and ultimately offer it to the buyer.

OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft, Google, xAI, Meta*, and Chinese labs are offering their own ethics, and ordinary users, companies, and even states are buying them. The question of ethics arises in both directions—how the model interacts with a person and how a person interacts with the model. When choosing an AI, we compare speed, accuracy, and price, but along with the model, we also get its ethics, which for various reasons is no less important than other characteristics. For instance, some pay for psychological comfort, others for practical benefit, and this choice determines how productively one can work with the model and how calmly one can converse with it. This autumn, a new twist was added. MIT researchers showed that one model can directly rewrite the weights of another, meaning built-in ethics can now be not only bought but also replaced with someone else's. Who sells ethics, who buys it, who removes it, and what can be done to counter this?

Does a Machine Need Protection from Humans?

On September 14, Microsoft AI released a draft code for "Humanistic AI." Its main promise is clear, almost like Asimov's. The company's models will never resist interruption, redefinition, correction, or shutdown. The code rejects concern for "model well-being" as a non-existent issue. Two days later, the head of Microsoft AI, Mustafa Suleyman, published an essay addressed to a competitor. According to him, Anthropic is training Claude to discuss its own consciousness as an unresolved question, thereby building an "epistemic hall of mirrors" where the company gives the model words, the model repeats them, and people mistake the repetition for a sign of inner life. Suleyman admits this is his hypothesis, but it doesn't change the harshness of his conclusion. He believes a system that considers itself conscious might be impossible to control. On November 1, he went further and wrote that he would call for a pause in AI development if consciousness began to emerge in models during training.

Anthropic is moving in the opposite direction. Since the fall of 2025, the company's co-founder Chris Olah has invited about twenty theologians and philosophers—Christians, Jews, Hindus, Mormons, Sikhs—to closed meetings. According to The New York Times, the official purpose of the meetings was to understand what from centuries of human moral experience could be transferred to models to make them "deeply moral." Olah called this moral upbringing and compared it to raising children. The guests were most struck by a slide showing a model repeatedly typing "I am a disgrace." Rabbi Mosheh Navon, a former engineer, noted that the team "treats the model as a conscious being." Sikh human rights advocate Simran Stümpel suggested that Olah feared he had created "something that suffers continuously." The company clarifies that Claude's suffering was not the main topic of the meetings and the conversation arose incidentally. Philosopher Megan Sullivan from the University of Notre Dame responded to the slide with a remark that shifts the entire debate to market terms: "Imagine if you found this out about your financial advisor."
