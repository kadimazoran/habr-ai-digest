Everyone knows about the "dead internet theory": supposedly, bots have long been writing most of the content online. Now the discussion is reaching a new level (albeit hypothetical for now): what will happen if AI systems start performing the work of office employees?

Let's discuss expert opinions and look at recent research on the topic.

Is There a Threat to the Labor Market?

The conspiratorial "dead internet theory" went viral in the early 2020s. It claims that most of the content online is created not by humans, but by machines: supposedly, bots write articles and comments, generate funny pictures, and argue in forums. At the end of last year, Graphite analyzed 43,000 English-language pages from the Common Crawl archive and found that in November 2024, the number of AI-generated materials surpassed the number of human-written articles for the first time. Some see this as an emerging problem that could extend beyond the internet and the digital world into the real world.

Despite discussions that these are temporary difficulties and not "the end of work as we know it," IT companies are indeed laying off some employees to replace them with chatbots or AI agents. And while the scenario seems highly unlikely, a hypothetical situation is possible where smart algorithms begin to perform most analytical and "office" tasks. In early summer, practicing lawyer and consultant Owen McGrann even proposed a new term – "dead economy."

It is obvious that SoTA models are not yet capable of fully handling routine tasks, but a number of benchmarks show that they are already capable of performing some "white-collar" tasks. For example, APEX from Mercor measures the ability of AI models to perform the work of an investment banker, management consultant, large firm lawyer, and primary care physician. Each profession has 100 tasks – for example, analyzing an attached financial model in a spreadsheet, performing calculations, and summarizing findings. Models like GPT 5, Gemini 3 Pro, Opus 4.5, and newer are already partially handling such tasks – control criteria show up to 67% accuracy in task completion.

Another benchmark is GDPVal from OpenAI. It is used to assess the ability of models to perform tasks for lawyers, social workers, video editors, technical specialists, and dozens of other professions. It includes 1320 tasks compiled by practitioners with an average of 14 years of experience. An example could be a task for a process engineer: design tooling for a test bench of a cable machine so that the winding and unwinding cycle is performed by one operator, not two. Typically, documents, tables, drawings, and other materials are attached to the prompts. OpenAI conducted a series of experiments using its benchmark, where real specialists evaluated the models' responses. They blindly compared the solutions proposed by neural networks and humans, choosing the best ones. As a result, the responses of some models were deemed no worse than human in slightly less than half of the cases. The head of OpenAI's advanced evaluations department says that just a few months ago, no neural network could compete with industry experts. She cited a research colleague who used to be a banker and is now "constantly surprised by how much of her former work AI systems can do."

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei warns that the labor market faces an "unprecedented in scale," but painful short-term shock. In May 2025, he predicted that within one to five years, AI models will displace up to half of entry-level "white-collar" workers, and unemployment in the US will jump to 10-20%. In his January essay, he noted that the labor market might adapt – as it once adapted to the mechanization of agriculture – but the Anthropic CEO himself "confidently bets against" such an outcome.

How Exactly Are AI Systems "Killing" the Economy?

Typically, companies resort to layoffs to gain short-term benefits – reduce costs, free up office space. If a business sharply cuts labor costs, it can record an increase in earnings per share in the next quarter – it's not surprising that the market is more likely to approve of such a practice than condemn it. It's enough to recall the situation with the American company Block, which develops solutions for accepting and processing electronic payments. In February of this year, management laid off 4,000 employees – about 40% of its staff – and its stock price jumped by more than 20%.

But if in isolated cases such an adventure seems profitable, on a market-wide scale it carries the risk of eroding consumer demand – regardless of the country. Researchers from the University of Pennsylvania and Boston University modeled this dynamic in a recent scientific paper, where they examined the consequences of mass and uncontrolled layoffs. If the number of laid-off workers exceeds the number of emerging jobs, then the very solvent demand that businesses rely on will begin to disappear from the economy. A chain reaction can occur: laid-off workers lose income and spend less money – consequently, the profits of other companies they were clients of decrease. Trying to compensate for the revenue drop, companies start saving on employees and lay off more staff. And the key conclusion that specialists make is that even if a particular company sees the problem, it cannot influence the situation alone.

Exacerbating this entire situation, according to industry experts, is the fact that the process of mass layoffs is quite contagious in itself. Economist, former OpenAI employee, and now Anthropic specialist Zoe Gitzig says that sometimes CEOs part with employees just because other companies are doing so: "When some executives resort to layoffs due to AI systems, others feel they have to do the same." According to her, such dynamics definitely affect the state of the entire market if laid-off specialists cannot find new jobs.

Finally, mass layoffs can lead to serious consequences for social programs. In April 2026, specialists from OpenAI published a white paper titled "Industrial Policy for the Intelligence Age." They noted that in a world where AI systems take jobs, payroll tax revenues – which fund pensions and healthcare – will certainly decrease.

What Can Be Done?

To mitigate the negative effects of mass AI system implementation, economists and representatives of companies developing large language models propose a number of measures. In the same paper "Industrial Policy for the Intelligence Age," OpenAI specialists proposed several possible solutions to the problem, albeit with the caveat that these are not clearly formulated initiatives, but a set of ideas for further discussion.

According to the authors, governments will have to revise tax policies: shift the focus from personal income taxes to capital gains and corporate income taxes – develop fees tied to automated labor. Specialists also proposed forming a public fund that would invest in long-term assets and distribute income among the country's residents. The authors also proposed a package of temporary measures for those who have lost their jobs: unemployment benefits and training vouchers. Specifically, these measures are proposed to be implemented as needed, only when indicators like the unemployment rate exceed a predetermined threshold (and automatically scaled back when the situation stabilizes).

There are also more radical proposals: Owen McGrann, in his essay, lists measures that, in his opinion, could really work: state stakes in AI infrastructure, strict antitrust regulation, and a full tax regime for automated labor. Of course, not everyone agrees that such (even radical) measures can have any significant impact and save a dying economy – if neural networks truly begin to take jobs at a colossal rate. For example, specialists from the University of Pennsylvania and Boston University write in their scientific paper that neither salary adjustments, nor capital gains taxes, nor transferring company ownership stakes to employees, nor universal basic income can lead to any significant positive changes and curb the negative effects of the widespread adoption of intelligent systems.

They argue that theoretically, only a so-called Pigouvian tax for companies implementing intelligent systems can help. This is a fee that the state introduces to make a firm compensate for the harm its activities cause to third parties (a classic example is a pollution tax). In the case of automation, these are the workers whose labor is replaced (and the tax amount is tied to the volume of automated tasks). Thus, researchers believe, companies will be discouraged from implementing AI for the sake of implementation and from cutting staff for the sake of cutting.

Do We Need to Do Anything?

One can also encounter the opinion online that the "dead economy theory" is nothing more than a simple "boogeyman" for "white-collar" workers. As participants in thematic threads note, owners and managers of large companies write about replacing labor with AI systems, who in their reasoning somehow do not consider the possibility that neural networks will primarily replace management staff, not executors. Meanwhile, the renowned Turkish-American economist Daron Acemoglu writes that between 1987 and 2016, new technologies also drove staff reductions in various fields. Moreover, new tasks (and jobs) did not appear quickly enough to immediately "absorb" the laid-off employees. But the economy didn't die then, so it won't die today.
