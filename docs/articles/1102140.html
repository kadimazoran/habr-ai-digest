Or why AI will most likely not replace you, and that's bad news

Why should you listen to me? I'm definitely not an IT guru, but I've seen this story from both sides. I built HR products for American big tech, meaning I know how you are evaluated, plus now I'm implementing agents in various companies and see what businesses actually think about you. And on the other hand, I'm an IT person myself who has spent several winters working from Asia, drinks filter coffee, can't tell it apart from Ethiopian, and wouldn't survive a week in Russia without Yandex Go.

Everyone is discussing whether AI will replace programmers, but almost no one is discussing what will happen if it doesn't.

I think it won't. Rather, there will be even more of us. It sounds like good news, but for most of us, it's the worst-case scenario.

To explain why, we first have to honestly talk about what we were paid for in the first place.

What We Were Paid For

The average doctor sits in a clinic, the average lawyer edits contracts from 9 to 6, and the average programmer has calls with their team lead from Bali and assigns 3 days to a task they'll finish in an evening. Their salary is like that of a factory director in their hometown, and their boss is afraid they'll leave.

Honestly, most people didn't go into IT because they dreamed of compilers since childhood. They went for this all-inclusive package, and programming was just a bonus. And that's okay. Stack Overflow asks developers every year if they are happy at work, and in 2025, only 24.5% said "yes." Another 47% chose the option "complacent," something like "It's fine, leave me alone."

The standard answer for why all this was paid for is scarcity. The market never cared about your love for code; it paid because the desired result was hard to obtain without you. This is true, but it's only half the answer.

You know the other half yourselves, but it's just not usually spoken aloud. Remember that task you finished on Tuesday, but it sat in Jira until Friday? The manager couldn't figure out why a button took two weeks to make, story points were essentially a ritual where both sides agreed not to delve into the details, and the phrase "it's complicated" shut down any conversation.

And in this gap between "how long it took" and "how long was stated" lies all the good life: the gym during lunch, a second job with a second remote position (people like that also read this post), calls from the beach. If you haven't done any of this, write in the comments, I want to see you.

We'll talk more about "it's complicated," by the way, as it's the most important thing here, in my opinion.

How We Ruined It Ourselves

Once the path became clear, an industry naturally grew around it. Courses like "Get into IT," roadmaps, interview simulators, and on top of that, the government added draft deferment and preferential mortgages. According to a Habr Career survey, 29% of IT professionals inflated their experience on their resumes, and 88% of them passed probation. This means a whole market of services emerged for getting into the profession without mastering it. In medicine, this is punishable by imprisonment.

The word "IT specialist" then ceased to mean anything. It used to be a weird guy who liked computers, but now it's a guy who likes remote work. But as long as there was scarcity, nobody cared.

What Does AI Have to Do With It?

We've somehow conflated two statements: AI makes a developer stronger → therefore, a developer is now worth more. But the second doesn't follow from the first at all; everyone was given the tool. You've become better than you were last year, but whether you've become better than everyone else is a big question.

Anthropic recently released data about themselves: non-technical employees had 2 sessions with coding agents per month, now it's 90; managers commit twice as much; and the share of juniors in technical hiring dropped from 27% to 9%. The scarcity issue is generally clear.

Now about "it's complicated." A year ago, I wrote here that all the necessary context is stored in the developers' heads, and AI doesn't have it. This was true, and it was, by the way, the best job security in the history of the profession. Only now, every team that properly implements agents is dismantling that security itself, because otherwise, the agents don't work: knowledge moves from heads to docs, decisions to tickets, meetings to transcripts. And anything recorded for the agent can be read by the boss (or their agent).

Plus, a manager can now ask Claude how long something should take, or even cobble together a crude prototype in an evening and come to a call with it. This annoyed me a year ago, and it still annoys me now, because a prototype and a system in production are different things. But now I have to explain why it can't be rolled out to production, whereas before I could just say "it's complicated."

It's funny that right now, many are having the best months of their careers – the agent does in an hour what you estimated in 3 days (I'm guilty of it myself, I only take some orders because I know how to do them 10 times cheaper than the market). But this won't last. Maybe we can just wait it out?
