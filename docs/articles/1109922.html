Decision Models are a new class of artificial intelligence

Lately, I've been working a lot with a new class of artificial intelligence models – Decision Models. The approaches underlying them have been known to data scientists for a long time. But these models only gained widespread recognition very recently (and some of you might be hearing about them for the first time right now).

What makes them interesting to me? With the advent of LLMs, decision-making models have taken on new colors. But, like all LLMs, they have an interesting drawback: our trust in them is often based more on faith in their capabilities than on statistically confirmed results.

At the same time, they are very attractive for building multi-agent systems. You don't need to train your own models – you just take ready-made, universal ones.

I believe in the immense potential of these models and will continue to work with them in my projects.

Recently, as part of one such project, I conducted several comparative experiments. I want to share their results, but before I show the comparison tables, I need to talk about the models themselves.

That's why ChatGPT and I (greetings from him!) wrote this short reference article.

We are already accustomed to the fact that modern artificial intelligence models can write texts, program, draw, analyze documents, and reason.

But in the fall of 2026, another direction began to actively develop in the AI market – Decision Models, or decision-making models. Sometimes they are called System One Models.

These models are not designed for communication with humans. They are primarily created for operation within programs, automated systems, and AI agents.

A Little History

On September 15, 2026, TypeSafe AI, founded by former OpenAI employee Diogo Almeida, introduced the Jev model.

The developers named the new class of models System One Models, referencing the famous book by psychologist and Nobel laureate Daniel Kahneman, "Thinking, Fast and Slow."

Kahneman described two conditional modes of human thinking:

  • System 1 – fast, intuitive thinking.

  • System 2 – slow, requiring analysis and conscious effort.

For example, we recognize a familiar person on the street almost instantly, but solving a complex mathematical problem takes time.

The authors of Jev proposed a similar division for artificial intelligence.

Just a few weeks later, models from other developers appeared:

  • September 15 – Jev from TypeSafe AI.

  • September 22 – Tev1 from Together AI.

  • September 24 – the open-source Kev model.

  • October 1 – Clef from Cloudflare.

  • October 5 – official presentation of the multimodal d1 from Liquid AI.

  • October 15 – Liquid d1-ultra.

  • October 22 – publicly available Decisions API from OpenAI.

  • November 11 – Gemini Decision 1 from Google.

In less than two months, an entire market of specialized models was formed.

What is a Decision Model?

Imagine we are developing an AI agent that processes customer inquiries.

A message arrives:

"I paid for my order yesterday, the money was debited, but the order hasn't appeared in my personal account."

We need to determine where to direct this inquiry:

  • delivery department;

  • technical support;

  • accounting department;

  • sales department.

You could send the message to ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, or DeepSeek and ask them to choose the appropriate option.

Or you could use a specialized Decision Model, which would return something like this:

Technical Support – 87%
Accounting – 10%
Delivery Department – 2%
Sales Department – 1%

No reasoning, explanations, or additional comments. Only a result that a program can directly use.

Moreover, most such models allow solving three main types of tasks:

Choice – selecting one of the proposed options.

Which specialist to transfer the inquiry to? Which tool to use? Which action to perform next?

Noul (Yes/No) – evaluating the truthfulness of a condition.

Does the document meet the requirements? Is there an error in the response? Is human intervention required?

Score – evaluation on a given scale.

How well was the work done? How urgent is the task? How relevant is the document to the user's request?

Many models allow asking multiple questions about the same object and receiving answers in a single request.

But wait, classifiers have existed for a long time!

This is probably the question that will arise for people familiar with machine learning.

Indeed. Logistic regression, decision trees, random forests, CatBoost, neural network classifiers – all of these have existed for many years.

And the very idea of classification without special training on specific categories (zero-shot classification) has been studied for more than just a few years.

So what's new here?

Let's imagine a classic classifier for banking operations. We trained it on historical data to detect fraudulent transactions.

It can perform its task perfectly. Fast, cheap, and with high accuracy.

But if tomorrow we want to use this same classifier to evaluate the quality of customer support responses, it won't help us.

For a different task, a different model or additional training is usually required.

Decision Models attempt to combine the universality of modern language models with the speed and convenience of classic classifiers.

Today, we ask the same model to determine the topic of an email.

Tomorrow – to check compliance with instructions.

The day after tomorrow – to select an action for a robot in a virtual world.

Moreover, we can specify the answer options and evaluation criteria directly in the query, without retraining the model for each new task.

Essentially, it's a universal classifier that understands natural language and context.

However, it would be a mistake to think that such models will now replace classic machine learning. For well-defined, stable tasks, a regular specialized classifier might be more accurate, faster, and cheaper.

And why are they needed if LLMs can already do all this?

In principle, a regular large language model can perform almost all the tasks listed.

But there are a few nuances.

First – speed.

A regular LLM generates a response sequentially, token by token. Even if we only need a single "yes" or "no" answer, we use the text generation mechanism.

Many Decision Models are structured differently. They evaluate the proposed options in a single neural network pass, without generating text at all.

The response time of some models is measured in tens or hundreds of milliseconds.

Second – cost.

For example, when Jev was launched, the cost was $0.042 per million input tokens. Output data was not charged.

Liquid d1-ultra has a similar cost.

Gemini Decision 1 – $0.10 per million input tokens.

For a single request, the difference might seem insignificant. But imagine a system that processes millions of small decisions daily.

Third – predictability of response format.

An LLM can return text, JSON, an explanation, a refusal, or a result that doesn't match the expected format. Modern Structured Outputs mechanisms can significantly reduce this problem.

Decision Models are initially oriented towards a strictly defined set of possible answers.

At the same time, a correctly formatted answer is not necessarily correct in substance. These models can also make mistakes.

Fourth – assessing self-confidence.

Decision Models return probabilities for possible answers. Ideally, these probabilities should be calibrated.

What does this mean?

If a model claims to be 90% confident in an answer a thousand times, it should be correct approximately 900 times.

This allows building systems where simple cases are processed automatically, and complex ones are passed to more powerful models or humans.

However, calibration needs to be checked on your own data. The mere presence of the number 0.95 in the answer does not mean that the model is actually correct in 95% of similar cases.

Where can this be used?

The scope of application is enormous:

  • distribution of incoming requests among AI agents;

  • selection of tools for task execution;

  • verification of results from other AI models;

  • evaluation of software code quality;

  • content moderation;

  • detection of attempts to manipulate AI agents;

  • analysis and sorting of documents;

  • determining the relevance of search results;

  • control of game characters and robots;

  • automation of business processes.

The application of Decision Models in the architecture of autonomous AI agents is particularly interesting.

Imagine a system where a powerful language model defines the strategy and creates plans, while a small and fast Decision Model constantly analyzes the current situation and selects the next action.

The powerful model is only called upon when a truly complex or non-standard situation arises.

In a sense, this is a division of AI into "brain" and "reflexes."

Although, of course, the analogy with human thinking here is very conditional.

What models already exist?

As of November 15, 2026, there is a choice.
Note. The article was written before it was published on Habr. Changes happen very quickly. For example, on November 17, Liquid AI d1.2 was released.

Cloud models available via API:

  • Jev – TypeSafe AI. The model that started the current wave of interest in this area.

  • Liquid d1.1 and d1-ultra – multimodal models from Liquid AI.

  • Gemini Decision 1 – Google's new model, capable of working with text, images, audio, video, and PDFs.

  • OpenAI Decisions API – a specialized interface based on GPT-6 Luna.

  • Perplexity Decider and Decider Flash v1.3 – models from Perplexity with open weights and a cloud API.

  • Clef, Clef-flash, and Clef-omni – a family of models from Cloudflare.

Models that can be run locally:

  • Kev 4B – based on Qwen3.5.

  • Liquid d1-2B and d1-Omni-2B – compact next-generation models.

  • Liquid d1-omni-600M – an experimental model with only 600 million parameters.

  • Perplexity Decider Flash – a compact version of Decider.

  • Laya – a family of small models based on ModernBERT and mmBERT.

  • Clef and Clef-flash – Cloudflare has published the weights for these models.

  • Tev1 4B and 0.8B – experimental models from Together AI, specialized in response selection.

Moreover, not all these models are structured the same internally. Some have truly abandoned token generation, while others are specially fine-tuned language models that return very short answers.

Thus, for experiments with this class of models, it is no longer necessary to use cloud services or have a powerful server with dozens of video cards.
