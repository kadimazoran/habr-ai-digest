A brief overview of the process: three TTS engines struggled with Russian stress (e.g., 'gudni' read as 'gUdni'), ElevenLabs' pronunciation dictionaries were unavailable on my plan. I relied on the ruaccent gateway and a custom substitution dictionary (51 words). It worked, but manually.

This article explains how I used two Sber models to automate the routine, and the main lesson learned.

GigaChat sets stress

The idea: feed the LLM the entire text bank and collect a lexicon of stresses. GigaChat offers a free tier for a year (250 million Lite tokens, 40 million Pro, 25 million Max, and 50 million Ultra), registration via Client ID, scope GIGACHAT_API_PERS. Integration is a single line:

giga = GigaChat(credentials=..., verify_ssl_certs=False, 
                scope="GIGACHAT_API_PERS", model="GigaChat-2-Pro")

Lite is useless for this task – it's noisy. Only Pro. Processing the entire bank of phrases: 7789 words, resulting in a lexicon of 7462 words (95.8% coverage).

A peculiar behavior of the model: it places the stress mark AFTER the stressed vowel (not before, like ruaccent) and sometimes truncates the end of the word. There was nothing to investigate here: we fixed it in the prompt and parser – and moved on.

The main lesson: large batches hallucinate

This is why this article was worth writing. The lexicon collected in one large run cannot be directly imported into the dictionary. 32 tricky words – like 'proskochila' and 'kuplena' – were only caught by re-checking with small batches: in one run, the model placed the stress one way, in another run, differently, and in a large batch, the errors get buried and look confident. The rule that remained with us forever: a lexicon in bulk is only a source of candidates; a candidate undergoes verification in a small batch; the final word belongs to human eyes when discrepancies arise between runs. Result: +50 words, the dictionary grew from 51 to 101, and I didn't have to guess a single one myself.

GigaAM listens to what we've said

The second task is quality control of the generated voiceover: we transcribe the generated wav and compare the words with the reference. GigaAM v3 (sherpa-onnx, 222 MB weights, MIT license) was used here. For 'clean' dry tracks, it's fast and accurate, but with a radio effect – all voiceovers from 'Channel 15' go through interference and compression – it gets confused, whisper remains. It doesn't process long files entirely: the practical limit is about 50 seconds, so long tracks are cut into 30-second windows. Both tools are integrated into the protocol: stress_suggest.py --giga suggests stresses, qa_recent.py and qa_recast.py --engine gigaam check the fresh resynthesis.

An unexpected bonus: GigaAM also transcribed my six-minute dictaphone recording – in seconds, with almost no errors. On a clean voice without interference, it's simply good.

What didn't work

The Lite model for stresses – garbage. Large batches – see above, this is the main pitfall. GigaAM on 'radio' with effects – gets confused, 'whistles and farts' are not informative signals for it, we stick with whisper. Long tracks – cut into windows, this is not fixable, it's accepted.

Instead of a conclusion

For me, a neural network is a tool, like a screwdriver, only more expensive. The dictionary doubled in size without a single guess from me, and the only word the model couldn't confidently place even once was 'gudni'.
