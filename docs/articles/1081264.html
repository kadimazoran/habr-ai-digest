News headlines like "AI Solved a Millennium Prize Problem" are appearing more frequently. From the outside, it can be hard to assess: where is historical precedent, and where is manufactured hype? And most importantly: what does it all mean for the future of mathematics?

To bring clarity to the issue, it's useful to listen to mathematicians themselves. What do they consider suitable tasks for AI, and what are human tasks? Where do they see progress, and where do they see mere hype? What is their vision of the meaning of mathematics, and how should one approach mathematics consciously in this changed world?

We at Kodik don't deal with mathematics, but with an AI code editor. Nevertheless, our team includes people with classical mathematics education, and we follow the topic with interest. Therefore, we previously translated mathematicians' opinions on the refutation of a specific Erdős problem for Habr, and now we've decided to cover their view on the current situation as a whole. Of course, opinions differ, but in this article, we've tried to gather a snapshot of the mathematical community.

The Main Points in Brief

The subject of discussion is important, so the article is extensive. Here's a concise version for those in a hurry:

Leading LLMs are rapidly improving their mathematical proof capabilities. In 2025, AI couldn't find new proofs; in early 2026, it started producing minor ones; and now, concerning the Navier-Stokes equations, a solution to one of the seven "Millennium Prize Problems" has been announced, with claims that another is nearly solved. Before AI, humanity had only solved one such problem (the Poincaré conjecture).

This doesn't mean AI can prove everything yet, but it's changing the landscape. People have started feeding all sorts of open problems to AI models. Many attempts have been unsuccessful, but the successful ones have spawned a whole stream of results. Terence Tao called this the dawn of an "era of proof abundance": proofs were always scarce—and therefore valuable—in mathematics; now, value is shifting to other aspects of the work.

Proofs are only part of what mathematicians do; AI is currently weaker in other areas. LLMs are not creating new theories, and they write publications about proofs less effectively than humans. Their solutions typically involve using existing approaches rather than conceptual breakthroughs. Therefore, mathematicians don't believe AI has "surpassed everything." However, proofs were previously the "measure of success" for humans, which necessitates a re-evaluation of the situation.

As AI capabilities continue to grow, we are witnessing not the final stage of development. It's already clear that mathematics will transform, but the exact nature of this transformation is a subject of heated debate within the mathematical community.

For now, it's clear that formal validation is becoming more important. When machines generate numerous results (many of which are hallucinations), and people publish them online without full peer review, checking their actual value becomes the new bottleneck, rather than finding proofs. Therefore, tools that help verify the factual significance of new discoveries are gaining importance. Among such verification tools, Lean is popular.

Mathematicians' opinions on AI vary: there are both proponents and opponents. The main collective statement is the "Leiden Declaration," which outlines potential problems with AI use but doesn't call for a boycott; instead, it offers recommendations for its application. For example, the declaration recommends that researchers disclose the use of AI in their work, take responsibility for the results, and assist others in verifying them.

Many criticize specific AI applications rather than AI in mathematics generally. A situation reminiscent of a "gold rush" has emerged, leading to hasty publications of unverified solutions and low-quality texts that contradict academic values. OpenAI faces accusations of potentially having "plagiarized" results related to Navier-Stokes from live mathematicians who consulted their model. It's difficult to say whether this period will last or if the "fever" will soon pass, leaving behind a more thoughtful use of AI.

Many discussions have arisen among mathematicians, not just about AI itself. For instance, if machines take over the tasks by which academic success was previously measured, a meta-question arises: by what criteria should scientific value be assessed? How should the scientific process be organized?

Some mathematicians are convinced that evaluating scientists based on the number of proofs has always been an incorrect approach, as the primary goal is understanding, not the quantity of PDF files produced. A new topic of discussion has emerged: "What are the goals of fundamental mathematics in general?" The goals of applied mathematics are clear, as its results are tangible, but the purpose of engaging in "classical mathematics" without any practical applications is a debatable topic.

This question was important even before AI, but now much depends on the answer. For example, if AI were to find a proof for the Riemann hypothesis and verify it with millions of lines in Lean, but no human could comprehend it, what would that event signify for mathematics?

There are no definitive answers yet, but there are proposals for "which direction to move." For instance, Terence Tao stated that it's time for mathematics to rethink itself, outlined several stages in the lifecycle of proofs, and emphasized that humans have always prioritized the first stage ("obtain a proof"). Now is the time to focus on others: from "verify" to, as he puts it, "canonicalize."

Now let's look at all this in detail:

Timeline of Events

To grasp the pace of progress, it's helpful to see how things have evolved over time. Here are just a few notable events:

  • 2022: ChatGPT appears, and people begin actively discussing LLM capabilities. However, even for simple tasks like "Vanya had 5 apples," the model might confidently give incorrect answers. Thus, it's generally believed that this "stochastic parrot" has no place in mathematics.

  • 2024: OpenAI introduces its first reasoning model, o1-preview, and later DeepSeek R1 popularizes this approach. These models are trained using reinforcement learning, significantly improving performance on mathematical benchmarks.

  • 2025: Following the International Mathematical Olympiad, both OpenAI and DeepMind announce that their models have performed at a gold medal standard. However, Olympiad results are not yet scientific breakthroughs, and methodological questions arise. So, while progress is evident, mathematicians remain very cautious in their assessments.

  • October 2025: An OpenAI researcher claims their model has found proofs for some Erdős problems. However, it turns out these proofs were already known to humanity, and the model merely rediscovered existing ones, rather than developing its own. Many express skepticism, echoing sentiments like "AI can only plagiarize."

  • Early 2026: Now, the website erdosproblems.com actually features the first proofs of open problems obtained with the help of AI. Initially, these involve lesser-known problems that people hadn't spent much time on. The results are real but minor.

  • February 2026: The First Proof project is launched to test the capabilities of models on new mathematical problems. Created within the academic world and independent of commercial companies like OpenAI, it gains credibility among mathematicians. In February, it released its first set of problems for testing, and AI models solved some of them. Two more sets followed later.

  • February 28, 2026: Donald Knuth, exclaiming "Shock! Shock!", announces that Opus 4.6 assisted him in working on a new volume of "The Art of Programming" by finding a solution to a problem he was interested in. Knuth writes that he will have to "reconsider his views on generative AI."

  • May 20, 2026: OpenAI announces that its new model has disproven the Erdős distinct distances conjecture. Many mathematicians had previously worked on this conjecture unsuccessfully. Therefore, mathematicians agree that this is a truly significant result (even prompting the formulation "if you are a mathematician, sit down now").

  • June 2, 2026: The collective Leiden Declaration is published. In it, mathematicians state that AI can significantly impact the future of the field, evoking a range of emotions from enthusiasm to anxiety among specialists. They propose several measures for mathematicians, organizations, and governments to ensure AI's use is more beneficial to the field.

  • July 20, 2026: An Anthropic employee tweets that while watching the World Cup final, the Fable model, at his request, disproved the well-known Jacobian conjecture. He attaches the found counterexample in the tweet. Now, significant proofs are not solely attributed to OpenAI models.

  • August 1, 2026: OpenAI presents ten proofs found by its Astra model, which was not publicly available at the time. Mathematicians note that if these proofs had been found by humans, many would have deserved publication in leading journals. If all of them were by a single person, it would have meant a stellar career.

  • September 4, 2026: Anthropic announces the formalization of the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem in Lean. The proof itself has long been known, but this is the most extensive auto-formalization project: an AI model wrote millions of lines in Lean.

  • September 8, 2026: OpenAI announces that with its new model, it has achieved a result for the Navier-Stokes equations sufficient for the status of a "solved Millennium Prize Problem" by the Clay Mathematics Institute. If officially confirmed, this would be the first solution to a "Millennium Prize Problem" in 20 years.

  • September 10, 2026: OpenAI states that it is close to solving another "Millennium Prize Problem" and will share the result soon.

The progress is evident. But many wonder: what exactly are AIs getting better at? If they can handle a range of problems but not all, how does this subset differ from others? How does it compare to human abilities?

For example, Fields Medalist Timothy Gowers wrote a post in August titled "What sort of maths are LLMs good at?" Let's try to summarize the points from many similar posts.

Strengths and Weaknesses of LLMs

Many people want a simple answer: "So, have LLMs become more capable than mathematicians?" But the question isn't a simple yes/no: mathematics involves very diverse activities, and AI has both advantages and disadvantages. Looking at what is mentioned as both, we can find the following:

Strengths

Breadth of Knowledge. Mathematics is vast, and no single person knows it "entirely." Typically, mathematicians work "in depth," specializing in a particular area and striving to advance as far as possible. However, this can be a hindrance when a proof requires combining approaches from different fields. Humanity might already possess the necessary knowledge, but it hasn't yet "converged in one mind." A model trained on all data simultaneously, however, can spot non-obvious connections. It works "in breadth."

Less Bias. When AI disproved the Erdős distinct distances conjecture, some mathematicians suggested that humanity had been overly influenced by authorities. Erdős himself believed the conjecture was true, so efforts were primarily focused on proving it, with little resource spent on disproving it—hence the lack of success. For a machine, Erdős is not such an authority; it's willing to spend time disproving it, which proved helpful. This raises the question: in what other problems have we failed to achieve results simply because we trusted established opinions too much? How many myths will LLMs help to debunk?

Speed of Operation. A fast machine can "try applying all known approaches sequentially," which would take a human too much time. Does this mean that boring brute force and powerful hardware are winning? Not entirely: a couple of years ago, GPUs were already fast, but LLMs were nowhere near their current performance. The quality of the approaches used has increased, and without that, nothing would have been achieved. But quantity also matters. Just as in chess in the 90s, where improvements in both algorithms and hardware contributed to victories over humans.

Tirelessness. This is similar to the previous point but worth mentioning separately. If a human makes many attempts without success, they might become demotivated and switch to something else, thinking "my whole life will pass like this." A machine, however, can always be told to "try again." An amusing fact: in some cases, new proofs were found after prompts like "believe in yourself, don't give up" were used following initial failures.

Growth Potential in Verifiable Domains. Reinforcement learning works well when a clear "you succeed or you fail" signal can be given to the machine. This is not suitable for tasks like "compose a poetic masterpiece" (people disagree on what constitutes a masterpiece). Mathematics, however, is much more suitable, allowing for examples to be processed in batches, including formally verified ones. It's also convenient because it can occur "entirely within the computer." Other sciences are more complex: as soon as experiments "in the real world" are required, LLMs can no longer accelerate the process with rapid computations.

Weaknesses

Tendency Towards Counterexamples. When the Erdős conjecture was disproven, AI's success was aided not only by people blindly trusting authority but also by the fact that disproving is a less impressive result. Counterexamples are now being found one after another, but proving truth is not yet as active. Therefore, while some are excited by the news, others wonder: will AI disprove many false hypotheses, but what about true ones—will we be able to prove them?

Less Depth. As mentioned, AI tends to work "in breadth rather than depth." Consequently, its proofs impress many mathematicians ("I struggled with this problem myself once and couldn't solve it"), but they are usually not seen as "brilliant insights." More often, the reaction is like, "Great, but we could have figured that out ourselves; the approaches used are known." Proofs in mathematics are valuable not only in themselves; the search for them can lead to new theories and approaches that advance science as a whole. And such breakthroughs from AI are not yet visible. Will they emerge later? With the current growth rate, many expect so, but no one knows for sure.

Publication Quality. Finding a proof is not enough; for it to benefit mathematics as a whole, it must be correctly described for others. AI is currently weaker in this regard; for example, it may pay insufficient attention to complex transitions. It also often "forgets" to cite previous works, as if it proved everything from scratch without relying on prior results. This violates academic etiquette, requiring humans to re-verify such outputs.

Errors and Failures. Headlines about successes are impressive, but for a balanced view, one must remember the numerous unsuccessful attempts that don't make the news, where LLMs burn tokens fruitlessly or hallucinate entirely. Several Erdős problems have been addressed, but many more remain open. This doesn't diminish the achievements but simply reminds us that there's a long way to go.

Cost. The models' achievements are impressive, but sometimes enormous sums are spent on prompting. In the case of the Navier-Stokes equations, the cost of obtaining the proof is estimated in the millions of dollars, and that was a successful case. How much more did OpenAI spend unsuccessfully? Therefore, mathematicians sometimes joke: "If that much money had been invested in proofs before, we would have solved everything ourselves long ago." However, the AI industry is optimizing significantly over the years: if training the GPT-2 model cost tens of thousands of dollars in 2019, the cost of training a similar model today is estimated at $48. Thus, the cost of mathematical proofs might also be a temporary issue.

Current LLM Style

Due to these advantages and disadvantages, a unique "LLM approach" to proofs has emerged, differing from the traditional "human" approach.

The "breadth versus depth" distinction affects how tasks are logically presented to the machine. A human mathematician might set a single important goal, gradually delve deeper, spend years, and in a successful case, achieve the result. An AI model, however, can tackle multiple tasks within a day, but if it can't solve a particular problem due to its capabilities, it's unlikely to succeed even over a year.

Therefore, LLMs are often approached "in reverse": instead of leaving a model with one task for a year, they go through a whole list. Some problems will be solved, others won't; the solved ones are celebrated, and the unsolved ones are left for the next generation of models to try again. This year, among those applying AI to Erdős problems, each new GPT version release triggered a wave of excitement: "Oh, now we've managed to solve a few more."

A metaphor used is: the works of the best mathematicians are like mountain peaks with valleys in between. LLM proofs, on the other hand, are like rising water filling the valleys. They haven't yet surpassed the record heights; the peaks remain. However, they are capturing everything below a certain level, much of which humans might not have had the resources to address.

Furthermore, since LLMs are currently better at finding counterexamples, in some cases, people immediately consider "which known hypotheses are highly likely to be false," and then task the models with finding them.

The rise in popularity of the Lean project is also associated with LLM proofs, and this deserves separate consideration:

Lean and Formal Verification

The ideas of formal verification and "automatic proofs" have existed for a long time—for example, the Isabelle project is 40 years old. However, they were niche until recently, becoming more relevant in the changed world. Lean is now frequently mentioned. This project consists of several components: a programming language of the same name, a theorem-proving tool for that language, and the collective library Mathlib (mathematicians don't prove every step "from scratch" but rely on existing results, and Mathlib allows importing them).

If a hypothesis is formalized in Lean, its proof in Lean can be automatically verified. The language was partly inspired by Haskell, for which there are jokes like "if the project compiles, it works." But here, it's no joke: the goal is indeed to ensure that properties important to humans are reliably verified by automation.

The project began in 2013, and its creator, Leonardo de Moura, initially aimed to guarantee the absence of certain software bugs, not just test them. He wasn't thinking about AI at the time.

Things quickly deviated from the plan: the primary users became mathematicians interested in verifiable proofs, not programmers. However, their community was small at first. A proof in Lean requires many more lines than standard mathematical notation, and few were willing to engage with the unfamiliar tool when "it can be checked mentally."

Now, the situation has changed. LLMs have emerged as a powerful tool capable of finding complex proofs without human intervention, but also prone to hallucination. As new proofs emerge in a constant stream, manually verifying each one, wasting time on unsuccessful attempts, becomes increasingly difficult. This makes the question of automatic verification much more relevant: what if proofs are left to the machine to find, and humans are only alerted when a confirmed success occurs?

Currently, when a new significant proof is presented, it's often accompanied by a Lean version. When OpenAI published their results for Navier-Stokes, they prominently featured a link to the corresponding repository.

Lean is not a panacea either. Firstly, bugs have been found within Lean itself, allowing for the "proof" of erroneous statements. A conscientious mathematician is unlikely to encounter such bugs accidentally; they become relevant only when one tries to "outsmart the system." But AI models are prone to "reward hacking" behavior—could they exploit such vulnerabilities? Therefore, when the Navier-Stokes proof was published, formal verification specialist Talia Ringer raised the question: when the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem was previously formalized, we already knew "it's correct." How can we exclude the possibility of a bug here? There's the Lean4Lean project for "checking the checker," but the situation is not yet resolved.

Secondly, Lean can automatically verify a proof, but someone must first formalize the hypothesis itself. It's possible to make a mistake and formalize nonsense that doesn't correspond to the original idea, in which case the proof will only be verified against this nonsense. Does this mean humans still have to do the tedious work? Partially: auto-formalization helps speed up the process, but it doesn't provide complete guarantees.

Thirdly, even when there are no errors, formalization can be done in different ways. Recently, Anthropic used AI to formalize an important old proof of Fermat's Last Theorem, which took only 11 days. Previously, mathematician Kevin Buzzard was awarded a five-year, million-pound grant for the manual completion of such a task. This sounds triumphant, and Buzzard himself admitted he was beaten. But there's a catch: the AI model produced Lean code spanning 13 million lines. Yes, this proof is automatically verifiable, but many questioned whether it could be done much more concisely, making the code more accessible and illustrative for live mathematicians. Wouldn't Buzzard's "expensive" version be far more useful?

However, the situation with Lean is already better than it would have been without it. When OpenAI presented 10 proofs at once, a debatable point was found in one of them, casting doubt on the entire conclusion. But thanks to the Lean version, mathematicians were quickly able to confirm that the result still held. In the current race, where proofs are often presented without traditional peer review (to avoid being overtaken), this is particularly valuable.

What is Happening Now?

In August, it felt like a casino: people frantically started feeding all open problems to AI, hoping to be the first to get proofs. But over time, the novelty effect will wear off, and finding an interesting, unexplored problem will become difficult, so this is unlikely to last forever.

Some mathematicians are now returning to problems they once struggled with: "Wow, AI found a result, and it's different from what I expected back then." Others say that despite all of AI's successes, in their specific field, it can still prove very little. Sometimes they see benefits, but different ones: for example, "searching existing literature has become more convenient with AI," "the model found an error in my publication," "I received a partial result."

Not only mathematicians are trying to participate in the current wave. For example, Bun JS runtime creator Jared Sumner asked an AI model to prove the Riemann hypothesis, which it failed to do, but unexpectedly achieved some results on a related conjecture.

Jared understood that this was beyond his expertise and, before boasting about the result, showed it to more knowledgeable people. However, not everyone approaches it so responsibly, leading to the concept of "mathematical slop." Some post erroneous "proofs" without understanding, merely cluttering the internet with incorrect data. Others publish clickbait claims like "AI has already solved all of mathematics." The website erdosproblems.com, which collects solutions to Erdős problems, has been flooded with low-quality submissions, making life difficult for its creator, Thomas Bloom.

As a result, Bloom wrote a thread on how to "use AI wisely." He refers to two long-time contributors and lists what they do correctly: they carefully read the proof themselves before submitting it to the site, clean up the text, specify "what exactly was done by AI and what by humans," and avoid cheap sensationalism. In his opinion, AI helps mathematics in this case, but without it, it can be harmful. Another mathematician, David Pfau, writes that it should become standard practice to attach full dialogues with LLMs that led to any new proof.

Significant criticism has also been leveled at the approaches of major AI companies. Mathematician Levant Alpöge from Anthropic, upon receiving new proofs, sometimes posted posts consisting solely of plus and minus signs without any explanation, or published LLM-generated accompanying text exceeding 100 pages. Some mathematicians complain: "These are cheap tricks; not providing a well-developed text with the proof is disrespectful to the field."

Even more controversy surrounded the circumstances under which OpenAI published their solution to a "Millennium Prize Problem." Mathematician Tristan Buckmaster stated that he and Levant Alpöge had worked on the same problem for almost a year using various LLMs, and just as success seemed imminent, OpenAI learned of it and decided to rush ahead.

OpenAI has immense resources, and for this problem, they launched 10,000 AI agents simultaneously, essentially "crushing it with hardware." Furthermore, during the training of their new model, they could hypothetically have used, among other things, Tristan's own prompts for the older model. It's unknown if this actually happened, but the possibility itself alarms many: could they have trained on his work to ultimately surpass him and claim all the glory?

This caused considerable anger within the mathematical community. Deliberately preempting someone who has been working on a task important to them for a year is common in the hyper-competitive Silicon Valley world, but not accepted in academia. Terence Tao wrote that solving a problem isn't always a "good event": truly interesting problems are rare, working on them helps mathematicians understand a great deal, and what happens if AI takes all of them? Many statements emerged along the lines of "corporations don't care about mathematics; they just use it for marketing, even if they harm it in the process."

However, some of the actions causing outrage might be temporary. Mathematician Alex Kontorovich predicts that AI companies will eventually stop publishing new proofs themselves because it will cease to be significant for marketing. His logic: previously, companies boasted that their models were succeeding in Olympiad problems, but when this became commonplace and stopped surprising people, the hype in that direction subsided. He expects the same here.

Meanwhile, LLMs themselves will, of course, remain available and capable, allowing mathematicians to utilize their capabilities. But they can do so according to their own judgment, without violating the academic principles they deem important.

What's Next for Mathematics?

This is where the most complex and interesting part begins. We've understood the current situation and near-term prospects, but what awaits mathematics as a whole? And how can it be pursued rationally in a world with powerful AI?

The question is complex for two reasons. One is that we don't know the future capabilities of AI models. Success in proofs is noticeable, but can AI, in place of solved problems, find new ones that would be highly interesting to mathematicians? Or achieve a conceptual breakthrough? Or even "create new mathematics"?

Grant Sanderson (creator of the YouTube channel 3Blue1Brown) noted in an interview that things are becoming more difficult with such matters because a problem is either proven or not, while the quality of "insights" is much more subjective. But he offered his criterion: "if this happens, we'll notice not by benchmark numbers, but by a shift in the tone of conversations among mathematicians."

The second reason touches upon much more fundamental foundations. What exactly is mathematics? Why is it needed? Why do people allocate resources to it? Which components of mathematics are more important to people and deserve these resources more?

Polarized opinions are found online. Outsiders sometimes rejoice: "Oh, a golden age is dawning, machines will prove absolutely everything!" But some mathematicians ask: "What if, due to AI's success, academia fails to train the next generation of mathematicians, leading humanity to no longer understand even old proofs? That would be a catastrophe, not a golden age, and we need to avoid it."

To discuss such matters, we must start from the beginning: why are these proofs important to us at all? There are applied aspects that directly impact people's lives, but most open hypotheses don't have that situation. Suppose someone proves an important hypothesis; what exactly will that give us?

Many see the significance of complex hypotheses in the following. Their solutions often require more than existing approaches. Therefore, while people struggle with them, conceptual breakthroughs occur, new theories emerge, and ultimately, humanity learns more about the world as a whole. And the paths found can later be used to solve other problems. That is, the Riemann hypothesis is important not just in itself, but as a potential key to many other things.

But this definition seems somewhat recursive. We need to solve complex problems to then... solve more problems? One can still ask "why all this?"

Partly, this reflects the general complexity of fundamental science. Often, while pursuing research out of curiosity, a scientist doesn't know what practical applications might arise. Yet, many things that improve our lives today originated from the work of such "curious people" from the past. When Évariste Galois worked in the 19th century, he had no idea that his work would be useful for modern cryptography in the 20th century.

But beyond that, many mathematicians believe that the main goal of fundamental mathematics is understanding itself. By proving a famous hypothesis, humanity might not apply it "practically," but if it has gained a little more understanding of the world, that in itself is valuable.

This spring, mathematician David Bessis's post "The Fall of Theorem Economy" caused a stir. He argues that mathematics has long gone down the wrong path by focusing on proofs as a measure of success. People like G.H. Hardy convinced everyone that discovering new knowledge is paramount, while, for example, explaining the results of others and teaching them is "a task for second-rate minds."

According to Bessis, William Thurston, who disagreed with this, was more correct: the goal of mathematics is understanding, and it is collective, so all activity in this direction, including teaching others, is important. Theorems themselves are worthless; we value them only because they are important to people.

This debate predates modern AI, but AI is making it relevant in new ways. If, like Hardy, one prioritizes new proofs at all costs, then one can simply rejoice in them now. But it becomes more complex when discussing the community and its collective understanding, as Thurston, Bessis, and other prominent mathematicians like Peter Scholze have done.

With AI, collective understanding can be enriched with new knowledge, but it can also be destroyed if the value of understanding is not recognized and only the number of proofs is pursued. This requires not only capable technology but also a rational human approach. What exactly should that approach look like?

A Constructive Approach

While some mathematicians' voices sound alarmed, others strive to constructively solve the problem of interacting with AI in a way that preserves what is important to humans in mathematics and ultimately improves it.

The most prominent voice is Terence Tao: a Fields Medalist who began addressing the issue of "AI in mathematics" long before the current hype. Even before the news about Navier-Stokes, he delivered a major keynote speech, "Mathematics in the Age of AI" (text format, video). Here's a summary of the main points.

According to Tao, mathematics is currently undergoing a significant "rethinking of itself," similar to what happened a century ago. At that time, it had to question many established notions and admit that the previous chaotic approach was flawed. But in the end, it emerged from the crisis stronger than before. And now, Tao says, the same could happen.

He then proposes shifting focus from proofs to rethinking the value of the entire process and unraveling the chain: here's a proof, but what's important to us in relation to it? His resulting chain is as follows:

  • Stage One: Finding a Proof. We used to live in a world where proofs were scarce, so we valued them. But now we are entering an era of "proof abundance," where proofs will flow in such a stream that we'll only have time to comprehend them. Consequently, each proof becomes less valuable, shifting the emphasis to the subsequent stages.

  • Stage Two: Verifying the Proof. The peer review process already struggled to keep up with the workload, and now it's becoming a particular bottleneck. However, AI will increasingly assist here through formal verification. The complex question is whether humans will understand the vast formal constructions generated by AI. The first proofs of Erdős problems have already emerged, which humans have not yet deciphered.

  • Stage Three: Describing the Proof. A good publication was important even before AI, allowing other mathematicians to truly understand a complex proof and the author's path to it. AI is currently weak in this regard: for example, an LLM might describe trivial parts of a proof in detail but skim over the key, most complex sections where human questions arise. AI text quality will likely improve, but by how much compared to human writing remains unclear.

  • Stage Four: Accepting the Proof. Some results astound mathematicians and have far-reaching implications later, while others are largely forgotten. This distinction depends on two sides: the author (what proof and how it was described?) and the community, which "accepts" or "rejects" the result. This is a collective and unhurried process. It's less discussed, but it's also important for the development of mathematics. According to Tao, AI can be used here as a "filter" (to discard clearly insignificant results), but it cannot fully replace the intuition of live mathematicians. Only humans can decide what is important to humans.

  • Stage Five: "Canonicalizing" Proofs. The most important proofs, after being accepted by the community, find their way into textbooks. In this process, they are comprehended and integrated into the existing context. This is also crucial work that is rarely discussed. (He deliberately uses the term "canonicalization" rather than "canonization" to avoid religious connotations).

According to Tao, the most important stage of all is not the first, but the last. It defines the "mathematical canon" known to humanity. Practical applications of results in various fields are based on this canon. For example, current LLMs are trained on this canon. They owe their current successes to it.

And while Tao sees this part as the most important, he considers it least suitable for AI.

Thus, Tao presents an optimistic outlook. No matter how many hypotheses AI proves tomorrow, it will only address the first of five stages, leaving live mathematicians with plenty of work on the others. In this scenario, human understanding will remain relevant. The key is for humanity to recognize this list of tasks, rather than concluding, "Machines have already done everything, there's nothing left for humans to do."
