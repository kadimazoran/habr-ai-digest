I took two paid Telegram channels with signals, read their posts for a month (August 2026) using an LLM, and backtested each signal on minute candles, including commissions and slippage. The first channel yielded +10.3% for the month, the second -18.1%. Both figures are without leverage. The difference is explained by the post format, and it's visible even before starting the backtest.

Why All This?

Imagine a trader who plans to pay for signals from Telegram and follow them. What kind of insurance do they need?

A stop-loss by itself doesn't protect. You can set a stop that triggers 100% of the time and lose your deposit while formally adhering to risk management. Whether the author's rules work or not can only be shown by running through historical data: real posts, minute candles, commissions, slippage. The output is a verifiable number.

This is what I set out to do. I chose channels with opposite formats:

  • Channel A publishes a complete trading plan in each post.

  • Channel B relies on personal discipline: "I take 1% of the deposit," "I accept losses and move on.".

LLM Parses Posts, Code Makes Decisions

The main decision in the project: the model does not make trading decisions. It was assigned a task that LLMs handle well – reading unstructured data.

A post like "Open LONG in the range $78600 – $79400, targets..., stop $77400" is text with typos, emojis, and promotional inserts. Sometimes the signal isn't text but a screenshot of a terminal with the word "Short" in the image. The model's job is to turn this into a typed JSON. The code should decide whether to enter a position and where to cut losses.
