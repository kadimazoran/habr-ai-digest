Ankyra: Implementing Neuro-Symbolic Reasoning

This article discusses the development of Ankyra, a system that combines the strengths of large language models (LLMs) with symbolic solvers to achieve more reliable and verifiable reasoning. It addresses the issue of LLM hallucinations by introducing a deterministic symbolic solver that rigorously checks the facts and inferences generated by the LLM. The article details the neuro-symbolic concept, the formal description of the algorithm, the different logics supported, and presents testing results on various benchmarks, comparing Ankyra's performance against other neuro-symbolic approaches like Logic-LM and LINC. The author highlights the potential applications of such systems in fields where accuracy and verifiability are crucial, such as law, medicine, and technical documentation.

Introduction

Currently, there are many reports about solving various problems in mathematics, proving famous theorems, generalizing existing results to a broader domain, and so on. The results are truly impressive. On the other hand, each of us has encountered, to some extent, hallucinations in language models, where the model invents non-existent facts, and gullible users take them at face value. The reason for such model behavior lies in the probabilistic nature of its reasoning algorithm. Tokens (words) in the model are represented by real-valued vectors (dynamic embeddings), and the self-attention mechanism is implemented in such a way that it captures analogy-based reasoning (associations) very well but can easily make mistakes when such analogies do not exist. In this case, the model begins to construct false analogies and fantasize.

Researchers have long recognized this fact, and significant work is being done to improve the accuracy of model reasoning. In this article, I want to talk about work in the direction of so-called neuro-symbolic reasoning, where the model's reasoning is not done in isolation but in tandem with a so-called symbolic solver – implemented based on a deterministic algorithm. This solver uses the apparatus of formal logic, well-developed over the past few decades.

Concept

In neuro-symbolic reasoning, we use a neural component – a language model – and a symbolic solver based on formal logic. "Symbolic" here refers to the fact that we use symbols as objects of reasoning (like in the formulas we all wrote in school), as opposed to the vectors used by the language model's neural network.

We assume in advance that, due to its probabilistic nature, a language model cannot be reliable by definition, but a symbolic solver is reliable simply by virtue of being verifiable based on its output. This is exactly the approach used in mathematics, where the only basis for a proposition being true is its derivation from known statements.

From this, the roles of these two components in reasoning become immediately clear:

  1. The language model can hypothesize, supplement with facts based on its vast erudition and knowledge.

  2. The symbolic solver verifies the correctness of these facts by formally matching the new fact with the current ones.

Here and below, we will call a theory the set of certain facts: the objects of our reasoning, the relationships between objects, as well as rules of the form – if something is true, then this is also true. Some facts are derived from the theory by formal rules by the symbolic solver, others are added by the model, and all this needs to be classified somehow, otherwise, we will get confused. This classification may seem unclear at first glance, but it's actually simple. Let's look at examples.

Input text: “It is raining. If it is raining, the ground is wet. Is the ground wet?”

At the very beginning, we ask the model to extract facts from the text. The model extracts the following:

  • fact raining() - "it is raining"

  • rule raining() => is_wet(ground) - "if it is raining, then the ground is wet"

Both facts are based on the texts, so we classify them as cited, meaning they become axioms of the theory for this sentence upon extraction. The symbolic solver supplements the theory with the fact is_wet(ground) because raining() is already an axiom in the theory, along with the rule raining() => is_wet(ground). is_wet(ground) is classified as derivable. Thus, the text "Is the ground wet?", which in the language of formal theory is actually just a check of whether the fact is_wet(ground) exists in the theory, receives the answer Yes with the status proven.

Another text: “The car has an engine, four wheels, 150 horsepower, and four doors. What kind of car is it?”

The model extracts the facts:

  • x - car

  • has_engine(x) - "has an engine"

  • wheel_count(x, 4) - "four wheels"

  • power(x, 150) - "150 horsepower"

  • door_count(x, 4) - "four doors"

All facts are confirmed by quotes from the text, so they become axioms. The question is the predicate is_a(x, ?c), where ?c is a variable. The theory does not contain car types or anything similar, so we cannot answer the question yet.

We feed the formed theory into the language model and ask it to supplement it with facts. The model proposes a rule: has_engine(?x) ∧ wheel_count(?x,4) ∧ power(?x,150) ∧ door_count(?x,4) ⇒ is_a(?x, car). This is an example of a so-called Horn clause, where the left side consists of predicates with logical AND, and the right side has only one predicate. In other words, the model forms a question in the form of a rule: if this car has an engine, four wheels, 150 horsepower, and four doors, what kind of car is it? There is no quote for this rule in the text, so it is classified as a hypothesis, say H1.

The solver derives the predicate is_a(x, car) from the theory's facts and this rule. However, this is too general an answer and provides no new information; the fact that it is a car is already in the statement. Therefore, we turn to the model again for an answer and get a new rule: is_a(?x, car) ⇒ is_a(?x, motor_vehicle). This is a hierarchy rule, has no quote in the text, is classified as derivable, and receives the status H2. Now the symbolic solver can derive the fact is_a(x, motor_vehicle) with the status proven_under(H1, H2).

Formal Description of Operation

A careful reader has surely noticed that in the examples above, the model was used for two different purposes:

  1. Building a theory from text: extracting objects, predicates, and rules, as well as questions in the form of predicates with variables.

  2. Supplementing the theory with new facts.

The symbolic solver helps develop the theory, formally deriving new facts from existing ones based on rules, i.e., building the so-called closure of the theory, but it cannot go further; this is a known limitation of formal logic. The language model acts in two roles here:

  1. A syntactic parser, extracting elements of formal theory from the text.

  2. A theory supplementer with new facts that the symbolic solver cannot derive in principle, but the model knows due to its erudition.

Hence, the proposed neuro-symbolic reasoning algorithm:

  1. The model builds a base theory from the input text.

  2. The symbolic solver builds a formal derivation based on the theory's rules – it "closes" it. If an answer is obtained, it is returned; otherwise, proceed to step 3.

  3. If the limit of model calls is exceeded, stop the process; the answer is not found. Otherwise, call the model to obtain new facts and inference rules.

  4. Proceed to step 2 to supplement the theory.

Each execution of steps 2-3 is called a wave. The author tested the algorithm on several popular collections, and typically, no more than seven waves were needed to solve the task. I think the reader familiar with the topic will recognize the ReAct loop in this algorithm. Yes, it's roughly like that, only the symbolic solver acts as the tool, which not only "executes the tool" but also corrects the model, preventing it from making mistakes or hallucinating, by correcting it with formal inference, provided that the hypotheses put forward by the model are true. I like the image of the symbolic solver as a skeleton for the muscles, which symbolize the language model.

Logics

Formal logics also have their structures and varieties. The simplest first-order logic is based on the presence of only Horn clauses: if given facts are true together, then this one is also true. Above in the example, we had a Horn clause: has_engine(?x) ∧ wheel_count(?x,4) ∧ power(?x,150) ∧ door_count(?x,4) ⇒ is_a(?x, car). Horn clauses are good because the theory on them is monotonically supplemented (grows) – this allows solving any problem formulated in the language of such logic. We will denote such logic as L0.

I will not go into a detailed, boring explanation of the list of supported logics; it is not very understandable to a reader not immersed in the context and seems superfluous here. I will say briefly: there are logics with existential and universal quantifiers, resolution-based reasoning, as in the Prolog language, as well as support for arithmetic and systems of equations. I introduced them specifically to try to connect symbolic solvers not only with logic but also with other classes of problems. Details can be found in the project repository (the link will be later, at the end of the article).

Here, it is probably worth noting that the logic is selected automatically based on the type of theory that the model extracts from the input text. The symbolic solver selects and applies it automatically.

Testing

I used quite a few synthetic examples to test the symbolic solvers and tune the prompts. However, I want to test the algorithm on real cases and compare it with other approaches. For this, known benchmarks are needed. I chose the following:

ProofWriter

A synthetic benchmark from AI2. The collection contains short texts, like "Anne is nice. All nice people are cold. Is Anne cold?". Each example is given a label True / False / Unknown. This is precisely L0 logic with Horn clauses.

ProntoQA

This is also synthetic, but with ontologies: classes, individuals, etc. Questions like "does an individual belong to a certain class?". For us, this is the predicate is_a and the addition of the negation operator, which is not present in L0. The solver is named L1.

ProntoQA-OOD

This is a more complex version of ProntoQA, which includes disjunctions, nested conjunctions, negations, proofs by cases, and proof by contradiction. This is a slightly different solver, which I named L2.

FOLIO

A serious benchmark, not synthetic, but manually written by experts. Each example is a complete logical story in natural language. It is accompanied by premises and conclusions, as well as True / False / Unknown labels. Like almost everything written by humans, the benchmark is quite noisy and looks complicated. Therefore, the model does not structure the texts of this collection very well; I got the worst results on it precisely because of this property.

AR-LSAT

These are so-called LSAT "logic games": arranging people at a table, placing plays on a disk, grouping objects – with constraints on order, adjacency, "all different", etc. This requires a completely different solver, as I mentioned above – not deductive, so it was implemented separately. Unlike the previous ones, where everything was implemented in a formal logic solver with flags for one property or another.

GSM8K

This is a collection of school math word problems, where the answer should be a number. Here, the solver is ordinary arithmetic. However, given that school problems also involve systems of equations with variables, all this was implemented by a solver based on systems of linear equations.

Why is this needed

As I said at the very beginning, it is best to use such neuro-symbolic reasoning where it is truly necessary – where verification is important, where if a result is obtained, it can be trusted. This is important for the legal field, medicine, technical documentation, education (where would we be without it?), and many other similar areas where the cost of error is high.

Another important and interesting fact about such systems: they do not lie. A symbolic solver can say no, unlike a language model, and this "no" is justified and verifiable.

The project code is on Github: https://github.com/merfill/ankyra. The project is open-source, written in Python using the LangChain and LanGraph libraries.
