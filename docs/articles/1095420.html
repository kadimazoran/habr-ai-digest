CPU Core Count Tells You Nothing: How I Calculated Whether a Self-Hosted ASR Server is Needed Instead of Deepgram

I needed live subtitles for one-on-one Russian calls. The easiest way to solve this is through a cloud API: literally with one call. This is a convenient option if you don't have many calls and their content can be sent to an external service.

In my case, it's not so straightforward: there can be many hours of communication, and the conversations may contain sensitive data that I wouldn't want to send to the cloud. Therefore, I set up a testbed and compared real-time cloud speech recognition with open-source models on a regular server, using only the CPU, without a graphics card.

The main result turned out to be about the hardware, with the models being secondary. One chunk of an acoustic model on a 2013 Xeon core without AVX2 costs 2.5 times more than on a modern Xeon with AVX2, and under full load, the difference reaches up to five times. In terms of tracks, this is 32 versus 5, but this figure should be read with a caveat about the methodology, which I'll explain below. Calculating the cost-effectiveness of your own ASR based on the core count in a hosting provider's tariff is pointless.

What Was Needed

Currently, I am testing and using cloud-based Deepgram, and the testbed is needed to determine if I can switch away from it. The call happens in a browser, each participant has their own microphone, so two separate tracks come to the server. This greatly simplifies things: diarization is not needed, and the speaker's role is known by the track. The requirements for recognition are simple:

  • the phrase should appear no later than 2 seconds after it's spoken (p95);

  • the cost per hour should be predictable;

  • audio should not leave Russia.

What the Authors Promise

The WER figures for different models were obtained on different datasets, so they cannot be directly compared, but the order of magnitude is useful.

What is WER?

WER (word error rate) indicates the proportion of words that the model recognized incorrectly. An error is considered a substitution, omission, or insertion of a word, and the sum of errors is divided by the number of words in the reference. WER of 10% means approximately every tenth word is recognized incorrectly. The lower, the better. Before calculation, punctuation and case are removed, and numbers are normalized. Figures obtained on different recording datasets cannot be compared with each other.

  • T-one: call-center 8.63, other telephony 6.20, Common Voice 5.32. In the same table, the authors' Whisper large-v3 on call-center gives 19.39.

  • GigaAM-v3 RNNT: average 8.3 across ten datasets, call-center 9.5; e2e variant with punctuation 11.2.

  • Vosk ru-0.42: openstt calls 36.0, youtube 19.5. Small model on Golos crowd 11.79.

  • Deepgram: no public WER figures for Russian. I couldn't find them; if you find them, please share in the comments.

Testbed

I ran the same code on two machines.

Machine A: Xeon with AVX2, 8 vCPUs, 15 GB RAM. This was the first measurement, focusing only on the acoustic model via onnxruntime, with batches of identical chunks and greedy decoding.

Machine B: Xeon E5-2680 v2 at 2.8 GHz, 2013, 6 vCPUs under KVM, 11.6 GB RAM. Vector extensions only include SSE4.2 and AVX, no AVX2 or FMA. Steal was around 1%, and neighbors on the host did not interfere. Here, the full pipeline was measured: acoustics, silence splitter, and decoder, with each track having its own audio offset, two decoders, and two operating modes.

Full disclosure: the methodologies for A and B are different. On A, I only measured acoustics with a batch of identical chunks, while on B, I ran the full pipeline. Therefore, only the cost of one chunk (table below, same code and model) is strictly comparable. The capacity in tracks is a rough comparison: on A, I didn't run the full pipeline, and the actual number there is likely lower than 32.

The methodology on machine B is as follows. Every 300 ms, all active tracks receive the next chunk. The time for acoustics, decoding, and their sum is measured, as well as the lag from the schedule: if a tick takes more than 300 ms, the next one starts later, and this lag accumulates. The machine "holds" N tracks if the p95 tick time is less than 300 ms and the lag by the end of the minute is less than a second. Each point is a 60-second measurement after a 6-second warm-up.

Two modes:

  • batch: one model call for all tracks, onnxruntime with three threads. This is how I initially planned to build the service, and it's what T-one's documentation recommends for Triton.

  • threads: each track in its own Python worker thread; onnxruntime uses one thread (intra_op_num_threads=1). Acoustics release the GIL, and parallelism is provided by a pool.

Audio for load: samples from the T-one package, call center, 8 kHz, looped.

The testbed code is open-source in the github and gitverse repositories. The most useful part, in my opinion, is not the benchmark but the probe acoustic.py: it only depends on onnxruntime, can be installed in a minute, and in another minute, it will tell you the cost of one chunk on your server. You'll understand why this is important later.

KenLM

T-one has two decoders: greedy and beam search with a 5.4 GB KenLM language model. I expected KenLM to consume a lot of CPU. But no, it didn't: decoding a phrase costs 5–11 ms per second of speech; for a 51-second recording, the entire decoding took 0.1 seconds. It just spikes at the end of each phrase. However, memory-wise: the process with KenLM occupies 5.5 GB compared to 0.6 GB with greedy. On the sample, the greedy and KenLM texts matched verbatim, but on a half-hour live dialogue, KenLM was better by 0.9 percentage points on the client track and 1.7 on the host track.

What Didn't Help

I tried two obvious optimizations, both failed.

Dynamic quantization to int8. It turned out that out of 158 matrix multiplications in the model, 157 operate in float16, while the onnxruntime quantizer only supports float32. There's nothing to quantize.

Converting the entire model to float32, since it's in fp16, and x86 without AVX512-FP16 lacks fast fp16 cores. RTF didn't change: 0.392 vs. 0.395, meaning onnxruntime was already calculating in fp32. However, the model loaded twice as fast (3 seconds instead of 9) and used half the memory: previously, the runtime converted fp16 weights on each load. For a service, the fp32 variant is still useful.

What the numbers reveal:

  • KenLM on live speech provides a 0.9–1.7 percentage point improvement over greedy on both tracks (no difference was observed on the sample).

  • The path through the service (resampling 48→8 kHz) is 0.4 percentage points worse than offline on the client track, and even slightly better on the host track (25.6 vs. 26.2). At this volume, it's noise: resampling costs almost nothing.

  • The high WER for the host is due to sound leakage between channels. T-one and GigaAM have 60–69 insertions for 539 words. The host is silent 89% of the time; the client's voice from the speakers reaches their microphone, and the models record it, while Deepgram in multichannel did not record this.

  • However, Deepgram is not flawless here either: at 00:02 and 00:10, T-one and GigaAM agreed against it, attributing client remarks to the host. So, the host's WER is inflated, and only listening to disputed segments will provide certainty.

  • Vosk ru-0.42 fell apart on wideband live speech: 326 omissions out of 3028 words for the client (it was 5.9% on the telephone sample).

  • Disputed segments for listening amounted to 91 on the host track and 561 on the client track.

Caveat about the ground truth, and why it doesn't change the conclusion. The ground truth here is the Deepgram nova-3 transcription, so its score is zero by design, and the scores for others show the distance to Deepgram, not WER in the strict sense. I calculated how much this might distort the picture. On the client track, there are 74 instances out of 3028 words where engines of different families agreed against Deepgram. If Deepgram made all 74 errors, its WER would become 2.4%, and T-one's would decrease by the same 2.4 percentage points: the gap would shrink from 10.6 to 5.7 percentage points, but not disappear. This shift also doesn't change the order of the engines. It could only seriously affect the GigaAM line: 8.1% "distance" could mean either a real lag or simply different errors in different places.

How the Service is Structured

The curve suggested the service's architecture. One process, one loaded model, each track has its own state, onnxruntime with one thread, tracks are parallelized by a pool of worker threads based on the number of cores.

The browser sends 48 kHz PCM in 20 ms frames; the service downsamples to 8 kHz using a streaming FIR filter with a cutoff of 3.6 kHz and segments it into 300 ms chunks. The contract is short: create a meeting (two tracks are reserved, 429 immediately on overload), two WebSockets with roles, events like {"type":"phrase","role":"host","text":"…","start_ms":…,"end_ms":…,"lag_ms":…}, closing with finalize and the tail of phrases. There are no partial hypotheses, and this must be explicitly stated in the contract: T-one delivers text as a phrase upon pause; a word-by-word stream cannot be expected from it.

Live check on machine B, separate run on a synthetic stream: engine load 4.2 s, phrases arrive with a lag of 990–1200 ms from the live edge, tick p95 229 ms with two tracks, 517 MB memory. On the live dialogue above, it was 272 ms and 1080–1200 ms. The requirement "p95 no more than 2 s" is met with a buffer. The track limit is set by the config based on the curve results; beyond the limit, a new meeting receives a rejection, and switching to the cloud or disabling subtitles is decided by the calling party. Logs contain neither text nor audio, only identifiers and timestamps.

Money

An hour of meeting in Deepgram nova-3 streaming, two tracks: about ₽49 at the promo price ($0.0048 per minute) and about ₽79 at the regular price ($0.0077), exchange rate 84.9. A nuance that's easy to miss: stereo with multichannel is billed per channel; ten minutes of recording means twenty minutes of billing.

The following calculation is conditional. I haven't measured what processor is behind the tariffs below, and I'm assuming they are comparable to machine A. A server for T-one: Timeweb 8 vCPU / 12 GB for ₽2,790 per month pays for itself with approximately 57 hours of meetings per month compared to the promo price and 36 hours compared to the regular price. Yandex Cloud 8 vCPU / 16 GB for ₽10,944: 224 and 139 hours. A redundant server doubles these thresholds.

And here's the main point again. These calculations assume that 8 vCPUs can handle up to 16 meetings. On a machine of class B, the same money yields two, meaning eight times less. Calculating cost-effectiveness only makes sense after running the probe on the specific tariff of a specific host. This takes a minute, but without it, you can be wrong by orders of magnitude.

What's not included in the calculation: service development and support, on-call duties, redundancy, and final text after the meeting, because T-one doesn't add punctuation.

Pitfalls

  • Processor generation is more important than the number of vCPUs. AVX without AVX2 and FMA: 2.5 times slower per core, 4–5 times slower with all onnxruntime threads, and 6–8 times lower in terms of the number of tracks a server can handle.

  • 6 vCPUs are not equal to six cores. Indicator: more onnxruntime threads perform worse, p95 increases at half load.

  • T-one weights in fp16. int8 cannot be applied, fp32 doesn't speed up, but it halves memory usage on load.

  • Batching tracks on a weak CPU is useless. A thread per track is simpler and handles more.

  • 8 kHz vs. wideband WebRTC. Resampling changes recognition; on the sample, it added one error. Quality should be measured on the production path.

  • No punctuation or capitalization. Phrases arrive after a pause with a delay of 1–1.2 seconds. The interface should not wait for word-by-word text.

  • KenLM barely loads the CPU but occupies 5.5 GB of memory.

  • WER between engines cannot be compared without normalizing numbers.

  • Noisy neighbors. On another virtual machine with the same E5-2680 v2, but with steal 22–31%, one chunk cost 218 ms instead of 109 ms on a clean machine. Before measuring, check the st column in vmstat.

  • Golos might have been used in T-one's training. Five correctly recognized phrases from it prove nothing.

  • T-one doesn't have a production server, only a demo on FastAPI. You have to write the server-side code yourself.

Conclusions

The cloud wins when the number of meeting hours is small, punctuation is needed, and you want quality without hassle. A self-hosted server wins when audio must not leave the country, there are many hours of calls, and you have a processor with AVX2, which you've verified with the probe, not just read in the tariff. T-one on CPU honestly provides phrases with a delay of about a second, but in terms of words, it's inferior to both the cloud and GigaAM; for final text after the meeting, a different model is needed.
