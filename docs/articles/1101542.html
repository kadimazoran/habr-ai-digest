The Core BI team is responsible for the main financial dashboard. Every morning starts roughly the same:

  1. Open the main financial report.

  2. Check if the data has been updated.

  3. Look at revenue dynamics and go through the breakdowns.

  4. If the figure has changed significantly, figure out where exactly and why. To do this, we involve metric analysts from different teams.

  5. Briefly explain the result to colleagues, and if something is wrong with the data, warn them when it will be ready.

Nothing supernatural, but these actions take at least half an hour every day. Not because each time a complex investigation or deep dive into details is required. It's just that between looking at a graph and answering the question "What happened?" there are several mandatory checks – they cannot be skipped.

We decided to automate this part of the work and created Big10 Pulse – a bot in the corporate messenger Mattermost. It monitors key business metrics daily. As soon as fresh data is ready, it sends a PDF report and an analytical note with a factor analysis of changes and found deviations.

It sounds like an ideal task for a large language model – as we like it: collect context from different sources and threads, and then explain the found anomaly. But we quickly realized that the main thing here is not what work can be entrusted to the model, but what cannot.

Why a graph alone is not enough

Big10 collects key financial indicators of Avito in one place. It's convenient to track the overall dynamics, but the graph itself only shows that the metric has changed. It doesn't explain why it happened and doesn't break down the change into specific factors.

Suppose the team sees a revenue drop in the morning. Before discussing the reasons, the analyst needs to check:

  • whether all data for the day has been loaded;

  • whether the growth or decline differs from normal fluctuations;

  • whether the base for comparison is chosen correctly;

  • which business direction contributed the most to the change;

  • which type of revenue within this direction affected the result.

The last two points are especially important. The largest percentage change doesn't always have the biggest impact on the overall metric. A small segment can grow or fall significantly but barely change the total. Therefore, we look not only at the dynamics of individual categories but also at their contribution to the overall deviation.

And only after that can a hypothesis be formulated. If you start with an explanation and leave the checks for later, it's easy to mistake incomplete loading for a business downturn, and a regular holiday effect for an incident. The graph will look quite convincing in this case – which is often the problem.

Therefore, we didn't create a universal chat with a report. The task was narrowed down to the first analytical breakdown:

  1. Check data readiness.

  2. Separate signal from noise.

  3. Break down the change into factors.

  4. Show the user where to look next.

How Big10 Pulse works and why not everything was given to AI

The user logs into the bot's admin panel. If they don't have access yet, the system prompts them to request it. After that, they can select the desired report, check active subscriptions, and specify breakdowns, targets, or features they want to track.

The bot saves the subscription in the database and waits for the sources to update. When the data is ready, it sends the user graphs, a link to the report with the necessary filters, and a short analytical note.

Before that, the bot performs several checks:

  1. Ensures all sources have updated.

  2. Compares the metric with saved slices from previous periods.

  3. Assesses the significance of the change and breaks down the total difference by business directions and revenue types.

  4. Based on the completed calculations, the text is formed, and a separate verification step determines whether it can be shown to the user.

As a result, instead of a message like "revenue decreased by N%", the user receives an initial orientation: how typical the change is, in which segment it occurred, and what figures confirm it.

The analysis doesn't end there. The bot accesses the detailed report on factors and checks what exactly within the category could have influenced the result. For example, a change in the number of CPC clicks, calls, or other product metrics.

Thus, the comment shows not only where the metric changed but also what might be causing this change.

First, we check data readiness

This is the most down-to-earth part of the system and, perhaps, the most important. We'd like to move straight to the model and analytical conclusions, but first, we have to find out if today's data is up-to-date.

If one of the sources is delayed, the overall indicator may look like a sharp drop. The breakdowns will also look convincing: one segment has fallen more than another, shares have been redistributed, and an ugly anomaly has appeared on the graph. However, analyzing it is pointless – we compared the full previous day with an incomplete current one.

Therefore, the bot doesn't look for reasons at first but checks the last update date. Until the data is ready, the calculation is not launched. After a successful check, the system sends the report once. The fact of sending is saved so that the user does not receive multiple identical messages.

The language model is not involved here. It can eloquently explain even a loading error, but in such a situation, we need not eloquent text, but the absence of text.

Not every change is an anomaly

Comparing today's value only with yesterday's is not enough. Business metrics have weekly, and sometimes monthly, seasonality.

Some indicators change predictably at the beginning and end of the month, others depend on the day of the week. The metric's behavior can also differ depending on the product specifics: for example, in the travel category, weekend growth might be expected, not anomalous.

Holidays are a separate case. Pre-holiday days, the holidays themselves, and the return to normal rhythm after them affect metrics differently. Therefore, a sharp jump after a long weekend doesn't necessarily mean something unusual has happened.

The bot compares the current value with the figure from last week and the seven-day average. The acceptable limits depend on the type of day and the historical behavior of the specific metric. A separate rule helps to filter out the expected rebound after holidays.

Threshold values are calculated by ordinary code. This is crucial for us: on identical data, the system must make the same decision. We don't ask the language model to look at the graph and determine if it looks like an anomaly.

At the same time, the rule triggering itself doesn't prove that a problem has occurred. It's just a signal: the change has gone beyond expected limits and needs to be analyzed further.

We look for the biggest contribution, not the biggest percentage

When the system finds a deviation, it's necessary to understand where exactly it occurred. To do this, the bot breaks down the total difference by business directions and revenue types.

Let's say the overall indicator decreased by 100 conditional units. One direction accounted for minus 80, another for minus 30, and a third grew by 10. In total, we get the same minus 100, but now it's clear where to start the investigation.

From the bot's message, you can go to the original report. The user selects the breakdowns and immediately sees the desired data view. There's no need to set filters again and search for where the change occurred.

However, looking only at percentages is not enough. A small category can grow by tens of percent and have almost no impact on the overall result. Therefore, the bot ranks factors by their contribution to the total difference.

And here comes an important boundary. Contribution answers the question "where did the change occur?", but not always "why?". These two questions are easily confused, especially when you want to quickly provide a ready answer.

If one segment accounted for a large part of the decline, it doesn't prove that a technical failure or a product problem occurred specifically there. Such a conclusion requires additional context – therefore, when the bot identifies the main factor, it analyzes the indicators within the segment in more detail.

What was actually assigned to the model

By this point, all substantive calculations are complete. The system knows if the data is ready, how much the metric has changed, what it was compared to, and which segments contributed the most.

The model receives only this short set of facts and turns it into a coherent comment. Relative changes are pre-calculated, and absolute values are inserted by Python code. The model does not calculate revenue, does not select thresholds, and does not fill in gaps.

This division turned out to be convenient and slightly counter-intuitive:

The less freedom we gave the model, the more useful its answer became

Anything that can be unambiguously expressed by a rule remains in the code. The model gets the job for which it is truly useful: to assemble several facts into a short text and not force the user to decipher a table.

We also don't feed it the entire report. Extra context doesn't help here: the more random connections the model sees, the easier it is for it to come up with a plausible explanation. For the note, data on a specific deviation is sufficient.

Why a good prompt is not enough

Even with correct input data, the model sometimes amplifies the output. The calculation says: "The main negative contribution came from direction A." The text suddenly says: "Revenue decreased due to a technical error in direction A." It sounds logical, but the model had no information about a technical error.

Therefore, before the bot sends the generated note to the user, it undergoes two checks:

  1. Filter out placeholders, template responses, and messages without data.

  2. Compare the text with the original set of facts and look for numbers or statements that were not there.

However, checking only the text without the original data is useless: in such a case, we evaluate the persuasiveness of the wording, not its accuracy.

Here's how two answers might differ:

🟢 The metric deviated from the weekly benchmark. The main negative contribution came from direction A and revenue type B. Based on the available data, the change is localized in this segment, but the root cause cannot yet be determined.

🔴 The metric decreased due to a technical error in direction A.

The second answer is shorter, more confident, and even more pleasant to read. That's precisely why it cannot be sent: the system knows nothing about a technical error.

One system – different reports

Different teams need different parts of Big10. One user cares about a specific vertical, another about a category within it. The subscription stores the selected slice, language, and report type. The user changes the settings themselves via buttons in Mattermost, without our help.

If several people choose the same slice, the bot doesn't repeat the same calculation for each. The result is reused, and the sending log protects against duplicates.

After the digest, you can ask a follow-up question in the same chat. The bot selects available context, passes it to the model along with the question, and checks the answer again. Access rights do not change: the bot should not display information that the user would not see in the original system.

What we found out along the way

The quality of the explanation starts with the quality of the data. It sounds obvious, but it's precisely this that saves us from the most unpleasant errors. No prompt can fix incomplete loading or turn an incorrect figure into a reliable conclusion. Therefore, before analysis, the bot always checks the readiness of the sources.

Anomaly, contribution, and cause are not the same thing. An automatic rule detects unusual metric movement. Decomposition shows where it occurred. And the root cause is usually confirmed by a person – sometimes with the help of additional sources. If these stages are mixed, the system will start issuing plausible hypotheses as established facts.

Limitations make the language model more useful. The less freedom we give it in working with numbers and facts, the more reliably it performs its task – formulating a clear comment based on already calculated data.

The checking component needs the same source facts as the generator. Otherwise, one probabilistic model simply evaluates the answer of another – persuasively, but without the ability to verify if it matches the data.

As Big10 Pulse developed, less obvious tasks also emerged:

  • Access management. If a user cannot open the source report, the bot should not send them the content. Therefore, the system first checks rights and, if they are missing, suggests requesting access.

  • Observability. It's important to understand not only whether the bot works technically but also how it is used: which reports are subscribed to, how the audience is growing, and at which stages errors occur. For monitoring, we chose Grafana – there we track product usage and failures.

  • Feedback collection. The main users of Big10 Pulse are managers and executives who need to get answers quickly and report just as quickly if the report lacks the necessary breakdown, context, or feature. Therefore, feedback for us is not an optional extra but part of product development.

What was the final result

During the user testing phase, the bot had 9 confirmed subscribers. Even with this small base, we estimated savings of approximately 99 person-hours per month: 9 users × 30 minutes per day × 22 working days. Later, another 55 users independently set up subscriptions to the service.

This is an estimated calculation, not an exact timekeeping of saved time. The change in the scenario itself is more telling:

⏱️ Instead of 30–60 minutes to check a report, a user spends one to two minutes reading an already prepared analysis.

🤖 The bot did not replace the analyst and did not learn to find the root cause of any movement on its own. It took on a narrower task: waits for correct data → notices deviations → shows the main factors to the person and leads them to the first verifiable hypothesis.

The most important decision was not to add a language model, but to stop it in time

Numbers, calendar, and rules remained in ordinary code. The model received a small but suitable role – to explain what a person should look at first. Sometimes the best way to make an AI tool more reliable is to give the AI a little less work.

How to build a similar solution yourself

The architecture of Big10 Pulse is tied to our reports, but the sequence of actions can be transferred to other monitoring systems.

Start with one report and one metric. Describe when the data is considered ready, with which periods it is correct to compare it, and which fluctuations are expected. It's better to write these rules in ordinary code and cover them with a few examples – a model is not needed here.

Add decomposition of the total difference into components. It should answer the question "where did the change occur?" without trying to automatically prove the cause.

Only then connect the language model. Feed it a short set of already calculated facts and ask it to formulate a comment. A separate check should receive both the text and the same set of source data.

Delivery and personalization can be added last. They are useful when the calculation itself is stable and you understand which slices users really need.
