A Sensation from OpenAI

OpenAI announced that 10,000 agents worked for 88 hours on solving the Navier-Stokes problem, which has remained unsolved for 90 years. According to the statement from OpenAI's head of research, this solution cost several million dollars in computational equivalent.

The Navier-Stokes equations describe the motion of viscous fluids, essentially being the cornerstone of fluid dynamics. The problem is that we don't know if these equations are always correct. This "don't know" is called the Navier-Stokes existence and smoothness problem. To receive the $1 million prize from the Clay Mathematics Institute, one must either provide a counterexample or prove that the equations are always correct. The solution published by OpenAI shows that under certain conditions, one of the equations yields an infinite fluid velocity. Since this is impossible, it means the equations are not always correct.

At the time of writing this article, there has been no independent mathematical verification. Moreover, OpenAI refused to publish the proof itself. This means the scientific community cannot actually verify this fantastic claim. This is not just damn suspicious. It refutes the claim "we solved the million-dollar problem," as the Clay Institute requires a full peer-reviewed publication, two years of acceptance by the mathematical community, and nomination for the award (the two-year period is due to facts like the solution to the four-color problem – it was initially solved in the late 19th century, but an error in the proof was found ten years later. Similarly, an error was found in Wiles' proof of Fermat's Last Theorem, which he then spent about a year correcting – translator's note).

At first glance, this is a huge breakthrough. A problem that mathematicians couldn't solve for almost a century has finally been solved by AI. AI has finally broken free from its constraints, shown real understanding of the problem, and created an innovative solution that works. It looked like the awakening of machine consciousness...

But How?

The simple question is: how could the company have copied Buckmaster and Alpege's work?

So, if Buckmaster and Alpege did not opt out, then the company's models could have been trained on their data. Personally, I think these guys would hardly forget to opt out, especially considering Alpege's work at Anthropic. Unfortunately, I couldn't find reliable confirmation of this. But here's the problem: in theory, OpenAI could have accessed this data even if the scientists opted out.

Most models these days are reasoning models. They break down a user's prompt into several hidden sub-prompts, building chains. Similar to how a person breaks down a task into sub-tasks and tries to find a solution. This multi-step process looks like "AI reasoning." But it's hidden (although in some cases you can ask it to output it) – the user doesn't see the entire chain of reasoning.

However, in light of Buckmaster's accusations, many decided to re-check OpenAI's privacy policies. According to these checkers (here's one of them), opting out of training does NOT include data generated by the reasoning chain; it only includes what the user entered and the final output of the model. The company has access to intermediate prompts and can train models on this chain. That is, indirectly, it has access to your original prompt, just broken down and annotated. Now recall what the guys used to test their work – Codex! This testing is extremely complex, and it requires huge chains of reasoning. So, even if Buckmaster did opt into training, the models could still learn from his work and reproduce his solution.

This is precisely how the company could have stolen the mathematicians' work. OpenAI could have learned that two mathematicians were verifying a solution to a "million-dollar" problem. And then claimed that the AI "found the solution first."

This is why the phrase "10,000 agents, 88 hours" is very suspicious. This approach works precisely when the result is already embedded in the model, and you just need to extract it.
