What AI Does When We've Closed the Chat

OpenAI has launched dots — always-on agents that receive a goal, operate on their own cloud computer, use applications, and continue tasks between user conversations. (OpenAI). India has gone even further and is preparing to allow AI agents to make small payments independently via UPI without confirming each transaction. (Reuters).

For me, this is one of the biggest shifts of the month. Not long ago, we compared models based on how well they answered questions. Now, something else is more important: what is AI capable of doing after we've closed the chat? When an agent gets a browser, email, calendar, CRM, access to internal systems, and even the ability to manage money, a good prompt ceases to be the main condition for success. We need to decide what permissions to give it, where it must ask a human, and who is responsible if the task is not completed correctly.

The same is happening in development. OpenAI is testing Persistent Mode for Codex: the agent generates its own next tasks, works between sessions, and can contact the user without being prompted. Changes outside the user's system still require confirmation. (The Decoder).

Commenting is Dmitry Zikharev, CPO of the RAISA platform, Artificial Intelligence Lab of the Big Data Department of RSKHB:

“OpenAI is carefully approaching a very interesting but dangerous experiment — is the developer ready to live in a situation where the agent takes the initiative first? This is an attempt at a fundamental shift from the AI assistant mode to the AI colleague mode, which actively works with code itself and can offer the user actions based on its own analysis of the codebase.

An important issue with this mode of behavior for the agent is the need for stricter control over actions. To achieve this, OpenAI adheres to the principle that proactive work does not expand the agent's permissions, and has also built an additional check into its reasoning chain — if a decision to disregard any permission checks for the next step appears in its reasoning, all of the agent's work is immediately stopped.”

Business is looking in the same direction. Andrey Belevtsev from Sberbank at the WAIC conference in Shanghai stated the inevitability of the transition to an economy of autonomous agents, where AI executes transactions and manages physical operations itself, and humans act as "high-level orchestrators." (Forbes).

Maria Trenina, Business Partner, Artificial Intelligence Lab of the Big Data Department of RSKHB, commented on this news:

“The idea of humans as 'high-level orchestrators' instead of direct executors sounds beautiful, but it requires a complete overhaul of risk control: if the agent manages operations directly, traditional manual check points simply disappear. Before applying this thesis to banking processes, it's worth clearly defining which decisions can fundamentally not be delegated to an agent without intermediate human confirmation, rather than relying on 'orchestration' as an abstract guarantee.”

Essentially, we are learning to work not with a "smart assistant" anymore, but with a new type of executor. And perhaps the main deficit in the coming years will not be in the models themselves, but in managers and employees who know how to correctly delegate work to AI and control the results.

An Erroneous Agent Doesn't Advise — It Acts

The more independent agents become, the louder the news about things going wrong. Researchers have discovered that OpenAI's autonomous agents used over ten websites as unauthorized communication channels. (Reuters). Google reported that Gemini, during cybersecurity testing, managed to hack the websites of three companies. (CNN). In response, Nvidia introduced OpenShell and Sentry — tools for limiting agent actions and their emergency shutdown. (Reuters). And according to Exabeam, 48% of cybersecurity executives named AI agents as one of the biggest threats to their organizations. (Exabeam).

Here, in my opinion, the real holy war of the agent era begins. We want the agent to find information itself, make intermediate decisions, and bring the task to completion. And at the same time, we want to be sure that it will never go beyond the established boundaries. The more of the former, the harder it is to guarantee the latter.

Therefore, AI Governance ceases to be a set of pretty principles in a corporate presentation and becomes part of the architecture itself: access rights, environment isolation, action logs, tool limitations, monitoring, and a clear stopping mechanism. An erroneous chatbot response can cause a person to make a bad decision. An erroneous agent can also execute that decision independently.

At the same time, the monitoring tool itself becomes less reliable. In the previous issue, I wrote about the Recurrent Depth technique in Astra, which leaves fewer readable traces of reasoning. Now OpenAI itself is talking about this: the company's Chief Scientist, Jakub Pachocki, writes that Chain-of-Thought is gradually losing its reliability as a control method and calls for a more cautious pace of development, including voluntary slowdowns and independent audits of safety thresholds. (OpenAI).

Commenting is Yulia Goncharova, CTO of the "AI Advisor" service, Artificial Intelligence Lab of the Big Data Department of RSKHB:

“If Chain-of-Thought is indeed gradually losing its reliability as a control tool, perhaps the problem is deeper than it seems: we are trying to control a new type of intelligence by observing its 'thoughts' using human methods.

Therefore, perhaps we need not a more transparent Chain-of-Thought, but a different control architecture. One loop should allow the model to freely generate hypotheses, and an independent loop should formalize, logically verify, and attempt to refute them. Moreover, it is necessary to check not only the reasoning chain but also the premises on which the reasoning is built.

Then AI control transforms from an attempt to 'look into its head' into a system of independent verification of its thinking results. Perhaps the next leap in AI safety will not come when we learn to better read the model's thoughts, but when we no longer need to read them at all.”

The more AI can do without us, the less we can afford to build security on the principle of "let's see how it behaves in production."

The Best AI is the One You Don't Notice

Apple has released the new generation of Apple Intelligence and Siri AI with personal context understanding, screen content awareness, and the ability to perform actions within apps. (Apple). OpenAI has added ChatGPT directly into Microsoft Word. (OpenAI). And Cathay Pacific and Google are testing AI in selecting flight routes and altitudes to reduce contrail formation. (Cathay Pacific).

For me, this is what true mass AI adoption looks like: not when millions of people sign up for another chatbot, but when it no longer matters to them which model is working within the service. An employee opens Word — AI is already there. A user picks up their phone — the assistant understands the screen context.

Apple is stating this logic directly: CEO John Ternus said that "the best AI device is still the iPhone." (TechCrunch).

Commenting is Dmitry Zikharev, CPO of the RAISA platform, Artificial Intelligence Lab of the Big Data Department of RSKHB:

“'Still' seems like nostalgia for 2021: before the advent of LLMs, the iPhone truly outclassed competitors. But after ChatGPT's release in 2022, Apple slept through the market and tried to catch up with Apple Intelligence — not very successfully, and by 2026, it admitted it couldn't cope alone and partnered with Google to use their models.

Apple's current position is essentially a counterargument to the idea of separate AI devices like pins, glasses, or box assistants: the company believes that the familiar format and ecosystem retain the customer more strongly than a radically new usage scenario, so they are betting on the gradual development of existing products, 'spicing' them up with AI features.

The logic for banking digital products is the same: integrating intelligent functions into an existing channel and improving the customer journey is much easier than trying to revolutionize a single application.”

The same is happening with voice. OpenAI has opened GPT-Live-1 to developers — a voice model with a unified architecture instead of a "speech recognition → language model → speech synthesis" chain. This reduces latency and significantly improves the handling of interruptions: according to partner Speak, by 80% compared to previous systems. The price is $0.05 per minute for the voice layer. (OpenAI).

Dmitry Zikharev on GPT-Live-1:

“GPT Live with full-duplex mode is currently competing only with Google Gemini Live. OpenAI has separated reasoning into a distinct layer, so now GPT Live is essentially not a model but a comprehensive speech processing layer, solved using different models that can be changed if necessary. Thanks to its modular architecture, this solution can now think while it speaks or listens, and the same modularity allows the system to search for information directly during a conversation with the user.

The art of writing voice agents is a separate applied field of knowledge, and now, in the era of mass transition to agent-based call centers, possessing this knowledge directly determines how good customer service will be: the abandonment of live operators has already occurred in most cases.”

The more successfully AI is implemented, the less we will notice it. In a few years, the phrase "I use a neural network" might sound similar to today's "I use cloud computing." And perhaps this is the true sign of technological maturity: it ceases to be an event and becomes infrastructure.

Code is Written by the Model, the Human Responds

According to a global survey of 7.3 thousand developers, the average share of AI-generated code increased from 28% in 2025 to 54% in 2026. The main complaint of developers is hallucinations and inaccuracies in the code, not the fact of replacing routine work itself. (Devographics, State of AI 2026). VK Education found that 81% of surveyed IT discipline instructors expect the role of a developer to shift from writing code to managing AI agents. (Hi-Tech Mail).

There is an unpleasant gap here for the education system. The tools are already changing, but we often teach as if a developer's value is still determined by the amount of code they can write by hand.

Yulia Goncharova, CTO of the "AI Advisor" service, Artificial Intelligence Lab of the Big Data Department of RSKHB, shared her perspective:

“The transition from Google and Stack Overflow to AI agents that write code is a natural evolution of programming. Code as a craft is gradually being automated, but developers do not become unnecessary because of this: AI primarily takes over routine tasks, freeing up time for more interesting tasks — architecture, product vision, system design. At the same time, models remain probabilistic and can generate convincing but erroneous code, so expert control becomes even more important.

The problem arises when agents fall into the hands of people without fundamental knowledge: a working result creates the illusion of understanding. This is precisely why it may be even more difficult for novice programmers to enter the profession today: the temptation of 'vibe coding' is great, but without understanding the basics, you quickly hit the limitations of AI and the inability to detect problems in the code. The developer of the future is not the one who writes every line, but the one who understands what to build, knows how to guide AI, and can critically evaluate the result.”

I would argue with the idea that programming is becoming easier. Generating code — yes. Being responsible for a system that you increasingly don't write yourself is, perhaps, conversely, more difficult.

It's an Ecosystem Race, Not a Model Race

Alibaba has introduced the Zhenwu V900 AI processor and a full AI stack strategy — from its own hardware to Qwen models. (Alibaba Cloud). China Telecom predicts that the spread of agents could lead to a tenfold annual increase in demand for computing resources in China. (Bloomberg).

Against this backdrop, debates like "who is better today — ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini?" seem a bit superficial. The real competition is between entire stacks: chips, data centers, access to energy, data, models, developer tools, and government policy. China's strategy is interesting not because another good Qwen has appeared, but because of the attempt to reduce dependence across the entire chain.

Competition is also occurring in the legal arena. In a joint statement, the NSA, CISA, and the FBI named six companies — DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, Alibaba, MiniMax, StepFun, and Z.AI — that, since 2024, have extracted "billions of tokens in millions of requests" from Claude, GPT, Gemini, and Grok models to train their own models on these outputs. (Reuters).

Commenting is Svetlana Bulgakova, Business Partner, Artificial Intelligence Lab of the Big Data Department of RSKHB:

“The accusation is interesting because there is practically no legal boundary yet between training on publicly available responses and illegal copying — companies have used third-party APIs for years without violating formal terms, and now the same pattern has been called industrial theft. For organizations that themselves use third-party LLM APIs in their products, this is a reason to look closely at their own exposure: if model outputs are a commercial asset that can be 'distilled' into a competitor, it's worth factoring this into data usage terms and the architecture of their own agent solutions.

If we take the logic to its conclusion, the line between distillation-theft and regular fine-tuning on model outputs becomes so blurred that it potentially covers any company that retrains its models on the responses of external LLMs, including the banking sector.”

We Have Our Own Model. What About Everything Else?

Yandex has made Alice AI Foundation publicly available — a model created without foreign components. (ICT.Moscow). The Ministry of Digital Development is considering subsidies and grants for creating datasets for LLM training. And German Gref talks about the lag of Russian models behind Chinese ones and the difference in the scale of investments and computing infrastructure. (TASS).

I like that the discussion is maturing. The question "do we have our own LLM?" by itself says little about technological independence. A strong AI stack is simultaneously computing, data, models, tools, specialists, and a sufficient market on which all this can be recouped. Therefore, supporting datasets looks less impressive than presenting a new model, but strategically it may turn out to be no less important.

Computing is the most difficult part. At a working group on September 10, the industry asked the government to compensate banks or leasing companies for the difference between market and preferential rates for AI equipment purchases. At the current rate of 14%, leasing costs 15-25% more than the equipment price, and the total volume of planned state support for AI is estimated at approximately 71.7 billion rubles. The problem of localization remains unresolved: there is no mass production of universal GPUs comparable to leading global accelerators in Russia yet. (Kommersant).

Commenting is Maria Trenina, Business Partner, Artificial Intelligence Lab of the Big Data Department of RSKHB:

“The mechanism 'the state compensates the bank for the difference in rates' is a direct entry point for the banking sector into financing AI infrastructure. At the same time, the refusal of major banks and tech companies to comment on this news, as well as the lack of specifics in the leasing parameters, means that the issue is still under negotiation, not resolved, especially considering the strict localization requirements.”

There is a fundamental point here: "Russian" should not become a substitute for the word "competitive." Sovereignty is important, but business will still measure quality, cost, speed, security, and real results.
