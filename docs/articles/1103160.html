Why the most powerful tool for knowledge systematization we've ever created is being used to wall people off from knowledge, and what a more sensible division of labor between humans and models might look like.

In Alexander Belyayev's novel 'The Air Seller' (1929), the businessman Bailey pumps out the planet's atmosphere and sells bottled air to people. Today, a similar story is repeating with knowledge: what belonged to everyone is becoming a commodity.

Humanity's main task has remained unchanged for thousands of years: to gain knowledge about the world through experience and experiment, to record it, and to pass it on to future generations. For this, we invented writing, books, libraries, universities, and the internet.

Today, we find ourselves in a paradox. Never before has humanity accumulated so much knowledge, and rarely has access to it been so difficult. Two walls have risen between people and knowledge.

The first wall is cost and closure. Knowledge created by millions of people and made publicly available has become a commodity. Search increasingly serves advertising rather than inquiry. The texts of the open internet have become raw material for models, the results of which are sold to us via subscription.

The second wall is unsystematicity. We have accumulated more than we can read. Millions of books and billions of pages lie on shelves and in digital archives, and we ourselves no longer remember what's in them. Knowledge that no one can find is no different from lost knowledge.

Language models are the first tool in history capable of breaking down both walls: reading everything accumulated, systematizing it, and returning knowledge to people. But today, we are using them in a way that makes the walls only higher.

Part I. What Technology Has Given Us.

1. The Machine That Learned to Measure Meaning.

The alphabet is one of humanity's greatest inventions: a few dozen letters yield millions of words. But writing fixes how a word looks, not what it means. One word lives in different senses: a 'key' for a door, a 'key' to a problem. Different words mean almost the same thing: 'big,' 'huge,' 'colossal.' And forms of the same word – 'read,' 'read,' 're-reading' – appear as different words to a letter-based search.

As long as there were as many texts as a person could read, the reader themselves reconstructed the meaning. When the flow of texts increased by orders of magnitude, tools working with letters, not meaning, could no longer cope.

Engineers found a third way, between the letter and the word. A letter is too small: it carries no meaning on its own. A word is too large: each of its forms has a different spelling, and the overall meaning is lost. Therefore, text is divided into tokens—stable combinations of letters, smaller than a word but larger than a letter.

A set of tokens is compiled before the model is trained, by a separate program—a tokenizer. It scans a vast corpus of texts, iterates through candidates—all possible letter combinations—and statistically selects those that occur most stably and are most often helpful in assembling words. The size of the vocabulary is an engineering compromise. A character-level vocabulary turns every word into a long chain and multiplies computations. A word-level vocabulary yields millions of unrelated entries, and the model simply won't recognize a new word. The vocabulary of a modern model consists of tens and hundreds of thousands of tokens. For our Kabardian language translator, where one word can contain a whole phrase, a vocabulary of 32,000 tokens with a few dozen letters in the alphabet proved optimal.

The tokenizer knows nothing about roots, prefixes, and suffixes. These are human categories invented to explain language to each other, not to describe it economically. Yet, statistics often arrive at almost the same place: the most stable fragments often coincide with parts of words. Almost, but not quite.

Many do not know that models place not words, but tokens, in a multidimensional space. Each fragment receives coordinates—a vector of hundreds or thousands of numbers. By learning to predict the next fragment in billions of human sentences, the model shifts these coordinates slightly with each error, until tokens with similar meanings end up close together, and dissimilar ones far apart. The model was not taught grammar, yet researchers find parts of speech, tense, and number in its vectors. And the position of a word depends on context: 'key' in a sentence about a lock and 'key' in a sentence about a spring receive different coordinates. Ambiguity, which the alphabet could not resolve in principle, the model resolves in each specific sentence.

2. What This Offers: Search and Selection by Meaning.

Classical search finds matching letters. It doesn't know that 'doctor' and 'physician' are the same, or that in English, 'bank' (river) and 'bank' (financial institution) are different. The vector space measures the distance between meanings: a query and a found fragment may not share a single word but talk about the same thing. By the same principle, one can determine which sentences are closest to the essence of a paragraph, chapter, or entire book.

In my opinion, this is where the main power of models lies: not so much in 'thinking,' but in searching and selecting, in the ability to extract from a vast corpus of texts precisely what semantically relates to the query in fractions of a second. Give a person such instant access to the relevant fragments of thousands of books, and in many tasks, their conclusions will be no worse than those of large models. The gap between humans and machines is largely created not by a difference in intelligence, but by a difference in the volume and speed of access to context.

We now have a tool capable of breaking down both walls. The question is how we use it.

Part II. Why We Are Moving in the Wrong Direction.

3. The First Wall Grows: Enclosure of Knowledge.

In England from the 16th to 19th centuries, common lands where everyone could graze livestock were fenced off and turned into private property. Lawyer James Boyle, as early as 2003, called what was happening to information the 'second enclosure.' With the advent of large models, it has accelerated.

  • Search Has Stopped Searching. For many years, search engines led us to the most reliable sources. Today, the first results pages are occupied by advertising, sponsored links, and content optimized for the algorithm, not the reader.

  • Common Heritage Became Raw Material. Models are trained on the open web—articles, books, code, scientific papers by millions of people—usually without permission or compensation. The legal aspect is currently being decided by courts: in 2025, Anthropic agreed to pay authors $1.5 billion in the Bartz v. Anthropic lawsuit.

  • The Result of Training is Closed. It would be logical to expect that a model trained on common heritage would remain part of it. The opposite happened: the training datasets and weights of leading models are closed, and the knowledge synthesized from our own texts is sold back to us.

  • Sources are Closing in Response. According to the Consent in Crisis study (Data Provenance Initiative, 2024), a significant portion of key web resources have prohibited data collection for AI. The open web is shrinking.

The result is the formation of two levels of access to knowledge. The machine has the entire corpus of human texts, while humans have an advertising-driven search and a paid intermediary. We compare them and conclude that the machine is 'smarter.' But this is an unfair competition: one participant sees the entire library, the other sees a shop window. Human thinking is devalued not because it is worse, but because it has been cut off from its sources.

4. The Second Wall Stands: Knowledge Remains Unsystematized.

Models do indeed read everything. But the way we use them dissolves knowledge rather than systematizing it.

Knowledge is Dissolved in Weights. A model does not store books—it compresses them into billions of parameters, like a cramming student who remembers patterns but not pages. When answering 'from memory,' it cannot explain why this particular sequence of words was generated. Neither the page, nor the author, nor a verifiable quote.

Understanding is Discarded. I discussed the logic of the process in my previous articles: a model reads a book, builds a rich internal representation of it, answers one question about the book, and discards all weights at the end of the session. The next user and the next model start from scratch. Most of the energy spent understanding long documents is wasted on repeating work already done, recalculating weights for a new session.

Repositories are Fragmented. The industry's solution—searching vector databases—creates a new problem. A vector is a coordinate in the space of one specific model. Vectors from different models are incompatible: change the model, and you have to re-index. Even a simple index with one vector per sentence takes up tens of times more space than the text itself. And a thousand numbers will tell a person nothing.

As a result, instead of a unified, systematized library, we get thousands of fragmented repositories, divided by the spaces of different models and locked within different products. Each company re-translates the same books into its own vectors, no one can use another's work, and humans have no access to any of it. The tool capable of breaking down the second wall is today reinforcing the first.

Part III. Risks: Two Degenerations.

5. Humans Degrade from Super-Delegation, Models from Super-Synthesis

If a model is always ready with an answer, the incentive to figure things out oneself disappears: to seek cause-and-effect relationships, compare sources, structure information. Early studies from MIT Media Lab and Microsoft Research (2025) are already documenting a decrease in engagement and critical thinking among those who offload intellectual work to AI. The data is still scarce, but the trend is alarming. The danger is not that the model helps, but that it replaces.

The second degradation is less obvious. Models can only extract statistics from human knowledge. If people stop creating new texts, and the internet fills with generated content, subsequent models will learn from the conclusions of previous ones. Shumailov and co-authors demonstrated this experimentally in the journal Nature (2024): models trained on data from other models, from generation to generation, lost rare and unusual elements and converged to an averaged, impoverished language. The authors called this 'model collapse.'

I myself have often seen how models tend to drift towards the average when they have few examples. Rare languages, non-standard phrasing, unusual thoughts are the first to dissolve in averaging.

The two problems feed each other. People who delegate thinking to models stop creating new knowledge, and models without new human knowledge degenerate. The main task itself—to gain and transmit knowledge—is under threat.

6. Why I Don't Believe in Superintelligence

I am a proponent of enhancing human capabilities with models, rather than creating a 'superintelligence' that will supposedly surpass us in everything.

Models learn by copying a pattern. To create superintelligence, you need a pattern of superintelligence, and there isn't one. Proponents of superintelligence will object: AlphaZero surpassed world champions in chess and Go by playing against itself. But a game has clear rules and an infallible judge—victory or defeat. In knowledge about the world, there is no such judge. The truth of a conclusion is verified by reality: experiment, experience, people responsible for decisions.

Therefore, superintelligence, in my opinion, is a utopia. But a dangerous utopia: in pursuit of it, we may rashly give models what makes us creators of knowledge, and thereby halt its production. And along with it, the development of the models themselves, which depend on it.

Part IV. A Path to a Sustainable Future.

7. Annotate Once—and Make Accessible to All.

Billions of parameters are needed for a model to learn to understand language. To use this understanding, they are not needed every time.

The understanding of a large model can be 'extracted' from a book once and saved with it in a simple, open, and human-understandable format: the book, divided into semantic blocks (scenes in fiction, conceptual sections in science); the weights of each word, sentence, paragraph, and chapter—how important each is for conveying the meaning of the whole; key sentences of each block; groups of words close in meaning within the context of that specific book. How such a table is built and verified, I detailed in another article and will not repeat it here. I will add one thing: if a book is annotated by several independent models and their scores are averaged, the random deviations of each model are largely canceled out.

The result is not billions of opaque parameters or thousands of numbers per sentence, but annotated text with meaning tables: compact, verifiable, tied to specific pages, and independent of any single model. Heavy computation—once per book. Usage costs almost nothing and works on any device, locally and privately.

Imagine a book like Earth from space. From orbit, only continents are visible—the main idea. As you approach, cities emerge—chapters and scenes. Closer still—streets and houses, individual paragraphs and phrases. With a single slider movement, the reader can compress the book to the main content page or expand it to the full text. This is not a summary invented by the model, but a filtering of the text itself by weights: every word in the compressed version of the text belongs to the author.

This is how both walls are broken down simultaneously: knowledge becomes systematized and remains open. If a book can be compressed tens or hundreds of times without losing its main meaning, a person can work not with three books on a topic, but with three hundred. The gap in access to context, which today makes a machine 'smarter,' begins to close.

Models also benefit. On subsequent requests, the model receives not the entire book, but a few precisely selected fragments: an order of magnitude less data, a faster and cheaper response. A small model on a phone, relying on tables obtained from large models, answers questions about a book at a level close to that of a large model. And any model can rely on thousands of annotated books and quote them, instead of generating phrases from memory whose origin cannot be traced.

8. An Open Standard for Meaning Tables is Needed.

The key issue is not technical but organizational: how to create a universal open standard for book meaning tables, into which any model could export its analysis for use by both humans and other models?

Such a standard must be:

  • model-independent: storing not coordinates in someone's private space, but word weights, structure, key sentences, and connections tied to the text itself;

  • human-readable: anyone can open the table and see why a particular paragraph is considered important;

  • cumulative: annotation by a new model refines the previous one, rather than starting from scratch;

  • open: an annotated book should be as accessible as the book itself.

The printed book, the library catalog, the web page—each such standard transformed disparate knowledge into a common infrastructure. An annotated book can become the next: knowledge analyzed once will cease to burn up in sessions and be locked in closed repositories.

9. The Right Division of Labor.

Only one model is sustainable: each does what it does best.

Models take on a gigantic amount of routine work that humans could not accomplish in centuries: reading everything accumulated, annotating, weighting, connecting. The result is a new generation of open libraries—books that explain themselves, compress and expand on demand. Humans gain access to knowledge as fast as machines: not a summary from a black box, but direct access to the primary source with key passages, connections, and quotes. And they do what a model cannot: conduct experiments, test ideas against reality, make decisions, take responsibility for them, and create new knowledge.

This cycle is sustainable. Humans create knowledge, models systematize it, humans find what they need faster and create new knowledge, models receive fresh human material. Each strengthens the other, not replaces it. Humans do not degrade from super-delegation, nor models from super-synthesis.

Conclusion: Why We Need All This.

Each generation inherits more than it can read. Expedition reports, forgotten monographs, field notes, diaries, translations, texts in rare languages await on shelves and in archives, and most of them will never be opened by anyone. Not because they are useless, but because no one has the time to find out what's inside.

This is precisely where language models can do what no single generation of humans can: read everything we have collected, annotate it, and show what is contained in each text and how important it is for our understanding of the world. What would have taken humans centuries can be done in years. This, in my opinion, is their true purpose: not to think for us, but to return our own knowledge to us—systematized and verifiable.

The path we are on today leads in a different direction: to paid access, closed indexes, and answers without sources or the ability to evaluate them. To change direction, no breakthrough is needed. It requires a decision to record the understanding of a text in an open form once and share it as we have always shared books.

Models cannot discover how the world works. They can help us preserve what has already been discovered and navigate it faster. We will still be the ones to discover the rest.

Not a superintelligence that will replace us, but a tool that will break down both walls and return to people access to everything humanity has already learned.

P.S. I am personally trying this path in practice with SayFable—an offline library for iPhone and Mac. To be honest, we are at the very beginning. We are currently annotating public domain fiction books with large models: dividing chapters into scenes, marking key paragraphs, who is speaking, and what each episode is about. The first three dozen such books have already been published openly. The next step is for models to recalculate word weights and key sentences so that a small model on a phone can rely on this annotation. We are still a long way from a universal standard for meaning tables.

Recently, a reader wrote to me, that they would like to have locally (on their PC) the ability to feed it their own (offline) collection of books (I think many people have such collections of recommended and simply saved books) to get the broadest possible statistics for subsequent selection of what to read on a particular topic. In my opinion, this better than any theory shows what models should do. Not read for us and not think for us, but help a person see the statistics of each of a hundred books in minutes—and choose the one that suits them best.
