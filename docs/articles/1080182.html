When Code Ceases to Be King

I'll start with the topic that has most captivated me over the past month. Anthropic published The AI-Native SDLC Playbook — a practical model for re-architecting the entire Software Development Life Cycle around AI agents. It's no longer just about code generation: AI is being integrated into Plan → Design → Build → Test → Deploy → Maintain, with artifacts from one stage automatically triggering the next. (Claude).

This topic, in my opinion, is far more significant than another Claude Code or Copilot. We've spent years trying to bolt AI onto old development processes, but if code is generated significantly faster, the bottleneck simply shifts: from coding to task definition, architecture, code review, security, testing, and approvals.

I anticipate a shift from "developer AI tools" to an AI-native engineering system: requirements become machine-readable specifications, agents handle tasks from definition to PR and testing, CI/CD triggers subsequent agent actions, and observability generates new tasks based on production results. Researchers are already describing a similar model as Spec-Driven Agentic Development: discipline doesn't disappear; it shifts from writing code to the quality of specifications, verification, and governance. (arXiv).

And here lies the most interesting thought for me: code ceases to be the primary artifact of development. The main artifacts become intent, specifications, acceptance criteria, policies, tests, and proof that the agent did exactly what was required. For a developer, this is a significant professional change — value gradually shifts from "I know how to write code" to "I know how to assign tasks to an agent," "I know how to design a system, its constraints, and quality criteria," and "I know how to verify the result and prevent bad solutions from entering production."

There's also a degree of skepticism: there's a risk that "AI-native SDLC" will soon be used to describe any pipeline that simply incorporates a coding agent. A true shift will only occur when companies restructure processes, roles, quality gates, and governance, rather than just buying Claude Code licenses. But for me, this is one of the most important topics of the year because AI coding changes the developer's tool, while AI-native SDLC potentially changes the software development model itself.

While the Industry Debates the Rules

As of September 1st, a significant portion of Federal Law No. 243-FZ came into effect: it introduces concepts of large foundational models, a risk-based approach, support measures for Russian models, and specific requirements for AI use; some provisions will take effect in March 2027. (ConsultantPlus).

The Russian AI market is transitioning from an era of recommendations, codes of conduct, and experiments to an era of formal regulatory architecture. I would pay particular attention to the possibility of establishing areas where sovereign or national foundational models must be used. For the financial market, such decisions must be coordinated with the Bank of Russia. (Garant)

The most interesting part will unfold at the level of subordinate legislation: which systems will fall under restrictions, how risk levels will be determined, and what the requirements will be for data, labeling, and models. It is these details, not the law itself, that will determine the real compliance cost for businesses. Technological sovereignty is a clear goal. But a complex question remains: where is the line after which protecting the domestic market begins to limit companies' access to the best available technology?

Banks, by the way, are not waiting for subordinate acts. VTB has joined the Bank of Russia's AI Ethics Code. The memorandum itself is not a technological sensation, but it frames the question well: in financial AI, we are gradually moving from "can this be done?" to "who is responsible for the model's decision, how is it explained, and where is the limit of acceptable automation?"

Now, about the money: I believe businesses have been calculating the cost of AI based on the price per million tokens for too long. In practice, the main expenses begin later, when the model needs to be integrated into corporate systems, security ensured, data prepared, processes re-engineered, employees trained, and quality control established. CFOs and CIOs will increasingly calculate not the cost of the model itself, but the price of one effectively automated process. And this is a beneficial maturation of the market: "we use AI" is finally ceasing to be a business case in itself. (Forbes — "The Token Economy").

Trust Crisis — and What Banks Should Do About It

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei stated that the negativity towards AI is not a reaction to warnings from AI leaders, but a crisis of trust in institutions that has been brewing for decades. The main problem, he says, is that companies have not yet fulfilled their loud promises. (TechCrunch).

Maria Trenina, Business Partner, AI Lab of the Big Data Department of RSHB, commented on this news:

"Removing blame from the AI labs themselves does not absolve them of responsibility — banks and fintech companies implementing AI automatically inherit this trust deficit along with the 'AI' category itself. The practical takeaway: communication about AI implementation in customer services should be based on measurable results, not on promises of transformation."

An Agent Without Access is Just a Smart Conversationalist

Another thought that has been brewing for a long time: the intelligence of the model is no longer the main limitation for an agent. Without access to ERP, CRM, Git, databases, calendars, file systems, and APIs, it remains a very smart person who was forgotten to be given an office pass. As long as the agent lacks standard, controlled access to internal services, we are looking at an impressive conversationalist rather than a digital employee. (Forbes — "Agent Without a Job")

The main competition may now shift from the AI models themselves to their integration with real systems: interaction protocols between agents, access management, registries of connected tools, and control over agent actions. However, I doubt that MCP is already the future standard: it is dangerous in technology to prematurely designate something as the "TCP/IP for agents." The winner will not necessarily be the most elegant protocol — the one around which an ecosystem forms will win.

A telling example right now is DeepSeek's release of Harness, a platform for autonomous AI agents and a direct competitor to Anthropic's Claude Code. Each module in it is a swappable plugin, and the Cordis engine dynamically connects the necessary dependencies during agent operation. The Code Mode is particularly interesting: instead of step-by-step command interpretation, the agent describes all tools as an SDK and immediately writes a script that solves the task in one pass. (SCMP)

Dmitry Zikharev, CPO of the RAISA platform, AI Lab of the Big Data Department of RSHB, comments:

"Having your own harness for developers is now a must-have for a major AI player — Meta recently got one (Muse Code), and now DeepSeek. Competition is noticeably shifting from the models themselves to the 'model + tooling' combination — it's possible that DeepSeek will eventually move from token-based pricing to subscriptions, as is done with Claude Code."

The Race is Not for Power, but for Price

Z.ai has updated GLM to version 5.3 — a post-training upgrade of GLM-5.2 without changing the base model: Terminal-Bench 3.0 improved from 4.6% to 28.3%, CyberGym to 84.5% (best public result), and the context has been expanded to 1 million tokens. Open weights are promised approximately two weeks after release.

Kirill Malkov, CTO of the AI Lab of the Big Data Department of RSHB, commented on this news — first about GLM 5.3:

"We should approach such jumps in benchmarks with a healthy dose of skepticism — Chinese labs often fine-tune models specifically for the tests on which they will be measured, so high numbers don't always equate to a real breakthrough. But even with this caveat, the precedent itself is important: models that require enormous training resources are increasingly being made publicly available, and the gap with top labs like OpenAI and Anthropic is noticeably narrowing.

A separate — and seemingly more important — story has unfolded around the anonymous model Ox-Alpha: it recently appeared on OpenRouter, showed excellent results almost for free, and a couple of weeks ago it turned out to be the second model from Z.ai — GLM-5.3-Flash. Its strength is not in catching up to the frontier in pure quality, but in efficiency: the model is orders of magnitude more compact than top players like GPT-5.6 Sol or Mythos, costs pennies to run, but is already approaching their level in benchmarks and practice. It seems this is the main trend of recent times — Chinese labs are competing not so much for maximum quality, but for who can provide it cheaper."

Kirill Malkov on Model 2, Anthropic's internal model, which the company uses in parallel with the public Mythos 5 but does not plan to release to the market (Axios):

"Training a top-tier model already consumes almost all of the lab's computational resources, so a 'two different AIs' version — a weak public one and a secretly super-powerful one — is unlikely. It's more likely about the alignment stage: this is where the model receives domain restrictions (e.g., refusal to discuss cyberattacks) — not just due to filters, but because it was physically not trained to answer such questions. It seems labs have several versions of the same powerful base model: a public one, 'groomed' for safety, and an internal one, like Model 2, without these restrictions."

Smarter — Doesn't Mean More Transparent

OpenAI released Astra amidst growing concerns about the safety of autonomous agents: the model has strong cybersecurity capabilities, but control and observability of some reasoning processes are simultaneously decreasing. Reuters notes that OpenAI is considering additional control mechanisms and automatic agent shutdown. (Reuters).

We've reached an unusual situation: a model's capability is becoming a reason not only to buy it but also to restrict access to it. Access to a frontier model will increasingly resemble access to critical infrastructure: customer KYC, monitoring, tool limitations, and different functionality for different user categories. Although there are doubts here too: the industry simultaneously says "agents must become more autonomous" and "we need more control" — these goals coexist perfectly in presentations and much less so in actual architecture.

Speaking of observability: the Recurrent Depth method used by Astra has already raised concerns among AI safety researchers. A powerful model becomes less convenient for its own audit: recurrent depth allows it to conduct some reasoning less linearly, leaving fewer understandable traces for monitoring. And here lies an unpleasant fundamental conflict — we want both a smarter AI and a fully explainable AI, but these two goals may begin to diverge. (TechCrunch).

One possible answer to the question "how to control without losing privacy" is the Private Safety Processing mechanism that OpenAI has implemented in its API: the system operates on the client side, analyzes interaction with the model, and sends a signal about a potential security incident to OpenAI without the prompts and responses themselves — part of the company's transition to zero data retention for frontier models. (OpenAI).

Dmitry Zikharev, CPO of the RAISA platform, AI Lab of the Big Data Department of RSHB, comments:

"The full data retention opt-out (ZDR) option is available from any major cloud AI provider, but as model complexity increases, so does the need for the providers themselves to see at least something for security control — and here OpenAI and Anthropic have taken different paths. Anthropic includes storage of interaction history on its side for 30 days when using Fable 5 or Mythos 5 in the contract, while OpenAI relies on client-side analysis without transmitting the content of requests. The technical details of the mechanism have not yet been disclosed, but if it passes independent audit and proves effective, it could significantly shift the negotiating position of clients who are currently constrained by personal data and banking secrecy requirements."

The Shopper Who Talks to an Assistant

And finally, a piece of news closest to the average user. According to Yandex, traffic from "Alice AI" in Russian e-commerce has grown by 358% since the beginning of 2026, and shopping tasks now account for 12% of all chat requests. (CNews).

Svetlana Bulgakova, Business Partner, AI Lab of the Big Data Department of RSHB, comments:

"Such growth is not accidental but a result of user habit, which has been forming since at least 2024, plus targeted improvements to Alice for shopping scenarios. Yandex holds about 17% of the e-commerce market, so sellers in this market share have effectively had to integrate into Alice's transactional infrastructure — this aligns with the global acceleration of how AI is being incorporated into consumer requests.

But the issue is deeper than adapting content for one specific player: according to Mirakl, less than 1% of e-commerce pages meet even the minimum standards for LLM recommendations, and Amazon notes that almost 64% of products recommended by Alexa are not in the top 10 of regular search results. It seems we are entering an era of new content optimization, not for search engines, but for AI assistants, and the criteria for this optimization are still unclear to both retailers and platforms. For now, the algorithm rules this connection, and the share of purchases through AI continues to grow at an accelerated pace."
