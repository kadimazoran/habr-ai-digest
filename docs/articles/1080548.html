Transferring a file to an external system may violate the company's internal policies, and the very act of sending it means another copy of the recording exists, and someone else is now responsible for its access.

Therefore, I have assembled a local solution based on Whisper. Audio is processed on your own machine, and the output includes text and timestamps.

In this article, we will explore the origins of Whisper, how it converts sound to text, what the official console version can do without additional modifications, and how I applied this technology in practice.

❯ Whisper: The Beginning

OpenAI introduced Whisper. Along with the release page, the company made the code and model weights publicly available. The model was trained on 680,000 hours of multitask and multilingual data collected from across the internet. However, the training dataset itself was not released. Therefore, it is more accurate to speak of open models and code, rather than an open dataset.

The model is multitask. The same network can recognize speech, determine the speaker's language, and translate foreign speech directly into English text.

After its release, the technology did not stand still:

December 2022

large-v2 version released: improved training, added regularization methods; approximately 5% fewer errors in English and 10% in other languages.

March 2023

Whisper API launched: developers can send audio to OpenAI's cloud and receive text without purchasing powerful video cards; starting price — $0.006 per minute.

November 2023

large-v3 released: 128 frequency channels instead of 80, added Cantonese. Trained on 1 million hours of weakly labeled data and 4 million hours labeled by large-v2. For languages where large-v3 error on Common Voice 15 and Fleurs is below 60%, errors are 10–20% lower than with large-v2.

September
2024

turbo version released: decoder reduced from 32 to 4 layers, resulting in a significant speed increase; accuracy remains close to large-v2, but slightly worse for some languages (Thai, Cantonese).

Betting on Scale Instead of New Architecture

Whisper did not introduce a new architecture. The authors took a standard encoder-decoder Transformer and tested a different hypothesis: what happens if, instead of carefully fine-tuning for each dataset, they simply feed the model a vast amount of diverse audio with pre-existing transcriptions.

To understand why this was a controversial decision, context is needed. By 2022, robust speech recognition systems were typically built as follows: first, pre-training on unlabeled audio, then a mandatory fine-tuning stage for a specific task. The problem is that fine-tuning requires a skilled person to prepare it, and the result fits well with familiar tests but performs poorly when the microphone, accent, or topic changes.

The Whisper authors refer to their own experience with CLIP. Fine-tuning on ImageNet increased accuracy by 9.2%, but the average accuracy on seven other datasets did not improve at all. Hence their stated goal: a speech recognition system should work reliably "out of the box" across a wide range of conditions, without separate fine-tuning for each specific scenario.

The Data Wasn't "Unsupervised"

Each audio fragment had a text pair. These pairs were simply taken from the internet and not manually verified line by line.

A total of 680,000 hours of audio were collected. If you listen to such an archive non-stop, it would take approximately 78 years to finish.

The training data is distributed as follows:

  • 438,000 hours, or 65% — English speech with English transcription;

  • 126,000 hours, or 18% — non-English speech translated into English;

  • 117,000 hours, or 17% — non-English speech transcribed in the original language.

How This Mountain of Data Was Cleaned

The main threat was not typos, but other machines. A significant portion of online subtitles is generated not by humans, but by other speech recognition systems. Learning from them meant learning to repeat their errors. For this artifact, the authors coined the term "transcript-ese" and compiled a set of heuristics to clean it up.

How to Distinguish Machine Subtitles from Human Ones

Older recognition systems had almost no proper punctuation. They rarely used commas, semicolons, and question marks, did not divide text into paragraphs, and often wrote everything in uppercase. Humans rarely write like that. Therefore, transcriptions written entirely in UPPERCASE or entirely in lowercase were considered machine-generated. The same applies to text where there isn't a single comma throughout a speaker's utterance. If there are also no paragraphs and proper sentences, the chance that it's from another model, not a human annotator, becomes very high.

The language was checked twice. Audio was run through a detector based on VoxLingua107, and text through CLD2. If the languages didn't match, the pair was usually discarded, but not always.

If the text was English and the speech was not, the pair was not deleted but used for translation data. This is how the 126,000 hours of X→en were collected.

There was a second approach as well
First, a preliminary model was trained, its error rate for each source was calculated, and those with high error rates and large volumes were manually reviewed. These revealed flawed transcriptions, misaligned synchronization, and machine-generated subtitles that simple checks missed. Such sources were removed entirely.

Fragments without speech were not discarded. They were kept in training but used ten times less frequently than others, serving as data for voice detection. The model learned to both hear speech and remain silent when it was absent.

❯ Whisper Under the Hood

Externally, it's simple: audio file in, text out. Internally, between these two points, there are five stages:
audio file → mono 16 kHz → log-Mel spectrogram → encoder → decoder → text and timestamps

First, the Sound is Standardized

Whisper cannot work directly with mp3, m4a, flac, and other formats. The official version runs FFmpeg as a separate process and asks it to output "raw" audio in the only format it understands: mono, 16 kHz, 16-bit integers. These numbers are then divided by 32768 and converted into fractional values approximately between -1 and 1, which Whisper then works with.

This is where the "FFmpeg not found" error, encountered by almost everyone on the first run, originates. Whisper does not link FFmpeg as a library; it calls it. If FFmpeg is not in the PATH, recognition fails.

Next, the recording is cut into 30-second windows. This is not a setting or a heuristic, but a model limitation. 30 seconds at 16 kHz yields exactly 480,000 samples, and the encoder is designed for this specific size. If the file is shorter, the remainder is padded with zeros. If the file is longer, it is processed window by window.

The sound wave is inconvenient for the model. Therefore, the next step is the log-Mel spectrogram, which is a map of the sound. Time runs horizontally, frequency ranges are on the vertical axis, and the brightness of each point indicates how much energy was present at that frequency at that moment.

The logarithm is used to compress the volume difference. The difference between a whisper and a shout in a linear scale is vast. In a logarithmic scale, the difference becomes manageable. The Mel scale does something else. It arranges frequencies as humans perceive them. At the lower end of the range, a difference of a hundred hertz is easily discernible, while at the upper end, it's almost imperceptible. Mel scale mimics this.

The map is calculated using short windows of 25 milliseconds, shifting every 10 milliseconds, with overlapping windows. For 30 seconds, this results in exactly 3000 time slices.

Encoder: Listening

The frequency map first passes through two simple layers that compress it in time. The second layer takes every second slice and halves the sequence length. 3000 slices become 1500 positions.

From this, simple arithmetic follows. One encoder position covers 20 milliseconds of sound. This number will reappear in the timestamps.

Inside the block, the sound is compared to itself. The model looks at where the sound began, what was nearby, and the overall context of the entire chunk. Information about the order of positions is provided by pre-calculated numbers that are simply added to the features.

The encoder's output contains no text words. It's a numerical representation of the sound, which the second half of the model will then process.

Decoder: Writing

The decoder, on the other hand, is essentially a language model. It generates the output sequentially, token by token, and at each step, it looks in two directions: at the text already written and at the sound description from the first half.

Therefore, Whisper is not structured as a "sound → word" dictionary. It's closer to a model that writes plausible text based on what it has heard. This immediately leads to Whisper's main problem, but we'll get to that later.

Timestamps: Where Do They Come From?

The model doesn't measure time with a stopwatch. It guesses it, just like words.

Its vocabulary includes 1501 special tokens representing positions from 0 to 30 seconds with a 20 ms step. For example, the token <|12.34|> means: the next part of the phrase refers to approximately 12.34 seconds within the current 30-second audio segment. During training, such tokens were placed next to the text in the transcriptions, so the model learns to predict them along with words.

However, a 20 ms step does not imply 20 ms accuracy. It's merely a temporal grid from which the model selects the closest position. Therefore, a segment boundary might fall into a pause, slightly precede speech, or combine several short phrases. This is often sufficient for subtitles, but these markers should not be treated as precise word-level annotations.

Word-level timestamps in the official Whisper are obtained differently. After recognition, the model analyzes cross-attention: which parts of the audio were most important for generating each text token. Then, this data is smoothed and aligned with the audio using the Dynamic Time Warping algorithm to estimate the start and end of individual words.

Segment-level labels are predicted directly by the model, while word-level labels are calculated after recognition as an additional estimation.

Therefore, word-level timestamps are usually more convenient for editing and karaoke subtitles, but they can be inaccurate with complex speech, pauses, and noise.

Strict rules govern generation. Timestamps must be in pairs, cannot decrease, segments must have non-zero length, and the very first marker must fall within the first second of the window. The latter restriction was added by the authors specifically to prevent the model from skipping the beginning of the recording.

What Happens to Recordings Longer Than 30 Seconds

The model only sees one window at a time. Everything else is the surrounding framework.

In a single run, the model processes no more than 30 seconds of audio. For longer recordings, an external function splits them into such windows and feeds them to the model one by one. The next window does not necessarily start exactly 30 seconds later. The position is shifted based on the last predicted timestamp. This way, the processor doesn't miss the remainder of a phrase if the model only recognized part of the window.

The text from the previous window is added to the context of the next one by default. This helps maintain a consistent style, for example, by spelling names and terms the same way and continuing phrases across fragment boundaries.

However, context can also carry over errors: if the model starts repeating itself or misrecognizes a phrase, this glitch can sometimes affect the next segment.

For such cases, Whisper provides context reset after decoding with temperature fallback.

Where Fabrications Come From

The decoder does not transcribe in the literal sense. It predicts a plausible continuation of the text, relying on both the audio and its general knowledge of the language. As long as there is clear speech in the recording, both supports pull in the same direction. When the speech ends: silence, noise, unintelligible mumbling. Only the language support remains. And the model continues to write coherent text that was not in the recording.

The official model card explicitly states: due to training on weak, noisy audio-text pairs, the model may generate text that is not present in the recording. This most often occurs during silence, background music, and long pauses, when Whisper "finishes" writing standard phrases like thank-yous, advertising inserts, or simply coherent sentences.

The study "Careless Whisper" (FAccT 2024) ran 13,140 short fragments from the AphasiaBank corpus through the Whisper API. Hallucinations were found in approximately 1.4% of segments. Of these, about 38% contained potentially harmful text. Fabricated scenes of violence, false medical claims, words attributed to speakers. This is not just any local run, nor legal records, but a cloud API on speech with aphasia and a control group. On a large archive, even 1–1.5% yields dozens of instances where no one spoke. The 187 segments that failed in April–May 2023 were re-run in December: 12 remained problematic. The model is being improved, but the risk does not drop to zero.

At the generation level, the decoder selects tokens sequentially: start of transcription, language, mode, start timestamp, words, end timestamp. While the acoustic signal is strong, token selection is strictly tied to the sound; when the signal is weak, the probability distribution "blurs," and the model relies more on the language prior, generating coherent but fabricated continuations.

What Whisper Cannot Do

Whisper does not distinguish speakers. Its multitask format includes transcription, translation to English, language identification, and speech detection, but not speaker labeling like "Speaker 1" and "Speaker 2." The segment dictionary returned by the model simply does not have a field for the speaker.

So, for interviews, podcasts, or meeting recordings, diarization will need to be built as a separate layer. I have already addressed this issue: in my project, I integrated Nvidia NeMo for speaker identification and then matched its segments with words from Whisper. Below, I will show this project and how speaker separation is implemented in it.

❯ Working with "Bare" Whisper

By "bare" Whisper, I mean the official openai-whisper package from the OpenAI repository.

The installation of Whisper and FFmpeg depends on the operating system, so we won't go into detail here. It is assumed that Python, the openai-whisper package, and FFmpeg are already installed, and the command whisper --help executes without errors.

Next, we will look at what can be done with a working Whisper:

  • Obtain regular transcriptions;

  • Create subtitles;

  • Save segment and word-level timestamps;

  • Process multiple files;

  • Provide the model with terms and language hints;

  • Translate speech into English;

  • Pass the result to a separate diarization layer;

--task translate translates speech specifically into English. The turbo model should not be used for this mode: it is designed for transcription, not translation. The README of the official repository specifically warns about this limitation.

Local Doesn't Mean Secure

After downloading the weights, the official package can work offline and does not send audio to OpenAI. However, the original recording, model cache, temporary files, and transcriptions remain on the computer. The launch command does not encrypt or delete anything.

Access rights, disk encryption, backups, and retention periods must be configured separately. Authenticity also does not appear automatically: surnames, dates, amounts, and legal formulations need to be verified against the original recording. Whisper speeds up finding the right spot and provides a draft. It does not replace the recording.

❯ DotAudio: What Had to Be Built Around Whisper

I needed to transcribe recordings that I didn't want to send to a third-party cloud service. The console Whisper solved the problem for individual files, but as a daily tool, it was inconvenient. For each run, you need to select a model, remember parameters, wait for processing, and then separately parse timestamps and combine results.

I wanted a different scenario. Press a keyboard shortcut and dictate text into an already open field. Enable subtitles for computer audio. Open a long recording, jump to the desired utterance, correct an error, and save the result in a suitable format. And all of this should be local.

Thus, DotAudio was born. It's not a new model or a fork of Whisper, but a desktop application built around faster-whisper.

On the first launch, the program checks the computer's available resources and suggests suitable models. After selection, it downloads the necessary components and performs initial setup. CPU and GPU processing are available. It includes voice detection via NVIDIA NeMo and a local assistant for already transcribed text.

Live: Real-time Subtitle Display

Live subtitles display speech as it sounds: from a microphone or computer audio, optionally in a separate window on top of video or a call. While the phrase is still being spoken, the line may change. After a pause, it solidifies.

After stopping Live, the session is re-processed as a regular file, and drafts are replaced with the final result. Temporary audio is briefly stored on disk and then deleted.

Dictation

Ctrl+Alt+Space. The application remembers the active window and displays a small overlay on top of other programs: microphone level, draft text, stop button. Audio is not written to disk. After stopping, the text is refined, and your custom dictionary of terms can be applied. The original transcription remains separately in the history. The final result always goes to the clipboard, and if the window hasn't changed, it's also pasted.

Audio Transcription

A separate page accepts audio and video in common formats. The application does not make a copy for itself; the path to the original is stored in the history. The output is not a single txt file, but a list of utterances with timestamps.

Clicking on a phrase rewinds the recording; the current one is highlighted. You can change the speed, loop a section, edit text, search and replace, merge and split utterances, move boundaries, and mark items for review. The original version of a segment is not destroyed during editing.

Whisper doesn't know who is speaking. Voices are handled as a separate step. NeMo identifies anonymous roles like "Voice 1" based on the received audio, and these can be renamed.

Saving works like a separate window with a preview. Plain text, single line, utterances by role, protocol template, Markdown, SRT, VTT, CSV, JSON, LRC. Time accuracy, names, merging adjacent utterances by the same person, filtering by voice, only items marked for review. What is visible on the right is what will be in the file.

Assistant

The last layer is not directly related to Whisper. A local language model alongside the saved transcription: questions about the recording, summary, key points, tasks, topics. Timestamps in the answer are clickable and lead to the corresponding place in the session.

For short recordings, it sees the whole thing; for long ones, it breaks them down into parts using a special algorithm.

By default, recognition runs on the same machine. Draft subtitles are not written to the database, dictation keeps audio in memory, imported files are not copied, and the temporary live file is deleted after processing.

Internet access is only required during the preparation phase: to download Whisper models, the assistant model, FFmpeg, and NeMo. The project was initially designed so that nothing leaves the computer and goes online.

❯ Conclusion

Whisper was released in 2022 as a standard Transformer. There is no fundamentally new architecture. The bet was on the quantity of data, and it paid off: this is evident in tests under various conditions and in how quickly the tool spread.

The model doesn't turn sound into text. It guesses the next word. Sometimes it's correct, sometimes it writes something that wasn't in the recording.

In practice, a single command is enough for one file. An archive, searching by surname, live subtitles, edits – this is all additional work. Each person builds it for themselves.

Whisper makes recordings searchable, but you shouldn't rely entirely on its results.
