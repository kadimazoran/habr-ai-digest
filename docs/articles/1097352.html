Robert Cecil Martin

This year, it seems we've definitively moved into AI-assisted coding, and there's no turning back. For me personally, it's hard to imagine giving up AI – I'm hooked. But until recently, I couldn't quite grasp why some developers claim AI writes all their code, leaving them with nothing to do, while others insist AI is merely an assistant whose code needs careful review. And then there are those who are completely disillusioned with AI, using it more as a reference than a full-fledged development tool. If that weren't enough, in early October, a certain Brandon Thomas (a nobody to me – I'd never heard of him before) released his ArtCraft project, in which he essentially recreated all popular editors from MS and Adobe (from Word to Photoshop). It's mind-boggling, to say the least. It feels like the industry has surged ahead, and you're clinging to the handrail of a departing train, desperately trying not to let go. Worse still, you watch your fellow travelers jump off mid-ride, unable to endure it, giving up. It's sad.

However, I'm not so easily shaken, and I'm in no hurry to give up. I've seen too many of these "departing trains" in my life to panic. The key is to understand what's happening. If you can't, take a break and try again. The current situation is unusual because everything is changing so rapidly that there's almost no time to rest. But what is certainly present is the contradictory nature of the information coming in. And you'd think you could just figure it all out yourself. I tried, and I figured some things out. But why do my impressions contrast so sharply with those of other developers? This has been bothering me.

And then, quite recently, something happened. I listened to a few more podcasts where developers with vastly different experiences discussed, not in an interview format, but in a casual conversation, how they use AI, their problems, and their impressions – there's plenty of this online now. But then, after one particular podcast, something clicked in my head, and some things suddenly fell into place. I suddenly realized that some programmers write code exactly the way I did a quarter of a century ago. And, mentally transporting myself back to those good old days, I understood that if AI had existed then, I would have said the exact same things and used the same epithets about it. I would have been absolutely thrilled with AI! And I certainly wouldn't have bothered it writing code.

Jumps

So, what did I remember? I remembered how I used to try to write programs straightforwardly, "on the fly." Not in the sense of doing it in a day – my projects weren't that small. Back then, Kent Beck hadn't written his book on XP yet, and all I knew about design were standards like IDEF0. My approach worked, but only up to a point. And then – a wall. I didn't understand what it was, what I kept hitting. It took me several years to find the right literature and solve this puzzle. My development looked like a "jump" upwards, where, reaching a certain peak, I would invariably fall back and start all over again. I'd change my approach, my architecture, and jump a little higher. I used libraries that took on some of the work, allowing me to raise the bar a bit more. Then I decided to approach the issue systematically. In the country (1998), foreign contemporary literature began to appear, including that very book by Grady Booch, which changed everything. From the very first pages, this book precisely described my pain. And then – how to deal with it. At first, it wasn't very clear. But then it was like a third eye opened, some kind of epiphany descended. I called it a "shift" (I had several of them). My brain seemed to reconfigure, I gained an outside perspective on everything, and everything became crystal clear. The rest was technical details (well described by Beck, Larman, slightly later Fowler, and then – Uncle Bob). And they all wrote about the "architectural limit," the "cognitive barrier," the "mental capacity limit." But most importantly, I understood how to overcome this barrier or at least push it far enough away so that it wouldn't interfere. It's like reaching first cosmic velocity – you no longer fall back.

Since then, I almost never start projects from scratch. There's simply no point. You can always rebuild, rework. I no longer need to think through everything in minute detail from the very beginning. It's enough to have a good metaphor at hand and simply set goals. One after another. This is roughly how Beck describes development.

Why am I telling you all this? The thing is, AI can "jump." It jumps better and higher than anyone. For a developer who writes code in this manner, it's something unattainable. You can't jump over AI. It's the best at it. It's the champion. It can jump, do a flip at the peak, and jump a little higher. And such code is not just something you don't have to read; at a certain point, it's useless to read. A human hits their complexity wall, which they can't overcome. AI's wall is significantly further away. And that's when we hear something like "I don't write code myself anymore," "I don't look at what my agents write," "AI writes all the code on our project," "writing code by hand is so last century," etc., in yet another podcast.

Flat Software

So, what's wrong? To answer this, we need to look at the structure of a program from a certain angle. Let's take a simple example – a school problem where we need to create a calculator that evaluates arithmetic expressions like "1 + 2*3." The main load here falls on the parser. The calculation itself is trivial. Parsing the expression can be called the key feature of our solution, its core. Let's slightly complicate the core and add function calls to it. Now we can work with expressions like "1 + exp(2, 3)." And this is where the magic happens: without changing the core, we can add an almost unlimited number of functions to our program. The codebase can become huge, millions of lines of code. Yet, its complexity will remain at the level of a school problem.

In this example, functions are a counterweight to the core, an additive part (extensions, plugins, etc.). I'm confident that most of your programs can be described in a similar way (a basic architectural pattern). The additive part, in turn, can also consist of "core" spaces, but that doesn't change the essence: the nearest complexity barrier will be with the largest core.

Now, putting it all together, it's not hard to conclude that if the jump height is sufficient to "jump over" the largest core in the program, then the previously described approach to writing code is justified. Moreover, you can write large programs this way. The VS Code core, for example, is tiny compared to its environment. Nevertheless, it lacks some features that could be considered basic for such software... Perhaps that's why?

Volume

What, not all programs are like that? Almost all, but not all programs have a small core (e.g., operating systems). Large cores are written differently. They gain weight through thousands of iterations. I would compare it to winding fishing line onto an infinitely long reel. And this is precisely where AI has a problem: it can "wind the line," but it does so in a way that it quickly gets tangled, and that very barrier begins to materialize on the horizon, taking the shape of a fifth point. Normally, before making changes, refactoring of varying complexity is performed to give the code the necessary "elasticity." And only then is the feature carefully laid on the prepared ground. It's like hanging a shelf in an apartment: you can just nail it, or you can install anchors in the wall and screw the shelf to them. It's not hard to guess which case the shelf will last longer. In some cases, more serious preparation is required, up to a complete renovation. AI does none of this; it just "nails everything." No matter how you persuade it. It's all useless. Good code usually has some reserve of elasticity – AI consumes it instantly and can only do something by virtue of that.

So, maybe it just needs to be explained, given the necessary skills? No, it knows perfectly well what refactoring is; it has read all the books about it. It just doesn't do it. It doesn't recognize the moment when it's needed. It turns out that knowing and doing are not the same thing. I'll go further, not everything can be described in words.

The story of Botvinnik (a world chess champion and, incidentally, a Doctor of Science) is very telling here. He set himself an ambitious goal: to formalize all his knowledge and experience in chess and record it in the form of algorithms. That is, to create a program that would play at his level. He failed. He couldn't formalize it, couldn't create it. It turned out to be incredibly difficult. Experience can only be partially transferred, and words alone are not enough.

Language models are rather mediocre chess players... AI needs to be trained specifically. Like AlphaGo was trained back in the day. It's the same with refactoring. AI solves problems head-on: it makes insertions, additions, scattered branches – without any attempt to preserve the expressiveness and style of the code, to keep the barrier at the same distance. No, it doesn't even try. And that's precisely why we hear from some developers that "AI is weak," that "it needs constant supervision." So, it seems there are no contradictions – both sides are right.

Everything strongly depends on the project and the development approach. In some cases, you can rely entirely on AI and only control the final result. In others, you should never leave AI alone and should double-check everything it does. Personally, I lean towards the second option, as it's important for me to identify potential problems as early as possible to reduce the cost of fixing them. But now, this control itself has a cost. On the other hand, absolute trust in AI also has a cost, and this cost can be enormous. Only time will tell who was right.

Perhaps it's not so much about how well AI writes code, but about how much complexity we can manage with its help.
