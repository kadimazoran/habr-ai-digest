About eight years ago, I decided to combine two useful things: to delve deeper into JavaScript and improve my English at the same time. I found a technical book in its original language, opened the first chapter, and started reading.

The plan seemed ideal. In reality, it looked like this: I read a sentence, encountered an unfamiliar word, opened a translator, and saved it. Two lines later, another one. Then three more. Some sentences had to be translated entirely, even though I knew almost all the words in them individually.

Dozens of words were added to my dictionary in an evening, and I barely made any progress in the book. It turned out that I was simultaneously learning programming, translating from English, and trying to memorize a bunch of new words. And I didn't enjoy any of these activities.

Not immediately, but a simple thing dawned on me: I had replaced reading with collecting unfamiliar words. The fact that I encountered a word for the first time doesn't mean I need to learn it.

Since then, I've been reading differently, and most often not even books, but regular websites: articles, documentation, news, tweets. I'll explain what has stuck with me and why it works.

What Text to Choose to Avoid Reaching for the Dictionary Every Line

There's a useful guideline: reading is comfortable when you know about 95–98% of the words in the text. That's only two to five unfamiliar words per hundred. It's not a strict rule, but it's a convenient way to estimate the difficulty of a text.

If you have to stop at every sentence, the material is likely too difficult for you right now. Don't give up on English because of this; just pick something closer to your level.

The next tip might seem obvious, but I underestimated it for a long time myself: choose a topic you already understand. If you've been doing frontend development for many years, an article about a new framework will be easier than news about economics or medicine, even if the words there are simpler. You understand the context and can guess the meaning of many terms yourself.

I also like to read several materials on the same topic consecutively. For example, follow the news about a particular tool for a week: releases, reviews, discussions, documentation. The same words start to repeat, and over time, you no longer need to translate them.

Which Words Are Actually Worth Saving

I used to have one rule: if I encountered an unfamiliar word, I saved it. Now I first ask myself: will I see this word again, and do I need it at all?

For example, suppose you come across this phrase in an article:

The new model significantly reduces inference latency.

If you regularly read about models, it's worth remembering inference and latency. This is working vocabulary; it will appear again and again. As for a rare adjective from a beautiful author's introduction, you can safely skip it if the meaning is clear without it.

This immediately brings up the main question: how can you know in advance if a word will appear again or not? Especially at the beginning, when you know the language poorly and it seems like everything is important. Two things help. First: if a word appears a second time within a couple of days, it's a good sign that it's time to save it. Second: words have frequency, and you can simply look it up. I'll show how I do this below when I get to the tools.

I also set a small limit for myself, usually three to five new words per article. There's no science behind this number; it just helps me avoid falling back into collecting flashcards. Sometimes, I don't want to save anything from an article, and that's okay.

Words Are Familiar, But the Meaning Isn't Clear

Sometimes, all the words in a sentence seem familiar, but you can't understand it. This is usually due to the structure.

Rather than fine-tuning the model, the team improved retrieval.

Here, the main thing is to recognize rather than, which means "instead of." And here:

The agent falls back to a smaller model.

It's useful to save fall back to as a whole; it means "to switch to a backup option." These three words individually don't give much meaning.

Another example:

Researchers figured out how to reduce the cost of training.

The phrase figure out here means "to understand," "to find a way."

I save these kinds of expressions as a whole. This way, my dictionary ends up with constructions that really help understand the text, and fewer random fragments.

How I Read an Article

Suppose I open a review of some new tool or a long author's post. I don't translate the very first unfamiliar word I encounter.

First, I skim the title and subtitles. I understand what the material is about and what I want to learn from it. Then, I read through and skip words that don't prevent me from grasping the meaning.

For example, I come across this sentence:

The release was delayed due to an issue in the evaluation pipeline.

If it's unclear what happened without delayed, I translate it. If I encounter evaluation pipeline frequently in my work, I save the phrase. But if an unfamiliar term is only needed for one detail, I just look up its meaning and continue reading.

A separate note on doubts. Especially at the beginning, it can happen that you finish a sentence, seem to understand it, but aren't sure. Then I highlight the whole sentence and look up the translation to check myself and not lose the thread of the article.

What I Use to Avoid Copy-Pasting Words into a Translator

I quickly got tired of switching between the article and the translator, so I read with the Web Translator extension for Chrome. You double-click a word directly on the site, and a translation appears next to it. You can save it immediately, and saved words are then highlighted when they reappear in texts. The extension is free.

For me, the most convenient part is that saved words automatically appear in the mobile app. Nothing needs to be exported or transferred: save a word in the browser, and it immediately appears on your phone. I'll talk about this a bit later.

And here's the answer to the question from the beginning: how to tell if a word is worth learning. Each word in the extension has a frequency mark: "Top 1000," "frequent," "common," or "rare." If a word is frequent, it will almost certainly appear again, and it's worth saving. If it's rare, I first think about whether I need it specifically. For a developer, something like idempotent might be more important than a hundred common words, while a random bookish word from an introduction can be skipped.

You can also select several words at once and see the translation of the entire phrase, for example, on the other hand. And when you're not sure if you understood a sentence correctly, you can highlight it entirely and check the translation, as I mentioned above.

It works with books too. Click the extension icon, go to the "Profile" tab, open settings, and select "Open PDF file." The book will open within the extension, and words in it are translated the same way as on a website.

Read Separately, Review Separately

I don't learn words while reading. I see a word, check it, save it if necessary, and keep reading.

For review, I dedicate separate 15 minutes a day in the VibeLing app. Everything I save in the extension automatically appears there, and the app itself creates an spaced repetition schedule based on the chosen workload.

There are various exercises: recall a word, write it, recognize it by ear, and others. If your goal right now is specifically reading, you can disable listening exercises and focus on recognizing words in text.

As a result, the tasks are separated: I read in the browser, and I reinforce in the app. Then, I encounter familiar words again in articles and recognize them without translation. This is perhaps the most pleasant feeling in the entire process.

What Level Can You Start At

If you currently know only a few English words, starting with long technical articles will be difficult. First, you should master the basics: word order, questions, negations, the most frequent verbs, and simple tenses. You don't need to learn all the grammar; understanding short sentences is enough.

For A1–A2 levels, small adapted texts are suitable, for example, on British Council LearnEnglish. At B1, you can already read short news, blogs, and documentation for familiar tools. At B2 and above, full articles, research papers, discussions, and books are suitable.

If you don't know where to start, you can take the English vocabulary test on VibeLing. It roughly estimates how many words you recognize. It's not an A1–C1 level exam, but it can help you figure out which texts to start with. After that, the guideline is simple: when the number of unfamiliar words in your texts noticeably decreases and you read confidently, you can move on to more challenging texts.

Try It for One Week

Choose one topic that you are genuinely interested in. It could be your tech stack, a favorite game, travel, sports, cooking, anything. Read for 10–15 minutes every day, one or two short articles. Translate only what hinders understanding the meaning, and save a few truly useful words or phrases.

Set aside another 15 minutes a day for practicing with saved words. Don't try to learn everything in a week; the goal now is to get used to the rhythm.

At the end of the week, open a similar article and test yourself. Are you recognizing familiar constructions faster? Do you reach for the translator less often? Can you retell the main idea without translation?

Eight years ago, I measured progress by the number of words I wrote down from a book. Now, it seems to me that it's much more honest to count how much interesting material I managed to read and understand.

And the best moment comes when you finish reading a text and only then notice that it was in English.
