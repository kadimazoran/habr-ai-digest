NVIDIA Rubin Ultra — Almost Like a Supercomputer

In the second half of 2027, NVIDIA is releasing Rubin™ Ultra in the Kyber NVL576 configuration — this is 144 accelerators per rack, each composed of four dies. It also offers 100 PFLOPS FP4 per package and up to 1 TB of HBM4e memory. The power consumption of such a rack will be around 600 kW, forcing data centers to completely rethink power and cooling. For comparison, a Hopper rack consumed about 40 kW.

NVLink 6 provides a GPU-to-GPU bandwidth of 3.6 TB/s per accelerator. In total, this is 260 TB/s within the entire rack. Rubin™ Ultra solidifies the concept of The Rack Is the New GPU — the rack as a unit of computation.

AMD Instinct MI500 — A Thousandfold Increase in Four Years

AMD Instinct™ MI500 is the company's flagship accelerator expected in 2027. It is built on the CDNA 6 architecture, manufactured by TSMC using a 2nm process, and uses HBM4e memory. AMD has not yet disclosed exact memory capacity specifications, but its predecessor, MI455X, already has 432 GB of HBM4, so the MI500 with HBM4e should surpass this value. AMD claims a thousandfold increase in AI performance compared to the 2023 MI300X — this figure spans three generations over four years and is measured in tokens per second.

The Helios 500 rack solution is also included: it combines MI500, Verano CPUs, and Pensando™ Como/Monza network chips. According to preliminary data, the full MegaPod UAL256 configuration will occupy three racks. It will feature 256 GPUs and 64 Verano CPUs, interconnected by UALink switches.

Concurrently, the MI430X will be released in 2027. This is an accelerator with native FP64 support for HPC, targeting scientific computing and national laboratories.

HBM4e — A Standard and a Manufacturer Race

HBM4e is becoming the standard memory for 2027 flagships. Bandwidth will be up to 3.6 TB/s, with pin speeds of 16 Gbps.

Samsung has shipped engineering samples first, while SK hynix aims for mass production in the second half of 2027. Micron states support for customizing the logic die for specific clients. NVIDIA uses HBM4e in Rubin™ Ultra, and AMD uses it in MI500. The HBM shortage will remain a problem at least until 2027–2028, but it has been announced that the production capacities of the three vendors are contracted in advance.

AMD Venice-X and Verano — CPUs for Different Task Types

In the second half of 2027, the EPYC™ Venice lineup will be completed. In addition to Venice SP7, launching in 2026, the following will be released:

  • Venice-X (SP7): 96 cores, 1152 MB L3 with 3D V-Cache, boost up to 5.15 GHz. For HPC simulations and in-memory databases;

  • Verano (SB1): 72 cores, 24-channel LPDDR5X, up to 5 GHz. A host CPU for GPU-dense AI racks, its task is to continuously feed accelerators without delays.

Verano will become the CPU foundation for the Helios 500 rack with MI500 accelerators in the same year, 2027.

Intel Diamond Rapids — A Return to the Market

Intel has announced Diamond Rapids (Xeon® 7) on the 18A-P process, with release in the second half of 2027.

Key parameters:

  • 16-channel DDR5,

  • PCIe Gen 6 support,

  • up to 256+ cores.

Manufacturing is planned on Intel's own foundry, without reliance on TSMC. This is important: while competitors are vying for capacity on TSMC's 2nm process, Intel is building its own stack. The main intrigue is that AMD enters 2027 with the launched Venice and the announced Venice-X and Verano. Intel Diamond Rapids will have to compete with both an already established platform and AMD's new offerings.

PCIe 6.0 — A New Infrastructure Standard

PCIe 6.0 is transitioning from an "exotic" category to a "standard." AMD EPYC™ Venice is the first server CPU with 128 lanes of PCIe 6.0 (64 GT/s), doubling the bandwidth from CPU to GPU compared to the previous generation. Intel Diamond Rapids is following the same path.

Without PCIe 6.0, it is physically impossible to feed new-generation GPU accelerators with data at full speed. In the consumer segment, the standard is not expected until around 2030 — it belongs entirely to servers.

CXL 3.0/3.1 — Memory-as-a-Service

2027 marks a broad commercial launch for CXL 3.0/3.1 with a sufficient software ecosystem. The technology is built on PCIe 6.0 and provides 64 GT/s with cache-coherent memory pooling between CPUs, GPUs, and external storage devices.

An application scenario for 2027: KV cache for AI inference. Modern LLMs, when working with long contexts, hit GPU memory limits: the key-value cache grows with each token and quickly fills memory. CXL allows offloading the KV cache to external DRAM with latencies incomparably lower than when working with NVMe. Penguin Solutions has already released the first serial CXL server for KV cache with 11 TB — and reports a 75% increase in GPU utilization and a twofold increase in inference throughput on real clusters.

A second scenario: in-memory databases and RAG. Vector indexes and analytical tables that do not fit into local DRAM are moved to CXL extensions without application changes. According to forecasts, the CXL market will grow a thousandfold in value by 2028.

UALink as a Threat to NVLink's Monopoly

Ultra Accelerator Link 1.0 is an open standard for GPU interconnect from a consortium of major IT giants and market technology leaders. It connects up to 1024 accelerators in one pod, compared to 576 for NVLink. The first real hardware will appear at the end of 2026, with mass deployments in 2027.

UALink potentially changes the balance of power: cloud providers will be able to mix GPUs from different vendors in the same cluster without losing bandwidth speed for the first time. For NVIDIA, this is the first real threat to its monopoly on scale-up interconnects since the advent of NVLink.

Co-Packaged Optics — Optics Inside the Chip

Standard optical modules (SFP, QSFP) are installed in a switch port from the outside. The signal travels a few centimeters over copper on the board before reaching the optics, and it is this section that consumes energy and creates losses at speeds of 800 Gbps and higher. CPO (Co-Packaged Optics) technology eliminates this copper path: the optical engine is placed directly next to the switch chip on the same substrate. The signal enters the fiber faster. As a result, power consumption per 800 Gbps port drops from 15–16 W to 6 W.

The first wave of mass adoption is expected in 2027. TSMC will start CPO production in the second half of 2026. NVIDIA is transitioning its Spectrum-X™ and Quantum-X™ switches to CPO. According to LightCounting's forecast, by 2027, CPO will account for about 30% of all 800 Gbps and 1.6 Tbps ports. And with 600 kW racks, savings on each port cease to be a trivial matter.

Conclusion

Will 2027 truly bring major changes? The prerequisites are there: PCIe 6.0 and CXL 3.0 are changing memory and I/O architecture, CPO is moving network interconnects to optics, UALink is attempting to break NVLink's monopoly, and HBM4e is opening up a new class of bandwidth for GPUs. Those planning infrastructure now should already consider these changes and lay the foundation during the design phase of new systems.
