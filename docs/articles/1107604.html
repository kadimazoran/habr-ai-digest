I imported a large batch of HEIC photos from my iPhone. It turned out that even on a modern MacBook, there's nothing to scroll them with. The built-in 'Preview' and third-party programs showed the next photo with a delay. The programs themselves also opened slowly: there was a noticeable delay between double-clicking in Finder and the photo appearing on the screen. In previous years, I would have grumbled a bit, lamented developers who forgot how to write fast code, and resigned myself to it, because back then, no one would have bothered to write their own viewer for such delays. But we live in a new era!

Three evenings, a new Fable 5.2 model with sub-agents on Opus 5.5, and behold, Bolt, a native viewer in Swift and AppKit without third-party dependencies.

In my measurements, Bolt scrolls faster than 'Preview', Quick Look, XnView MP, and ApolloOne. The closest of them, ApolloOne, shows the next photo more than twice as slowly.

Optimization went in two directions. The first is cold start: the time from double-clicking in Finder to the photo appearing on the screen. The second is switching to the next photo: the time from pressing the arrow key to a sharp frame. What matters is the delay after a single press, not the number of photos per second when holding down the arrow key. A cold start takes about 350 ms. From pressing the arrow key to the photo appearing on the screen takes 41 ms, and Bolt itself accounts for 1.2 ms of that. The remaining time, the frame travels to the screen via macOS, and it wasn't possible to shorten this path.

What kind of program is this

In terms of UX, I was inspired by IrfanView, a program familiar to many from Windows. It's been around since 1996, and its website still says: "Fast and compact (just 8 MB)". I wanted to bring a similar experience to Mac: open one photo, scroll through the entire folder, and do everything with the keyboard using a minimal number of keystrokes.

The program turned out to be very simple. Delete sends the photo to the Trash and immediately shows the next one. There's no confirmation dialog, but any file operation can be undone with ⌘Z. Numbers from 1 to 9 move photos to designated folders. Square brackets rotate photos, changing only the Orientation tag, while the pixels remain byte for byte. There's export by preset and batch operations: conversion, resizing, renaming by template. There's also a grid view that shows all photos in the folder.

Photo and data panel, I key.

Folder grid, G key.

Bolt is not a cataloging tool. It doesn't replace Lightroom and other programs that manage your photo library. It works at the folder level, and its strength lies in its simplicity. I organize my photos into folders. I believe this approach is more fault-tolerant and independent. Bolt also doesn't replace a full-fledged editor.

Now I use it every day and have set it as my default viewer.

Measurement Methodology

You can't ask a third-party program to report when it has displayed a frame. Therefore, the benchmark watches the screen. It records the screen using ScreenCaptureKit, presses keys itself, and looks for the moment the correct photo appears in the recording.

Photos: 202 pieces from my iPhone 13 archive. 193 of them have a gain map: a second image inside the HEIC that indicates where and how much to increase brightness. On a screen with high brightness range, such photos are displayed as HDR. The brightness range is how many times brighter the screen can be than a standard white. This MacBook has a range of 2.0. Below, you'll see why this is important.

An reference helps identify photos in the recording. At the end of its cycle, the benchmark goes through all displayed photos in the same program, waits 0.6 seconds on each, and remembers the stabilized image. A recorded frame is considered sharp when it has at least 0.9 of the reference details, and the brightness differs by no more than 2 levels out of 255. The preview embedded in the file, a reduced copy of the photo, doesn't meet this threshold: it's blurry.

Five programs: Bolt, ApolloOne 5.1 Trial, XnView MP 1.12.2, 'Preview' 11, and Quick Look. ApolloOne is paid; we measured its trial version. The window size for all is 1400 × 870. Each program was run for three cycles in a randomized order. The computer is a MacBook Air 15″ with Apple M5 and 16 GB of RAM, macOS 27, 60 Hz screen.

The table below includes five scenarios. In three of them, the arrow key is pressed 90 times: once per second, once every 400 ms, and once every 150 ms. In the fourth, the arrow key is held down for three seconds. The first repeat comes after 500 ms, then presses occur every 83 ms, as with system auto-repeat. In the fifth, the program is closed, and the benchmark opens a file in it from Finder.

41 ms from press to screen: what depends on Bolt

It takes 41 ms from pressing the arrow key to the photo appearing on the screen, but Bolt's entry in the comparison table is 31 ms. These are different clocks. The table measures from a synthetic press to the frame in the screen recording. However, the screen recording, according to calibration, sees the frame earlier than the panel displays it. A background color change is detected 21–33 ms after the command, while according to the system's timestamp, the frame takes 40 ms to reach the screen. Therefore, the table is suitable for comparing programs, but the absolute time is only obtained by measuring from within the application.

For such a measurement, the agent embedded a probe in Bolt. The probe sends a key event to the application, and along with the photo, in the same Core Animation transaction, places a 2-point Metal layer in the window. A transaction is a package of layer changes that the application sends to WindowServer all at once. We can ask the Metal layer for its presentedTime, the moment the frame is displayed, and this time is set by the system, not the application. The measurement was done on photos already in the cache.

First, the probe helped decide what to send in the same transaction as the photo. Everything at once seems logical: the photo, the window title, the file icon in the title, and the status bar. This results in 54 ms, and the photo appears on the screen a frame later than it could have, because AppKit lays out and draws the title text and status bar within the same transaction. The agent removed them one by one and observed how the time to screen changed.

The window title is primarily to blame. Its change costs the main thread about 1 ms, but without it, the photo appears on the screen 9.5 ms earlier, and without all three, it's 13 ms earlier, almost a full frame. Therefore, in Bolt, the photo is sent in its own transaction first, and the title and status bar follow in the next one.

CATransaction.begin() CATransaction.setDisableActions(true) imageLayer.contents = newFrame.layerContents // ... CATransaction.commit() // The photo is sent to WindowServer in a separate transaction. // The title and status bar follow, at most one frame later. if window?.isVisible == true { CATransaction.flush() }
