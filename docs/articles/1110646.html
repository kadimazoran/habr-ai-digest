Startup Osmo is building the world's first "foundation model" for scent recognition. Researchers then plan to train neural networks to create new scents based on text prompts.

According to company founder Alex Wiltzko, the project's goal is to "give computers a sense of smell": "Smell molecules are essentially the only language that most living creatures on our planet can communicate with. Many species cannot interact through sound or visuals, so you could say it's Earth's most ancient language. For us, smells have incredible power: they are closely linked to memory and emotions. You only need to smell something once, and it will be remembered for a lifetime. You can catch a scent on the street and instantly be transported back to your school years."

Wiltzko claims that Osmo's model has reached the level of "human olfactory intelligence" in recent months. This means that in its ability to recognize and distinguish scents, the system is on par with the average person, and in creating fragrances, it's comparable to a novice perfumer. The company reports that its technology is already being used in the perfume industry, helping brands develop and produce new scents. Last month, Osmo stated that its AI model outperformed the in-house perfumers of one of the world's largest consumer goods companies. The founder believes that in the future, the technology could be used, for example, for threat detection at airports or for disease diagnosis.

Wiltzko founded this startup in 2023, building on his work at Google Brain, Google's former artificial intelligence research division. He operates on the same principle as many other AI startups: take a complex problem, "feed" it vast amounts of data, and see what happens. The main difficulty in working with scents, Wiltzko notes, has been the severe lack of quality data. By his estimates, before Osmo's emergence, existing databases contained fewer than 200,000 human-generated scent descriptions. Furthermore, the chemical analysis equipment needed to collect such data is expensive, and the perfume industry prefers to keep its research in olfactory science secret.

A significant portion of Osmo's work has been dedicated to creating its own dataset, which includes over 10 million human evaluations and 800 million "aroma tokens"—precise descriptions of molecules and their concentrations that make up specific scents. The company uses spectrometers and other chemical sensors operating around the clock to analyze the molecular composition of everything from fruits and flowers to food products and even internal organs. A team of about 70 specially trained specialists in the US and other countries is tasked with smelling various objects and recording their sensations, using standardized terminology to describe aromas.

This approach to scent systematization allows for the identification of unexpected parallels. Just as changing the order of words can drastically alter the meaning of a sentence, varying the composition and proportions of scent molecules can radically change how a person perceives an aroma. "The set of molecules that defines the scent of a newborn baby largely overlaps with the set of molecules characteristic of freshly made spaghetti," Wiltzko notes.

Additionally, Osmo creates its own fragrances using a robotic system the size of a school bus. It mixes ingredients according to precise formulas, producing samples for both the company's internal research and for clients. The system can develop and "print" a scent based on a text prompt, much like how ChatGPT works.

Osmo's technology is already being used in the creation of commercial perfume products, including for giants like Procter & Gamble. Furthermore, it has collaborated with the online clothing marketplace StockX: the company proposed using scent analysis to identify counterfeit sneakers, relying on differences in the chemical composition of the materials they are made from.

However, not everyone is convinced of this technology's effectiveness. Avery Gilbert, an olfactory scientist, has sharply criticized Osmo in his newsletter on the Substack platform. He claims that some of the company's marketing statements are unsubstantiated, and its patent applications focus primarily on human sensory panels and perfumers, rather than artificial intelligence. "Such a strong reliance on subjective human evaluations seems strange for a company that is based on cutting-edge AI," he wrote. In response, the founder notes that "human perception is by no means a 'crutch' for AI": "Language models learn from what people have written. Our model learns from what people perceive. Smell is essentially a human experience, so the model needs to learn from humans to connect chemistry with perception. This is what allows it to predict how people will perceive a scent—just as accurately as the human nose does."

Michael Schmuker, a former professor of neurocomputation at the University of Hertfordshire who previously advised Osmo, believes this technology is a significant achievement. Along with colleagues, he created an open-source, reverse-engineered version of Osmo's 2023 model, but it lacks the vast amounts of data the company has collected since then.

Nevertheless, according to Schmuker, even with all this scent data, Osmo might overlook certain factors that make human olfaction unique. "Smell is incredibly complex. Context plays a huge role. The same molecule can evoke completely different associations depending on the situation," he explains. "For example, the same molecules found in Camembert cheese are also present in used baby diapers. But you wouldn't confuse them—precisely because of the context."

In controlled environments, such as a perfume laboratory, the absence of context might not play a significant role. However, the situation changes if Osmo's technology, as the founder suggests, becomes an "olfactory channel" for AI-generated world models.

For this to become possible, Schmuker believes, sensor technologies must significantly improve, and the models themselves must continue to evolve. Medical diagnostics represent the most important and promising goal here. "There are many examples in history of companies that tried to tackle this mountain peak 'head-on,' but unfortunately, they failed—because it's incredibly expensive and extremely difficult," says Wiltzko. "So, we are doing it differently: laying the foundation at the foot of these mountains. We are sending expeditions, conducting reconnaissance—essentially, 'sniffing out' the entire world around us."
