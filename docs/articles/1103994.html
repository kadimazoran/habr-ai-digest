In 2026, the number of cyberattacks continues to gain momentum, and the damage from them is growing. According to Cybersecurity Ventures, global losses from cybercrime will increase from $10.5 trillion in 2025 to $12.2 trillion by 2031. The main targets for cybercriminals remain companies and organizations in healthcare, finance, energy, industry, technology, as well as government institutions. Each successful attack leads to the leakage of company and customer data, and sometimes money directly.

Binance: $6.3 Billion and a North Korean Connection

In recent days, everyone has been discussing the Binance hack, already being called the largest cyberattack on the crypto market in history. On October 30, it became known that hackers had withdrawn digital assets worth $6.3 billion from the exchange.

The company stated that while initial checks indicated a hack of an employee's laptop, which allowed the attacker to make unauthorized withdrawals, subsequent investigation revealed additional points of compromise.

The media has already attributed the attack on Binance to the North Korean cybercriminal group TraderTraitor, also known as Lazarus Group, which in 2025 stole a then-record $1.5 billion from the cryptocurrency exchange Bybit. Binance has already stated that it will fully cover user losses incurred as a result of the theft.

Canvas: 3.65 TB of Data and a Ransom

In April 2026, hackers from the cybercriminal group ShinyHunters breached the Canvas cloud-based learning management platform by Instructure. It is used by 41% of colleges and universities in North America and over 8,000 educational institutions worldwide.

The hackers claimed to have obtained 3.65 terabytes of data from 275 million Canvas platform users and demanded a ransom. They threatened to release the personal data of students and teachers on the platform if their demands were not met.

In May 2026, Instructure paid the ransom to the attackers to prevent the leak of personal data. The ransom amount was not disclosed.

Stryker: Three Weeks of Downtime and $375 Million in Losses

In March 2026, Stryker, an American medical technology company, was attacked by a cybercriminal group linked to Iran. The attackers claimed to have destroyed data on thousands of servers and mobile devices, as well as stolen information.

During an investigation conducted jointly with cybersecurity experts, including Palo Alto Networks' Unit 42, Stryker discovered that the attackers used a malicious file to execute commands that allowed them to hide their activity within the company's systems.

Experts estimated that due to the three-week downtime caused by the attack, Stryker lost approximately $375 million in revenue. Its stock plummeted by 9%, leading to a decrease in Stryker's market capitalization by nearly $6 billion.

Telus Digital: 1 Petabyte of Data via Stolen Credentials

In March 2026, hackers breached the Canadian business process outsourcing giant Telus Digital. The ShinyHunters cybercriminal group claimed responsibility for the attack and announced the theft of 1 petabyte of data from the company.

The attackers gained access using Google Cloud Platform credentials that were stolen in a data leak at Salesloft in 2025.

ShinyHunters demanded a $65 million ransom for keeping the stolen data confidential. The company recovered the compromised credentials, involved law enforcement (including the Vancouver Police Department), and external cybersecurity experts, and offered affected individuals identity theft protection services. There is no information on whether Telus agreed to pay the ransom.

Nike: 1.4 TB of Data and a Class-Action Lawsuit

In January 2026, sportswear giant Nike was hit by a ransomware attack from the cybercriminal group WorldLeaks. Hackers stole approximately 1.4 terabytes of Nike's internal data. This volume is equivalent to millions of documents, spreadsheets, and files extracted from the company's corporate systems.

Initially, Nike stated that customer data was not affected. The attackers only obtained internal corporate materials: product designs, manufacturing documentation, material specifications, and research and development files.

However, in the following months, it became known that Nike customer financial data was also among the stolen materials. This included names, email addresses, billing addresses, phone numbers, transaction details, and customer payment card information.

In April 2026, a federal class-action lawsuit was filed against Nike. The company was accused of negligence, insufficient control over third-party vendors, and untimely notification to consumers about the breach. Legal proceedings are ongoing. Nike has not disclosed the specific financial losses incurred as a result of the cyberattack.

Systemic Patterns

Supply Chains as an Entry Point. Telus Digital was not directly affected; attackers used Google Cloud Platform credentials stolen in a Salesloft breach a year earlier. Canvas is a cloud platform serving thousands of educational institutions; an attack on one provider affected millions of users. Nike is accused of insufficient control over third-party vendors. This means perimeter security is no longer a viable strategy: attacks come through partners and contractors.

Ransom as a Business Model. ShinyHunters demanded ransom from Canvas and Telus Digital. Instructure paid. There is no information on Telus's payment. This illustrates the dilemma: to pay or not to pay is a question with no universal answer, but one that each company resolves under extreme pressure.

Data as the Primary Asset. Leakage volumes are measured in terabytes and petabytes. Canvas: 3.65 TB, Telus: 1 petabyte, Nike: 1.4 TB. These are not just "leaks"; they are complete data sets that can be used for years for fraud, social engineering, and customer attacks.

Links to State Actors. The Binance attack is attributed to North Korea's Lazarus Group. The Stryker attack is attributed to a group linked to Iran. This blurs the line between cybercrime and state sponsorship, complicating both investigation and response.

Financial Consequences Extend Beyond Direct Damage. Stryker lost $375 million in revenue during three weeks of downtime, but its market capitalization fell by nearly $6 billion. Nike faced a class-action lawsuit. This means the cost of an attack is not just stolen assets or ransom, but also reputational damage, legal costs, and stock devaluation.

Security is Architecture

For those responsible for security and infrastructure, these incidents offer several practical takeaways.

Supply Chain Audits. If your company uses cloud services, third-party contractors, or open-source components, perimeter security is an illusion. An audit is needed to determine who has access to your data and what credentials are circulating within your ecosystem.

Credential Management. Telus Digital was affected due to stolen Google Cloud Platform credentials. This is a classic example of how compromising one set of keys can grant access to an entire infrastructure. Key rotation, privilege minimization, and anomaly monitoring are not options but necessities.

Incident Preparedness. Stryker lost $375 million during three weeks of downtime. This means a recovery and customer communication plan must be ready before an attack occurs. The question is not whether an incident will happen, but when and how quickly you can recover.

Legal Ramifications. Nike faced a class-action lawsuit for negligence and untimely notification. This is a reminder that security is not just a technical but also a legal responsibility.
