What Has Changed in Sales Over 40 Years

Since the late 80s, sales have been influenced by three major changes: the information revolution, the quality revolution, and the shift to consent-based selling. Rackham himself acknowledges this. Each of these has, in its own way, changed the conversation between a salesperson and a client.

The Information Revolution

In the late 80s, the only way to learn about a salesperson's product was from a brochure, which you had to order. There was nowhere for the salesperson to find out anything about the client. Long, in-depth meetings were the norm, and the question 'What does your company do?' sounded natural, as the salesperson saw nothing beyond the company sign. Today, the client already knows something about your product and may know details that the salesperson doesn't, simply because they've read the technical description and manual (this is especially noticeable in IT). The salesperson, in turn, can find out the client's turnover and the number of their salespeople in advance – and may not even need to search, as many answers are instantly provided by neural networks. Therefore, many situational questions become irrelevant – we'll get back to which ones below.

Questions about what is readily available in the public domain sound trivial and irritating today: the client immediately sees that the salesperson did not prepare for the meeting.

From this, we derive two rules.

First: do not ask about what is easily found in the public domain.

Second: assume that the client already knows something about your offering (sometimes more than you do), and start the meeting from there: what they already know, what of their own tasks they were primarily thinking about when considering products of your class or agreeing to meet with you, and what their current situation is.

The Quality Revolution

In the late 80s, patents played a significant role: if a company invented something, competitors couldn't copy it. Now, products are more or less equal, and it's extremely difficult to stand out through product or service quality. Questions that used to work well (e.g., 'Are you satisfied with your service contract?') have almost stopped working: everyone has SLAs now.

At the same time, meetings themselves have changed. Previously, an hour-long meeting was the norm, but now meetings are shorter, and if the client doesn't understand what the conversation will personally bring them, they start to get nervous. It happens that a salesperson tries to ask the client about their tasks, and the client repeatedly asks to move directly to the demonstration. This is constantly the case in IT: meetings are scheduled and started in such a way that the client expects a short demo and is not ready to discuss their business and tasks, so the questions irritate them, and they want to see what they haven't yet seen in the sent presentation or on your website as quickly as possible.

Managers in such situations don't understand what to talk about with the client if everyone has the same thing. Partners implementing systems for clients ask vendors for battle cards to prove to the client that their solution is the best.

SPIN suggests discussing the client's problematic tasks (invoicing, checking inventory, working with dealers), leaving routine aside, and showing how to quickly and correctly solve a task that currently takes more time and nerves than desired. How the client will work with the system daily will be addressed during implementation.

Consent-Based Selling

Previously, the final decision was quite often made by a specific person, the very magical decision-maker whom managers still want to reach: it was enough to talk to the person who disliked something the most, provide an economic justification, and the director would make the decision because the economics added up. Now, for any major project, a working group is assembled, and the consent of the entire group is required. Each participant must see the meaning of the changes for themselves, otherwise, the project goes into a long-term backlog. For the group to agree, one thing is needed: people must feel that the pain of leaving things as they are is greater than the hassle of implementing a new system.

Pain of 'leaving things as they are' > Hassle of implementation

The SPIN methodology helps explain the benefit to each participant in the language of their role. An IT director, for example, needs to see that the new system will relieve their department and not become another headache with customization and support. However, the decision is ultimately made by the working group, and unanimity on which option is better is rare within it. Simply presenting your product as a tool of ready-made benefit for all participants is not enough, so the salesperson needs an ally within the client's company: a person who influences the decision, sees their benefit in the project, and is willing to defend it to colleagues, explaining its usefulness to the entire company. In sales, such an ally is called a champion. Not only SPIN but also the Miller-Heiman methodology, another well-known approach to complex sales (more on this below, in the 'Tactics within Big Approaches' section), advises seeking and preparing one. It's not enough to simply be liked by the client or maintain good relationships with someone on their side: a champion doesn't appear on their own; they need to be prepared, otherwise, competitors will do it.

To prepare a champion, you first need to understand who is actually involved in the decision-making, what their roles are, what they agree on, and what they argue about in the working group. It happens that the IT director the salesperson is talking to honestly says, 'I've checked my part, but the final decision is made by the commercial department.' In this case, the IT director won't become the champion, and you need to look for an ally in the commercial department. Preparing a champion means working with them to gather arguments for discussion in the working group. The idea is that they will have four or five points in favor of your solution in the language of overall company benefit, while the opponent won't have as many counterarguments. No one in the group will object without arguments: no one wants to look foolish in front of colleagues.

It's convenient when this picture is stored directly in the deal. In SimpleOne B2B CRM, there are two tools for this. The Decision-Making Map shows who at the client's company makes the decision (center of power), who is dissatisfied with the current situation (center of dissatisfaction), and who is open to new ideas (center of receptivity). The Deal Criteria record what is important to the client and what is secondary, and where your solution is stronger or weaker on each point. These show which arguments the champion should rely on.

What Has Remained: Finding Unresolved Problems

The main idea of SPIN is universal for any communication: if you want to be heard, first talk about what concerns the other person. This means talking about the client's business, looking for problems and unresolved issues, and only then showing solutions to those that turn out to be important, without rehashing all the system's capabilities. In IT sales, the idea rests on three things: talking about the client's tasks first and only then about functionality (and showing scenarios for working with the specific client's problem situations instead of modules), finding out what lies behind their request, and understanding when the methodology is not needed at all.

Your Solution is Just a Tool for the Client's Tasks

IT salespeople often talk a lot about the system, even though the client is primarily concerned with their own tasks. Rackham himself described a scene: a salesperson lists a dozen system features, and the client asks, 'But how will this specifically help me?'

It's a fair question. We often think that an interesting demo will show the client a 'new level' in their work, but in reality, most demos impose needs on them and barely show how to address their own. Yes, any live IT product evolves, gains features, and can meet the needs of various clients, but a specific person uses a specific part of the system and solves their tasks with it: saving time, avoiding errors. You can compare it to any smartphone with 80 apps installed, but we actually use about ten.

Talking about everything also hinders sales. The client listens to features they don't need, then sees the price tag and realizes they are paying for them too. You can't just remove the unnecessary parts and halve the price; it's all part of a unified solution. So why talk about everything?

“SPIN is primarily the implementation of an idea: understand where the person hurts, help them understand the causes and consequences of that pain, help them articulate the benefit of the solution, and show exactly what will provide it. And SPIN questions are just a tool we use to solve this task.”

What Lies Behind the Client's Request

In IT, finding the client's unresolved problems is approached differently. The classic methodology is designed for cold sales, meaning meetings with clients when they have no active needs. In IT, most deals start with lead generation. Cold sales require a lot of effort, so many companies invest in lead generation, and the client often comes on their own, already with a request. It seems that the client's need is already formed, and all that's left is to show a solution for it, but this is precisely where the task for SPIN appears, which many miss. For example, a company writes: 'We need to invoice buyers faster.' The salesperson replies that they've come to the right place and shows auto-filling or integration with some service. It's all logical. However, behind this requirement might be something completely different: preventing discounts from reaching the manager that their salespeople can give themselves, or speeding up data retrieval so it doesn't contradict warehouse records. Therefore, before demonstrating the system, it's worth going through a short checklist of questions:

  1. How does your process look now?

  2. Where do delays usually occur?

  3. Who feels this most acutely and why?

  4. Are there any rules that can be introduced?

  5. What metrics do you track daily?

  6. Which of these are worse in accuracy and timeliness than you'd like?

  7. What does this affect and how exactly?

Often, the client themselves doesn't fully understand what they need, and then the salesperson's task is to help them articulate it. For example, someone comes to a meeting saying, 'We have a standard sales process, show us what fields, statuses, and reports you have in your deal.' They are convinced they need 'all marketing tools,' and when asked which ones, they reply, 'Well, all of them: email campaigns, campaign results.' This is available in any system, and demonstrating it will be useless until it becomes clear what the client is actually evaluating: how the system looks or how it will solve their tasks. Otherwise, the sale turns from a discussion of a business solution into trying on clothes in a chain store: 'like it – don't like it.' Therefore, the conversation should be shifted to their business: 'You ran a promotion or advertising campaign, but the return is unclear. How do you decide what to change next, based on what data? Do you have enough of it? What data do you need?' And only then show how to get this data from the system.

When the Methodology is Not Needed

Sometimes, SPIN is not needed at all when there is simply no task for it in a particular sale. If the client has already analyzed their tasks, prioritized them, allocated a budget, and asks to see specific scenarios for working with your solution, and you understand exactly what it will provide them, all that remains is to show that you have a solution for the formulated task. This can happen, for example, when supplying licenses for a popular product to a corporate client who has announced a tender for them: the product cannot be customized for the client, and the salesperson is left to boost loyalty and improve relationships with those who are looking at other systems.

There is a task for SPIN if you don't fully understand what the client wants to achieve, why, and for what purpose. As practice shows, clients often don't understand this themselves, even when they come with a ready-made technical specification. In such cases, the salesperson is left to decide what to ask the client during the meeting and what to find out in advance. This is the subject of the next section.

AI Changes the First Letter of SPIN

The first letter of the methodology denotes situational questions, i.e., questions about how things are currently set up with the client. Previously, you couldn't start a conversation without them, but today, the salesperson can find some answers before the meeting: what the company does, its turnover and number of salespeople, the situation in its market, and even the company's future goals and priorities. With AI, gathering this is even easier, leading to the conclusion that situational questions are no longer needed in meetings, leaving only problematic and implication questions. This is only half true.

What You Can Find Out Before the Meeting

The line is drawn between facts about the company and how work is organized within it. The client's turnover or the number of their salespeople can already be found online. But if you are selling a CRM system that automates reporting, you need to know something else: what reports the client currently prepares, how much time it takes, and what the process looks like. Without these answers, it's easy to miss the mark. If the data in the report is inaccurate (e.g., it's compiled from data from other systems), then a system that compiles it faster won't solve the client's problem.

“Some people write that situational questions can be reduced. But how can I not ask what reports you use if I want to find out which ones are prepared too slowly or with inaccurate data?”

How Many Questions to Ask in a Meeting

The number of questions itself doesn't solve anything. Implication questions are needed to understand the details of the problem, and if the details are already clear, there's simply no need to ask them. It happens that the client tells everything in response to a simple question like 'How is this process organized for you now?': work is slow, there are no results, the budget is insufficient. Then the salesperson only needs to clarify a couple of details and offer a solution for these problems. According to the methodology, they asked almost no questions, but they fulfilled the methodology's task: understood the client's problem and made an offer for it.

How to Divide Preparation with a Neural Network

Since company facts can be gathered in advance, it's convenient to divide the preparation. The neural network gets everything that is publicly available, while the salesperson leaves questions about how work is organized at the client's for the meeting. The neural network can also help with these: it can draft questions that will then need to be adapted to the client's answers. Below is an example of a prompt that can be copied into any neural network and have the text in square brackets replaced with your own data:

Prepare a briefing for the first meeting with the company [company name, website].

1. Based on open sources: what the company does, key products and markets, turnover and number of employees, notable news from the last six months. Provide a link to the source for each fact.

2. We sell [product and the tasks it solves]. Suggest 5-7 questions about how [process] is currently organized in this company. Only those for which there is no answer in open sources.

The briefing should be double-checked against the links: a neural network can confidently make mistakes with numbers, and mentioning the wrong turnover in a meeting is worse than not mentioning any at all.

Summary

In 38 years, Rackham's methodology has changed: many situational questions have become unnecessary. The client learns about the product themselves, and the salesperson can also find out a lot about the client in advance, making the question 'What does your company do?' sound like an admission of not preparing for the meeting. Questions about how work is organized at the client's remain: what reports they prepare, where delays occur, who feels it most acutely. Without understanding the client's real problems and tasks, it's difficult to show how your solution will be useful to them. Therefore, the main idea of the methodology has survived until 2026 unchanged: first, understand where the person hurts, and only then show what will relieve that pain. A good doctor doesn't burst into the room shouting, 'I have an excellent medicine for you, it's raspberry-flavored and inexpensive.' Yet, we IT salespeople often do just that. A good doctor first diagnoses, then prescribes treatment, and SPIN suggests the same approach.
