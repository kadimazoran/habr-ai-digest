1. Limitless / Blue Origin — agent completes processing program for Inconel

According to the head of Limitless, a Blue Origin programmer started preparing the machining in Siemens NX and then handed the work over to an agent. The agent considered the remaining stock material, part geometry, available tools, machine kinematics, and fixturing. The developer claims the agent completed the program in minutes, and the part was actually machined on the equipment. 60% of the trajectories in the final program were created by the agent.

The technical aspect is interesting: the system reads CAD geometry directly, at the level of surfaces and their connections. It additionally uses a physics engine to estimate tool deflection, heating, wear, and material deformation. These are approximate estimates, not full finite element analysis.

The process engineer remains the verifier. For example, they might know about a specific machine axis issue or about workpieces from a particular supplier that require attention after rough machining.

For me, the most useful aspect here is the ability to pick up work that has already begun within a familiar CAM system, using its tools, limitations, and accumulated solutions.

Source: Limitless Labs.

2. Design-to-Plan — from 3D model and drawing to manufacturing technology

Researchers from Singapore have developed a system where specialized agents analyze geometry, read drawings, find technological rules, select operation sequences, and choose tools.

In the flange example, the system recognizes the central hole, outer hub, six mounting holes, chamfers, and threads. It then creates a machining plan, including control recommendations. The moment with the M42×1.5-6g thread is particularly illustrative. The system ambiguously matched the drawing designation with the geometry, and a human had to correct the association, specifying the outer hub. After this, planning continued.

Details like these are much more interesting to me than claims of "100% success." They show precisely where an engineer is needed. The authors tested the agents' performance on 300 test tasks. Manufacturing and acceptance of 300 parts were not conducted here. This is currently research into the preparation of technological solutions.

Source: Design-to-Plan paper.

3. SIMCON — agent redesigns a plastic part for molding

SIMCON engineer Markus Fornoff gave GPT-6 Astra access to FreeCAD, Cadmould casting simulation tools, and the OpenRadioss structural solver.

The test part was a landing gear support for a quadcopter. The agent modified wall thicknesses, ribs, radii, and draft angles. When the initial design made mold creation difficult, it separated the guide from the frame and reworked the geometry.

Next came mold filling simulation, comparison of modes, warpage assessment, and return to CAD for further changes. Cadmould AI Solver was used for rapid iteration, and the 3D-F numerical solver for refinement. Two examples of mechanical loading were then added: latch pressing and landing impact.

The strength and durability of this part have not yet been proven. The author explicitly states that validation of calculations and physical tests are needed. But the experiment itself is interesting: the engineer sets the criteria and reviews intermediate results, while the agent links multiple programs into a single design refinement cycle.

Source: SIMCON and author's experiment description.

4. Carnegie Mellon — selecting laser sintering parameters based on test results

At CMU, a system based on Claude was researched for selective laser sintering of polymers. They worked with three materials: PA12 GF, PA11 Onyx, and a mixture of two PA12 powders.

Here, there is already physical feedback: samples are printed, a human conducts mechanical tests, and the results are fed back to the agent. It considers previous parameters, failures, and measured properties, then suggests settings for the next build.

According to one of the authors, for PA11 Onyx, the best measured tensile elastic modulus increased from 934 to 1571 MPa over four agent-controlled builds—an increase of approximately 68%. This is the elastic modulus, a measure of stiffness. Calling such an increase a "68% increase in strength" would be incorrect.

I like the logic itself: experience doesn't end with a report in a folder. Measurements from the previous batch are used when preparing the next one. Human testing and supervision remain part of the process.

5. UNIST — correcting 3D printing defects with operator input

UNIST researchers combined printing photos, current printer parameters, and textual observations from a human. The model analyzed the situation and adjusted settings.

In a scenario with insufficient bed heating, the warping height decreased from 1.29 mm to 0.33 mm on average across three experiments. This is a reduction of approximately 74%. In other scenarios, the results were more modest: with external airflow, warping decreased by about 44%, and with vibration, layer shifting by 35%. There is no universal defect elimination here.

The most interesting detail is the operator's role. A human could report that nearby equipment was operating and causing the printer to vibrate, or that airflow was directed at it. The system did not always identify such causes from images and equipment data.

My conclusion: human observations should be immediately included in the production agent's architecture. This example clearly shows why the agent needs operator observations. A camera sees part deformation, but a human can suggest its cause: ventilation is blowing onto the printer, or nearby equipment is causing vibrations.

Source: Journal of the Korean Society for Precision Engineering.

6. A-RAM — preparing 3D prints considering industrial robot movements

When a six-axis manipulator prints, a geometrically correct trajectory is not enough. It's necessary to check if the robot can execute it and how its joints will move.

In A-RAM, a language model analyzes the task and constraints, while specialized tools check slicing, part placement, kinematic reachability, and motion characteristics.

In one comparison, the calculated execution time for the plan was reduced from 1900 to 1138 seconds — by 40.1%. Different permissible infill patterns were compared, with the worst considered option taken as the baseline.

In another studied set, the maximum jerk of the sixth joint was reduced to 53.5% relative to the least successful permissible candidate.

This is not proof that the agent accelerates all robotic printing by 40%. But it's a good example of why preparation must be done for a specific geometry and a specific robot. A beautiful nozzle path doesn't guarantee successful movements of the entire machine.

Source: A-RAM research.

7. Peak Energy — finding the causes of cracks in the current busbar of a battery module

Peak Energy develops sodium-ion batteries for power systems. In the published case, an engineer investigated a current busbar that could crack due to repeated thermal expansion and contraction.

This requires linking two simulations. First, determine the temperature field under operating currents, then transfer it to a mechanical model and observe the resulting stresses.

According to the engineer, the thermal part was consistent with real tests. This is a significant detail: at least the first stage of the model was verified by measurements.

His estimate of labor costs: calculations that would have taken him one to two weeks independently can be completed in one to two days with Cosmon. This is a personal estimate, not an independent assessment of the entire team's productivity.

Even more interesting is the organizational effect. According to the engineer, some calculations were previously skipped entirely or sent to specialists for queuing. Now, simpler tasks can be handled by generalist engineers themselves.

Source: Interview with Peak Energy engineer.

8. Frore Systems — calculating liquid cooling for GPUs

A Frore Systems engineer discussed using Cosmon in the development of LiquidJet—cooling plates for data center chips.

The work involves building computational meshes, simulating flow and heat transfer, changing boundary conditions, and processing results. After geometry changes, part of this process needs to be repeated.

The agent helps automate preparation, run multiple studies in parallel, and generate tasks for the compute cluster. The latter point is particularly crucial. Engineer's time is also spent on scripts, submitting jobs, configurations, and data transfer between stages. All of this is also part of the calculation cost.

There is no measured acceleration in the interview. However, there is a description of its application in the engineer's current work and understandable operations that are being offloaded to the system.

My conclusion: when evaluating such a tool, the entire workflow from question to verified result must be considered. The solver's runtime is only one part of it.

Source: Interview with Frore Systems engineer.

9. Dexory — reducing temperature by changing radiator orientation

According to Dexory's report, the SimScale agent found a radiator installation orientation in 20-30 minutes that resulted in a calculated temperature 12°C lower. This refers to the result of exploring options, not a confirmed measurement of a finished device.

The team also uses the agent to configure complex assemblies: one section of the robot tower contains 198 contacts and 192 bolted connectors.

The engineer honestly noted: a simple, single calculation is sometimes faster to do manually. The advantage appears in repetitive configurations and parallel tasks.

Source: Dexory report in SimScale recap.

10. CoLab + SimScale — a design remark turns into a modification and recalculation

In a demonstration, an engineer confirms a recommendation to thicken the part's ribs. The agent then finds the relevant surfaces, modifies the geometry, and repeats the compression calculation.

As a result, the equivalent von Mises stresses and maximum displacement decrease. Numerical values of the improvement are not provided in the recap.

What's interesting to me here is the task transfer between tools: a remark from design review goes through human approval and ends with a computational check of the modification.

Status: demonstration of a workflow. Physical tests of the modified part are not shown in this source.

After examples like these, I have a very practical question: what part of our engineering work can already be handed over to an agent, and how can the result be verified? Program preparation? Parameter iteration? Repetitive calculations? Defect cause analysis?

I would start implementing AI agents with a specific task where engineers regularly lose time: program preparation, parameter selection, repetitive calculations, or defect root cause analysis. First, document how the process works today: how many hours it takes, what data is needed, and where human decision-making is required. Then, integrate the agent and compare the results based on the same criteria.

For me, a successful pilot ends with a part that passed inspection, a calculation confirmed by tests, or a task that now takes two hours instead of two days. After that, there are grounds to move forward.
