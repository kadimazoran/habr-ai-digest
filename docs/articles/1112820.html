Screenshots of interfaces generated by neural networks from major brands raise a logical question: have companies with billion-dollar budgets run out of designers and testers? (reasons below)

Unfortunately, in many cases, this is not far from the truth. The problem isn't that AI 'can't' draw buttons. The problem is that the interface production process has been rebuilt around AI capabilities, not user needs.

The question is whether the brand itself understands this? Apparently, sometimes not.

1. AI Produces "Output," Not "Outcome"

The key difference was formulated by Cathy Dill from Stripe: "AI generates a screen in ten seconds, but it doesn't know if that moment deserves confetti."

The model provides "output" – layout, components, states. But it is not responsible for the "result" – a person's ability to complete a task. In the pursuit of speed, the success metric becomes the number of screens produced, not whether they work. This is how "zombie UI" is born: there's a shell, but no life in it.

2. AI Has No Taste, and the Process Has No Closed Loop

Generative models are statistical machines. Without clear direction, they gravitate towards the most frequent patterns from the training data: gradients, glass, six identical cards in a row, characteristic rounded corners, and an overload of details. This is not a bug; it's the default.

Worse still: the generation process remains a one-off. An agent creates a mockup, a human looks at it, manually corrects obvious errors, and in the next session, the model repeats the same mistakes. The "generate -> criticize -> correct -> re-verify" loop is not closed, so the "slop" is reproduced again and again.

3. It's a Process Problem, Not a Tool Problem

It's tempting to blame Claude or GPT for everything, but the root is deeper. Anthropic itself warns: Claude has a "persistent default style," and now it's creeping in everywhere. The default is not a tool malfunction; it's its normal behavior when not overridden.

Someone in the room simply doesn't have a designer to catch the "sameness." Someone doesn't have the time or budget. As a result, what "looks normal and works" according to someone who doesn't have to understand design goes into production.

4. Economic Pressure and Reduction of "Expensive" Roles

This is the most pragmatic and unpleasant reason. Demand for testers has fallen by 55% in a year, and UI designer positions have also been heavily impacted by AI. The business logic is simple: why keep a UX researcher and a QA engineer if "AI will do everything"?

Some companies genuinely hope that neural networks will allow them to "work with an optimized team." The problem is that AI takes on the production of artifacts but not the responsibility for quality. Testing, which used to catch such interfaces, has disappeared with the people, and automatic linters and screenshot tests don't catch semantic emptiness.

What to Do About It (Briefly)

We need people who understand the difference between "the screen exists" and "a person can use it." And there are fewer and fewer such people.

AI is excellent at accelerating production. But if you remove those who were responsible for meaning from the process, speed simply helps to produce emptiness faster.
