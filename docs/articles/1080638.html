What is a Dashboard Anyway?

A dashboard is one of the most convenient ways to handle regularly recurring questions. A manager looks at plan execution daily, a financial controller monitors accounts receivable, and the HR team tracks staffing levels and hiring dynamics. For these scenarios, metrics, data composition, update frequency, and presentation methods are predefined.

This can be summarized as: a dashboard is a set of questions the organization has decided to answer in advance.

However, work processes rarely consist solely of recurring questions. They constantly involve clarifications related to a specific task, document, employee, or counterparty:

  • Has payment for a specific contract been confirmed;

  • What are the details for a particular counterparty;

  • Until what date is the employee on vacation;

  • Is there a current balance for the required account;

  • Does the document status in 1C match the task status in the workflow;

  • Why does the amount in the request differ from the amount processed in the accounting system.

Such questions may arise regularly across the company, but each is unique in its details. It's impossible to build a dashboard for all combinations of documents, dates, employees, accounts, and statuses: by the time it's approved and released, the original question will have lost its relevance.

Therefore, we don't consider the agent a replacement for BI. Dashboards and the agent have different application areas.

This approach aligns with the evolution of the analytics market. SAP is developing Joule Agents, Databricks has Genie, ThoughtSpot has Spotter, and Tableau has Pulse. The general idea behind these products is similar: users should be able to ask questions about data in natural language without waiting for a new report.

Why a Chatbot Doesn't Solve the Problem of Answering Questions

In a corporate environment, it's crucial to understand the source of a fact, whether the user has permission to view it, and whether the model is fabricating information. Let's break this down with an end-to-end example.

In "Pervaya Forma" (First Form), a manager is handling a payment task. The card includes the invoice number, counterparty, amount, and due date. The supplier reports that the funds have not yet arrived. The manager needs to check the situation without leaving the system or involving accounting for manual reconciliation.

They ask a question in the workspace:

Has payment for invoice #154 dated September 3rd been made? What amount is recorded in 1C?

To provide a useful answer, the agent needs to know how payments are typically structured, as well as the specific database's current data: document details, status, posting date, amount, and sometimes the associated contract or counterparty. The agent then determines what information is needed to answer and invokes the 1C access tool. In our case, this is a read-only connector that retrieves data and returns it to the workflow workspace.

The answer might look like this:

According to 1C data, payment for invoice #154 was recorded on September 5th. The amount is 248,000 rubles.

The task indicates an amount of 250,000 rubles: there is a discrepancy of 2,000 rubles, which should be clarified before closing the task.

An important part of such an answer is traceability. The user must see that the information was obtained from a specific query to 1C, not invented by the model based on similar examples.

Why You Can't Rely on the Model's Memory

A large language model is good at formulating answers, working with unstructured context, and helping choose the next step. However, it doesn't know the specifics of a particular company's 1C configuration. Furthermore, it doesn't know the account balances from this morning or how a document's status changed a few minutes ago.

If an agent answers a question about current data without accessing the source, it might use:

  • general knowledge about the subject area;

  • system documentation;

  • information from previous context;

  • a plausible assumption.

This is insufficient for work data. Even a very convincing answer doesn't become a fact just because it's stated with confidence.

During testing, we encountered this directly. In repeated scenarios, the agent sometimes answered based on available documentation instead of querying 1C. In 11 out of 12 cases, it chose this path, and the facts in two answers were incorrect.

The problem cannot be reliably solved with a request like, "Please always check the data." The model might agree with the instruction and still skip invoking the tool. Therefore, we moved the rule from a suggestion to a verification level: for questions requiring a fact from 1C, the absence of a tool invocation is considered a scenario error.

In other words, the agent should not claim to have "checked the data." It must actually retrieve it during the current session.

How the Agent Works with 1C

1C is not a single, standardized data schema that can be described once and applied everywhere. Configurations vary, containing hundreds or thousands of objects: directories, documents, information registers, accumulation registers, reports, and industry-specific customizations.

One of our financial configurations has over three thousand available objects. The HR configuration has over eight hundred. Industry-specific modules might have even more. Therefore, the approach of "pre-listing all tables and teaching the agent their names" scales poorly.

The connector dynamically retrieves 1C metadata. The agent works not with a hardcoded set of fields but with an up-to-date catalog of objects for a specific configuration. This allows for gradual addition of scenarios without tying the entire system to one version of the accounting environment.

Currently supported scenarios include:

  • searching for a counterparty card;

  • checking contracts and details;

  • searching for a document by identifier;

  • checking payment status;

  • retrieving a payslip;

  • querying registers, for example, to check balances.

A simple query by exact identifier or TIN can take about a second. Checking counterparty details usually requires one call and takes about twenty seconds, including response preparation. Complex register aggregations might require multiple queries and take longer—up to one and a half to two minutes.

If a person needs a complex data slice, the agent still needs to find the relevant objects, construct a correct query, and get the result. However, the user doesn't have to recall the specifics of the configuration, find the right section, and cross-reference data in multiple windows.

How the Agent Works Within a Process

A separate BI chat is useful for interacting with the analytical layer of data. However, process work is usually broader than just analytics.

A task has context: participants, deadlines, documents, approval history, related objects, and actions the user can take next. A question about payment doesn't arise in a vacuum. It's linked to a specific request, contract, supplier, and process stage.

Therefore, the agent is integrated into the workspace. It can use the task context and correlate data from 1C with process data. If they diverge, this isn't hidden within the model but becomes part of the answer.

For example, the agent might report:

In 1C, the payment was processed for 248,000 rubles, but the task card indicates 250,000 rubles.

According to the process data, the task is in the "Awaiting Payment" stage. Perhaps the amount needs to be adjusted or the status updated.

Such an answer doesn't replace human decision-making. It helps to quickly see the facts, understand where the discrepancy occurred, and choose the next step.

Read-Only Access is Part of the Design Solution

Questions about corporate data are almost always related to security. If the agent is given the ability to freely perform actions in the accounting system, convenience quickly turns into risk.

Therefore, in the current setup, the agent operates in read-only mode. It can request information from 1C but cannot post documents, change counterparty details, or adjust accruals. All changes go through standard processes, synchronization, and actions by employees with appropriate permissions.

This limitation doesn't hinder the primary task—helping a person quickly understand a situation. Instead, it makes the system boundaries clear.

Access rights are equally important. The agent accesses 1C under a separate user account with limited read-only privileges, and access to the agent itself is managed on the "Pervaya Forma" side. The same rule applies to the sources shown below the answer: you cannot display information in the evidence that the user doesn't have the right to access independently.

This approach addresses common criticism of naive text-to-SQL: it's not enough to teach the model to build queries. You need to predefine what actions it can perform, what data it can return, and how the user will verify the result.

Origin of the Answer

Verifiability is a central part of such a solution. Below the agent's answer, we display a "Sources" section. It records which tool was invoked, with what parameters, and what result it returned. The source is generated not by the model's text but by the execution environment based on actual actions performed.

The mechanism, simplified, looks like this:

User's question

↓

Agent selects a tool

↓

Read-only query to 1C

↓

Result saved in execution context

↓

Agent forms an answer

↓

Execution environment adds the source from the real result

This is a crucial detail. The model can write a convincing text but cannot forge an executed query and its result on its own. It can only refer to what has already been obtained during operation.

This mechanism should not be confused with model self-verification. We don't ask one generation to verify another. The limitation is built into the architecture: the source's origin is generated outside the model.

However, the guarantee has a fair boundary. The system confirms that the source is real and reproducible. It doesn't automatically prove that every sentence in the answer perfectly interprets the obtained data. In ambiguous situations, the user can still open the primary context, look at the query result, and make a decision themselves.

For a corporate assistant, this is, in our opinion, a healthier trust model than promising infallible AI. The system should not feign infallibility but give people the opportunity to verify significant facts.

What Remains for Dashboards and Reports

The introduction of an agent does not mean that reporting can be abandoned. Regulatory forms, balance sheets, tax declarations, and other documents with established preparation rules should still be generated by specialized tools. Dashboards remain a convenient tool for regular performance monitoring. Frequently used management reports should also not be replaced by conversations with an agent: a stable, repeatable scenario is best formatted once and made available to the entire team.

We believe the agent is needed where a question arises in the context of a specific situation and where no ready-made report exists. Additionally, not all complex questions can be resolved with a single query. Some business reports are implemented programmatically, and analysts don't have direct access to them. There are also tasks that require the involvement of a live expert.

We consider these normal limitations. AI saves time, but this technology is not yet omnipotent.

Conclusion

Dashboards are well-suited for answering frequent and pre-defined questions. They remain an important part of working with data. However, for questions like "Has this payment gone through?", "What is the current balance?", and "Why doesn't the amount in the task match 1C?", a separate report cannot be created, and an AI assistant helps here. It finds current information within its granted permissions, explains it in the context of the process, and shows the facts on which the answer is based.

From this, our key conclusion: in corporate systems, the value of AI is determined by whether a person can understand the origin of the answer, verify it, and use it safely in their work.
