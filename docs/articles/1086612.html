Why large language models are better understood not as an independent "artificial mind," but as a new class of human Information Tools.

We too quickly agreed on what the two letters AI stand for. "Artificial Intelligence" has long been perceived not just as a historical term, but as a near-literal description of a new entity. From this name has grown the entire modern drama of human-machine relations: does AI think, does it understand us, will it become smarter than humans, whom will it replace, and when will it start making decisions independently? But perhaps the error occurs before all these questions—in the name itself.

The term "artificial intelligence" preemptively suggests accepting the main assumption: we are dealing with a form of intelligence. After this, we inevitably begin to compare it with human intelligence. Meanwhile, large language models can be viewed in a different frame of reference—not as an emerging artificial subject, but as a human information tool, and then the familiar abbreviation takes on a different meaning:

AI — Information Tool.

This is not an attempt to deny the capabilities of modern technologies, nor a proposal to abolish the international term Artificial Intelligence. It is an attempt to more accurately define the technology's position relative to humans. Language is never neutral. A name shapes expectations, and expectations shape perception. When we say "measuring instrument," we define its function. When we say "optical instrument," we define its field of action. But the phrase "artificial intelligence" is constructed differently: it transfers one of the most complex human properties to a technological system. "Artificial" implies a human-created analogue of a natural phenomenon. "Intelligence" refers to the ability to understand, reason, form intent, choose, and comprehend. By combining these words, we have created not only a technical term but also an almost ready-made character. Hence the characteristic grammar of modern conversation: "AI decided," "AI thinks," "AI understood," "AI created," "AI recommends."

The technological tool gradually becomes the subject of a sentence—and then the subject of our perception. This problem is already being discussed by researchers. Pablo Sanguinetti in IE Insights directly writes about the categorical error supported by the term Artificial Intelligence: the confusion of tool and agent [1]. In 2026, Alan G. Fraser in European Heart Journal—Digital Health also proposed considering the anthropomorphism of AI as a separate problem of language and perception, emphasizing that LLMs are statistical tools for working with text, and real intelligence remains human [2]. Therefore, the novelty of the approach proposed here is not in the assertion itself that LLMs can be considered tools. It lies elsewhere: if the distortion begins with the deciphering of the abbreviation, it makes sense to change the deciphering itself while preserving the familiar designation. Not "Artificial Intelligence," but Information Tool.

Language Creates a Particularly Convincing Illusion

With large language models, anthropomorphism is particularly strong. The reason is understandable: humans are accustomed to considering language an external manifestation of thought. Anyone who answers a question, argues, explains, offers arguments, and continues a conversation is intuitively perceived as a thinking interlocutor. LLMs hit precisely this peculiarity of human perception. The system creates coherent text, maintains context, responds to clarifications, elaborates on theses, compares positions, and offers solutions. The external form of interaction so closely resembles human conversation that functional similarity is easily mistaken for similarity in nature. But they are not the same thing.

Research by Christine Cuskley, Rebecca Woods, and Molly Flaherty in the journal Open Mind (MIT Press) shows that the similarity between the linguistic behavior of LLMs and humans is largely functional, while the methods of result formation differ significantly. The authors formulate a fundamentally important conclusion: LLMs are not a theory of human language and cognition, but a tool that can be used for specific research tasks [3]. Therefore, it is more useful to abandon the question "how similar is the machine to a human?" and ask another: what does this system do for humans?

From Assumed Property to Observable Function

A large language model receives information, transforms its form, compares elements, identifies patterns, structures, generalizes, elaborates on a brief problem statement, forms options, and returns a new informational result to the human. This formulation sounds less impressive than "artificial intelligence," but it more accurately describes the system's practical function.

It is telling that the current OECD definition also describes an AI system through input data and the results it generates—predictions, content, recommendations, or decisions—rather than through the presence of human consciousness or independent agency [4]. Therefore, the technology can be defined by its function without first resolving the philosophical question of whether it possesses its own intelligence. This leads to a working formula: Information Tool—a technological system that allows humans to receive, transform, structure, compare, and generate information for further human action.

Why "Information," Not "Intelligent"

The formulation "intelligent tool" seems attractive but returns the original ambiguity: the adjective "intelligent" is easily perceived as a characteristic of the tool itself. "Information" is structured differently. It denotes not the internal quality of the system, but the subject of its work. A measuring instrument works with measurement. An optical instrument—with optical phenomena. A computational instrument—with calculations. An informational instrument—with information. Such a definition does not attribute human properties to the system. It describes an observable function. Moreover, the formula "information tool" is already found in academic literature concerning generative AI.

In an article by Theo J.D. Bothma and Rufus H. Gouws, published in Lexikos in 2025, GenAI is directly considered an information tool for meeting user information needs [5]. This is not identical to the reinterpretation of the AI abbreviation proposed here, but it confirms the correctness of the functional language itself. At the same time, it is not about an ordinary digital tool. Modern LLMs are significantly more complex than databases, search engines, or text editors. They can work not only with access to information but also with the form of its organization.

A New Class of Information Tool

A search engine helps find an existing information object. A database stores and retrieves information. A text editor helps change the form of already created material. A large language model does more. It can transform an unstructured problem description into a structure, compare multiple positions, identify contradictions, create variations, change the complexity level of text, expand a short idea into a system of arguments, or, conversely, condense a large body of material into a few essential points. This is precisely what makes an LLM a new class of information tool: it works not only with the quantity of information but also with its organization, presentation, and transformation. But the complexity of the tool does not turn it into an independent subject.

Information Is Not Meaning:

This is where the fundamental boundary lies. The model can generate an informational result. But the meaning of this result arises in a human context. The same answer can be useful in one situation and erroneous in another. A logically constructed solution may contradict the actual goal. A convincing text may contain a false statement. An option optimal according to given parameters may be practically or ethically unacceptable. The model works with the context provided to it. The human is inside the situation that generated that context. They know why the task was set, can change the goal itself, determine acceptable consequences, and accept or reject the result. Therefore, the tool can participate in the informational part of the solution, but this does not make it a source of human meaning.

From Competitor to Tool

If you change the definition, the entire system of relationships changes. If we are dealing with "Artificial Intelligence," humans find themselves alongside another supposed intelligence. A competition arises: who is faster, who writes better, who analyzes more accurately, who knows more, and ultimately, who will replace whom. If we are dealing with an Information Tool, the scheme becomes different: human → tool → expanded human capability. Then the question "What will AI be able to do instead of humans?" ceases to be central. It is much more important to ask: what will humans be able to do now, with such a tool? This is a fundamental shift in perspective. The technology does not become less significant. On the contrary, its scale becomes more accurately visible—without the need to first turn it into an artificial human.

The Stronger the Tool, the More Important the User

There is a paradox: the more operations a system can perform, the easier it is to conclude that humans become less necessary. But the process can be read differently. If generating options becomes cheap—the value of choice increases. If text can be obtained in seconds—the ability to distinguish persuasiveness from accuracy becomes more important. If the system offers dozens of solutions—the value shifts from producing a solution to defining the criterion. If information becomes practically unlimited—the deficit is no longer information itself, but the ability to determine its value.

A powerful information tool does not necessarily diminish the role of humans. It shifts it: from performing an operation—to setting a task; from producing an option—to choosing; from searching for information—to evaluating it; from answering—to formulating the question correctly.

Responsibility Remains Human:

Terminology is especially important where decisions have consequences. The phrase "AI decided" not only anthropomorphizes technology. It creates the possibility of blurring responsibility. An information tool can generate a recommendation, prepare a draft decision, or compare alternatives. But someone chooses whether this result will be used. Therefore, the more powerful these systems become, the more important it is not to automatically transfer grammatical subjecthood to them. Not "AI made the decision," but "the decision was made using the AI system." The difference seems small, but it returns the subject to its rightful place.

AI and AI: Two Abbreviations—One Functional Framework:

The Russian formula has an important advantage: the familiar abbreviation can be preserved without loss of meaning. AI — Information Tool. In English, the literal equivalent—Information Tool—yields a different abbreviation: IT. Therefore, for the English version of the concept, one can propose not a literal translation, but a functional addition: AI — Augmenting Instrument. This is not an established scientific term, but an author's formula, and it should be designated as such.

The word Augmenting is crucial here. It describes not the supposed internal capability of the machine, but the action of the tool relative to the human—the expansion of their capabilities. Such an idea has historical roots. As early as 1962, Douglas C. Engelbart, in his work Augmenting Human Intellect: A Conceptual Framework, considered computing means as part of a system that increases human ability to understand complex situations and find solutions [6]. As a result, two complementary formulas emerge:

AI — Information Tool—defines what the system works with.

AI — Augmenting Instrument—defines what it does for humans.

These are not literal translations of each other but two sides of a single functional model.

Not to Abolish AI, but to Stop Being Held Hostage by the Name:

There is no need to demand that the scientific and technological community abandon the term Artificial Intelligence. It is too deeply embedded in the language of science, industry, legislation, and culture. But a historically established name does not have to remain the only framework for our thinking. AI can be preserved as a technical category, and at the same time, the automatic conclusion that every system in this category represents an independent intelligence can be abandoned. And the Russian abbreviation AI can be preserved, giving it a second reading in the context of modern practice: Information Tool.

This approach does not diminish the technology's capabilities. It restores its more accurate place—not instead of humans, not as a digital double of humans, and not as a new carrier of human subjectivity, but between human intention and a significantly expanded space of informational possibilities. Perhaps, therefore, the main question of the era should be formulated differently.

Not: "What will artificial intelligence do to humans?"

But: "What will humans be able to do with the new Information Tool—and what responsibility are they willing to take for the result?"
