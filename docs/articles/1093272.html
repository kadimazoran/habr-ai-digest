🔥 AI Now Controls Computed Tomography

In standard CT, the number of projections is predetermined: the tomograph takes the entire planned set of scans and only then constructs a 3D image of the object. However, different objects may require different amounts of data.

Sometimes, additional projections do not improve the result, while increasing scan time and radiation exposure. Conversely, sometimes there isn't enough data, and the scan has to be repeated.

🐈 Imagine this: you bring a restless cat for a CT scan. The cause of the problem is already visible, the doctor has enough data for a diagnosis, but the scan continues simply because that's how the protocol is set. Why unnecessarily irradiate your beloved pet and make them endure the procedure longer than needed?

💡 Smart Engines scientists decided to change the very principle of CT scanning and developed the anytime CT approach, implemented in Smart Tomo Engine.

The controlled tomographic reconstruction method allows for evaluating the intermediate result during the scan and determining whether to continue collecting projections. If enough data has been acquired, the scan can be completed.

Is it Possible to Reduce CT Radiation Dose by 15%?

🫁 In medicine, this primarily means reducing radiation exposure for the patient.

The development was tested for pneumonia diagnosis using materials from the open COVID-CTset database, which contains clinical data from 377 patients – both healthy and with diagnosed viral pneumonia.

The study showed that the method preserves high diagnostic accuracy while using fewer X-ray images. On average, radiation exposure was reduced by 15%, and by 25% when pathology was detected early. For healthy patients, the approach also reduces the risk of needing to repeat the scan due to insufficient data.

“There are several diseases and conditions where a single CT scan is not enough: you need to observe how the patient's condition changes over time – for example, how the lungs recover after pneumonia or how a bone heals after a fracture. Since the dose from such scans accumulates, it is especially important to reduce it with each scan,” noted Marina Chukalina, Head of the Tomography Department at Smart Engines, Doctor of Physical and Mathematical Sciences.

🔬 In nanotomography, the key effect is reduced scan time.

Studying the morphology of nanostructured materials in the lab takes tens of hours. Anytime CT allows experiments to be completed earlier, significantly shortening the optimization phase of technological parameters for the structure formation process with desired properties.

The technology for reducing CT radiation dose has already been patented by Smart Engines in the USA.

However, controlling scan duration is only one way to change conventional CT. Another task is to extract more information about the object itself from tomographic data.

🔥 What If a "Gray" CT Image Isn't Enough?

Standard computed tomography shows the internal structure of an object well, but different materials can have similar X-ray densities and appear almost identical on a standard CT. Yet, sometimes it's important not only to see the object internally but also to understand what it's made of.

This is precisely the capability offered by energy-sensitive computed tomography.

It allows for the identification of areas with specific chemical characteristics – for example, hemorrhage zones within atherosclerotic plaques. Such detail helps the physician more accurately assess the nature of changes, rather than relying solely on the shape and density of the formation.

Smart Engines, in collaboration with the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (JINR), is working on data processing methods for energy-sensitive CT.

As part of this joint scientific effort, specialists are improving energy-sensitive CT algorithms: working on reconstruction quality and speed, data processing accuracy, and methods that help distinguish materials within an object and visualize their distribution on CT scans.

What this means in practice:

🔹 In medicine – more data on tissue composition and pathological changes, leading to more informative scans and a stronger basis for accurate patient assessment.
🔹 In the study of complex objects – the ability to distinguish materials that look similar on standard CT.
🔹 In materials science – more precise analysis of complex objects and the distribution of different materials within them.

And in industry, differences between materials within a single object can themselves pose a significant challenge for CT. Especially when dealing with a complex structure like a jet engine.

🚀 Can You See Inside a Jet Engine Without Disassembling It?

A jet engine is one of the most complex objects to diagnose. It contains numerous blades, channels, tubes, and other components, some of which cannot be inspected when the engine is assembled. It needs to be disassembled and then reassembled.

This process takes several working days, requires high qualifications, and can lead to assembly errors. Therefore, non-destructive testing of the entire engine is particularly important for such tasks.

However, simply placing a jet engine in a tomograph and obtaining a correct 3D model is also not feasible.

The problem is that its "hot" and "cold" sections are made of fundamentally different materials. The hot section must withstand high temperatures and overloads, so it uses dense, heat-resistant alloys. The cold section – the casing and compressor – is made from lighter materials to reduce engine weight.

For X-ray tomography, this is a challenging scenario: dense materials can almost completely absorb radiation, while light materials can become nearly transparent. This can lead to real defects being invisible, and false indications appearing where data is insufficient. The effect can be so strong that an image shows emptiness instead of a complete blade.

What can be done? 🔍

Smart Engines researchers have proposed a new non-destructive testing method combining computed tomography and AI. The engine is scanned entirely at "compromise" energies around 450–600 keV to acquire data for both dense and light components.

Then, Smart Tomo Engine algorithms compensate for scattering, polychromaticity, and noise to reconstruct an accurate digital twin of the engine.

As a result, cracks, voids, delaminations, and foreign objects can be detected in a single measurement without disassembling the unit.

🔩 What Else is Tomography Used for in Industry?

When we hear "computed tomography," we first think of medicine. But the same principle – looking inside an object without destroying it – is also needed in industry.

💡 Tomography allows for obtaining a digital 3D model of a part or an entire product and seeing what cannot be inspected from the outside.

In manufacturing, CT is used for non-destructive testing: internal cracks, pores, voids, and delaminations can be detected, part geometry can be checked, and compared against CAD models – all without cutting or disassembling the product.

Such inspection is particularly important for additive manufacturing. As 3D printing technologies continue to evolve, tomography allows for controlling the internal structure of finished parts and identifying hidden defects.

CT is also used to study new composite and biomedical materials, inspect fatigue damage, printed circuit boards, and integrated circuits.

However, the capabilities of tomography are not limited to industrial production.

🛢 In the oil and gas industry, it is used to study core samples – rock specimens extracted from wells – and examine their internal structure without destruction.

💎 And in gemology, CT helps to look inside pearls and gemstones, study their structure, and detect inclusions and internal damage.

Industrial tomographs are not just "X-ray machines for parts" but tools for quality control, defect detection, metrology, and new material research.

But more information can be obtained not only by analyzing composition or more accurate reconstruction of dense objects. In medicine, there is another challenge – to make soft tissues and structures, which are poorly distinguished by standard CT, more visible.

🔬 More Detail in Computed Tomography – Without Additional Contrast Agents

To better distinguish vessels and some tissues on standard CT, contrast agents are often used. However, their administration remains an additional intervention in the patient's body.

One way to enhance the distinguishability of soft tissues without additional contrast agents is to use a different physical mechanism for image formation.

Phase-contrast tomography works precisely on this principle. The method utilizes the phase shift of an X-ray wave as it passes through a sample and is sensitive even to small changes in the wavefront. This makes the internal structures of soft tissues more visible without additional staining.

Smart Engines is developing methods for reconstructing tomographic images from phase-contrast projections.

The initial projections are processed, phase information is restored, and then a detailed tomographic reconstruction is formed.

🏷 For example, in a study of the human olfactory bulb, this approach allowed for images with detail down to individual cells and capillaries.

However, high detail complicates further analysis: the boundaries between adjacent morphological layers can be barely discernible, and manual annotation of a large number of tomographic slices is time-consuming.

To automate this work, Smart Engines specialists, along with colleagues from the CNR Institute of Nanotechnology in Rome, have developed a method for segmenting morphological layers in phase-contrast reconstructions.

The neural network learns to work even with a small amount of annotated data. To achieve this, the team adapted the training process: they modified the data splitting method, added smoothing between adjacent slices, and used sparse training.

As a result, the Dice score on test data increased from 0.86 to 0.93.

🔍 In conclusion, phase-contrast CT provides more detailed reconstruction, and Smart Engines' computer vision helps automatically segment the desired structures.

Tomography can also work with objects that cannot be safely disassembled or even opened. Here, 3D reconstruction becomes a way to first create a digital copy and then study its contents.

📚 Can You Read a Book Without Opening It?

If an ancient scroll or book has been damaged by time, moisture, or fire, attempting to unroll or open it might cause further damage. But what if we first create its digital copy and then "unroll" it virtually?

Smart Engines scientists and the Federal Research Center "Computer Science and Control" of the Russian Academy of Sciences (FIC IUC RAS) have developed an automated system that combines X-ray tomography and machine vision algorithms.

The fragile document is placed in a tomograph, after which its digital model is constructed, and further work is done with it. The main challenge is not just to see the layers but to automatically separate them and reconstruct the text, even if the pages or coils of the scroll have fused and deformed.

💡 Researchers have succeeded in creating a fully automated virtual unrolling system that requires no manual parameter tuning. Previously, the need for constant specialist intervention was one of the main limitations of such methods.

In the future, such technology could help study paper, birch bark, and even silver scrolls, as well as ancient books that cannot be safely opened physically. Moreover, it can be useful not only for museums and researchers but also for owners of private archives and collections where it is crucial to study the content while preserving the original intact.

“We hope that our development will allow us to take a step forward in the research and preservation of cultural heritage and open up new opportunities for historians, archaeologists, and other specialists in the humanities,” said Vladimir Arlazarov, CEO of Smart Engines, Doctor of Technical Sciences.

And sometimes the problem is not that an object cannot be opened or disassembled, but that it – or the patient themselves – cannot be transported to a stationary tomograph. In such cases, the conventional CT geometry has to be abandoned.

👀 A Tomograph in a Suitcase – Myth or Reality?

Typically, a computed tomograph is a large stationary system with rigid mechanics and precisely defined imaging geometry.

Smart Engines decided to abandon this principle and created a wearable, free-positioning tomograph that fits in a suitcase, weighs less than 5 kg, and can produce a 3D X-ray image almost "freehand."

The main advantage of this approach is mobility. The tomograph can be used where stationary CT is inaccessible: in ambulances, mobile surgical units, paramedic stations, emergency zones, and remote areas.

Initial tests show that the resulting 3D images are sufficient, for example, to locate large fragments and assess their position relative to bones and other anatomical structures.

The development is based on X-SLAM technology. The operator freely moves the portable X-ray source around the object, and algorithms automatically determine its position for each frame and then reconstruct the 3D structure. Only 10–15 X-ray projections are required for reconstruction – significantly fewer than in classical CT schemes.

This allows for simultaneous reduction in scan time and radiation dose.

At the same time, Smart Engines did not focus on creating a new complex hardware platform. The system is built on ELTECH-MED X-ray technology, and the fundamentally new functionality is achieved through mathematics, computer vision, and reconstruction algorithms.

The development has already received a Russian patent, and in 2026, the Smart Engines portable tomograph project won the international "Gravity" award in the direction of breakthrough scientific research and development 🔥

How Handheld CT Works

In classical CT, the precise positions of three elements are known: the radiation source, the detector, and the object being scanned. This geometry is necessary to correctly reconstruct its 3D structure from a set of X-ray projections.

However, in an emergency, transporting a patient to a stationary tomograph is impossible. After accidents and disasters, victims may be on stretchers and untransportable.

For such scenarios, Smart Engines is developing X-SLAM – handheld computed tomography technology. The source and detector can be freely moved around the object, and Smart Engines algorithms simultaneously determine the imaging geometry and reconstruct its 3D structure.

Handheld CT places particularly stringent demands on algorithms. The result needs to be obtained quickly, and the number of X-ray projections must be limited to reduce radiation exposure. Smart Engines' optimized algorithms allow reconstruction from a small number of projections and work with low-contrast data.

The entire computational cycle is performed directly at the examination site: from processing the acquired projections to constructing the 3D reconstruction. The handheld tomograph does not require an external computing center, so it can be used even where there is no stable network access.

Based on X-SLAM, Smart Engines has already created a prototype handheld tomograph. The technology has made CT mobile and autonomous, allowing low-dose scans to be performed directly where the patient is located.

At first glance, anytime CT, energy-sensitive and phase-contrast CT, virtual unrolling, and X-SLAM solve completely different problems. But all these developments are united by one idea: the capabilities of a tomograph can be expanded not only through new hardware but also through software.

CT System Functionality Can Be Expanded Without Replacing Equipment

All these approaches are implemented in Smart Tomo Engine – a software suite for reconstruction, analysis, and 3D visualization of tomographic data.

The technology performs 3D reconstruction of objects from a set of X-ray projections. The quality of the resulting image allows a physician to easily make a diagnosis, and a technologist to build an expert assessment of the process quality.

Smart Tomo Engine handles the key stages of working with tomographic data:

🔹 performs automatic calibration;
🔹 selects the reconstruction algorithm for the task, aiming to reduce the appearance of ring, star, cupping, and other artifacts;
🔹 processes large volumes of data;
🔹 works with high-resolution images;
🔹 reconstructs up to 5 times faster without loss of quality;
🔹 allows working with a selected region of interest to avoid wasting resources on the entire volume.

For objects extending beyond the detector's field of view, the patented FOVEA algorithm is used.

The reconstruction result can be visualized layer by layer in 3D, used in automated analysis systems and for model calculations, and also transmitted for 3D printing.

Smart Tomo Engine runs on CPU and GPU, supports Windows, Linux, and Russian operating systems. The API for C, C++, C#, Java, Python, and PHP allows integrating STE into existing infrastructure or using it when creating new hardware-software systems.
