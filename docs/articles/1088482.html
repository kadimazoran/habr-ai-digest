Hello everyone! This is Andrey, Head of Client Security Products at Selectel. Corporate IT is transitioning to a service model, but simultaneously consuming more and more energy. According to a Uptime Institute study, the share of workloads on third-party sites has for the first time exceeded the share on proprietary ones, and the average rack power density has surpassed 11 kW. This study describes an industry that continues to grow and invest, but under increasingly stringent constraints: rising electricity costs, declining reliability of local power grids, supply chain disruptions, and staff shortages.

Over 800 data center owners and operators participated in the survey, with more than half from North America and Europe. Almost every fourth respondent works with external IT infrastructure providers.

Third-Party Sites Lead for the First Time

Uptime Institute annually surveys corporate respondents about the proportion of their IT workloads supported by their own (on-premise) infrastructure versus third-party capacity (IaaS). In 2026, third-party sites took the lead for the first time: 46% compared to 44% in corporate data centers. (The remaining 10% of workloads are kept by respondents in server racks without dedicated server rooms).

Experts predict that by 2028, the share of workloads in private server rooms will remain at the same level, while the share of third-party sites will grow to 48% due to a reduction in the number of small server rooms and standalone racks. Third-party services in this analysis include colocation, IaaS, public cloud, SaaS, and hosting. Edge and micro-data centers are considered enterprise-owned: the questionnaire does not specify their ownership or operating model. However, if they were classified as "third-party sites," the gap would be even more significant.

Server rooms and standalone racks, for example, in an office, are not considered data centers by researchers for a clear reason – they lack the key characteristics of such specialized facilities. What is surprising is that their share is decreasing slower than one might expect with the shift of workloads online.

In a separate article, we discussed in detail how data centers are structured and what tasks they perform. You will learn about important aspects to consider when looking for a suitable data center, as well as about security and legislation in this area.

Interestingly, not long ago, owning a data center was considered a sign of mature IT infrastructure. In 2026, it often becomes a sign that a company has taken on someone else's work: it requires predicting in advance what capacity will be needed in two to three years, which hardware will be relevant, and how much electricity it will require.

Rack Density is Growing, But Not Quite as Headlines Suggest

If many news sources are to be believed, AI infrastructure is pushing densities to 120 kW per rack and above. For example, racks on the NVIDIA eg; Vera Rubin rade; platform can consume 190–230 kW. However, the reality is more modest: most data centers and servers operate at significantly lower capacities, and ultra-high-density facilities are still few in the industry.

The average density of the most typical racks in 2026 exceeded 11 kW for the first time. There are two reasons: the gradual transition to more powerful equipment and a small number of new high-density data centers with racks over 30 kW, which pull the average value up. Without these facilities, the average density is 7.8 kW, compared to 7.5 kW in 2025.

An important detail: 11 kW is already a quite common indicator and can hardly be called solely a result of the "AI boom." Today, a rack reaches this density through regular hardware upgrades, even without a single accelerator.

Let's calculate for a 42U rack filled with general-purpose 2U servers – that's 20+ machines. In the AMD EPYC 9005 series, the TDP of top processors reaches 500 W, meaning two processors in one server already account for about a kilowatt just for the CPU. Even the mid-range has 300+ W per socket.

Let's take a conservative estimate: 700 W per server entirely – including memory, storage, and network cards. Twenty such servers provide a peak load of 14 kW per rack. Even with a concurrency factor of 0.7, this is a 10 kW rack. And we haven't even installed a single GPU in it: with top processors and full configuration, the peak budget for the same rack goes over 25 kW. Therefore, the question "where to put a GPU server?" quickly turns into the question "where to get another few tens of kilowatts?" In a private data center, solving this problem involves redesigning the engineering infrastructure.

24% of respondents report operating racks of 30 kW or more, compared to 19% a year earlier. The growth was mainly in the range above 50 kW, and some respondents are deploying GPU servers in racks over 100 kW.

The trend will continue as hardware is modernized. New servers offer more computing power and better energy efficiency, but to fully realize this potential, the supplied power must be proportionally increased, notes Uptime Institute.

This is most evident on GPU platforms. NVIDIA eg; HGX rade; B300 is the senior card of the Blackwell Ultra generation with a TDP of up to 1100 W. An HGX system with eight such cards consumes about 14.5 kW according to specifications, and at peak, depending on the configuration and workload profile, we see up to 18 kW per chassis. NVIDIA's solution examples suggest placing four such systems in one rack – that's already about 56 kW of supplied power.

Shortening Upgrade Cycles

The classic procurement model looks something like this: buy equipment → use for several years → depreciate → replace. A server can be purchased today with a five-year lifecycle, but the regular release of new AI models changes the usual hardware depreciation path for companies that want to run the latest models under their own control. An example is Kimi K3 from Moonshot AI, whose weights were published on July 27, 2026: an MoE architecture with 2.8 trillion parameters, 16 active experts out of 896 per token, and a weight size in MXFP4 format of about 1.4–1.6 TB.

Even with a high-performance server equipped with eight H200 cards, interconnected via NVLink into a single memory pool, their total VRAM would be around 1.12 TB. This is insufficient to load Kimi K3 weights into memory, let alone provide headroom for KV cache and context. To run such a model, you would need either distributed load across multiple servers or even more aggressive quantization (e.g., NVFP4), which reduces accuracy.

What does this mean for business? Companies implementing AI want to work with the most powerful models and do so in an isolated environment, without sending data externally. But models are growing faster than servers are becoming obsolete: hardware purchased for inference at the beginning of 2026 will not be able to run the next generation of models. A server that was planned for several years of depreciation must be replaced within a year – and these are expenses that were not in the budget.

This leads to the logic of switching to a service rental model, where you can always use up-to-date hardware. The Enterprise-grade Data Center service addresses precisely what is hardest to scale in-house. Equipment is housed in Tier III data centers, the IT infrastructure provider is financially responsible for server operability, and isolation at the hardware level and security control are the same as in a private data center.

Fewer Outages, But Each is More Expensive

The outage situation has been improving for the sixth consecutive year: the percentage of respondents who experienced an outage in the last three years has decreased by three percent. Approximately one in ten outages is serious or severe. However, Uptime Institute warns against complacency. Factors causing outages are becoming more relevant: limited and unstable electricity availability, reliability of local power grids, supply chain disruptions, and extreme weather.

The flip side of improvement is the rising cost of outages. Organizations are increasingly dependent on digital infrastructure, so the financial consequences of failures are more severe than before. 71% of respondents reported that their largest outage cost at least $100,000 – compared to 57% a year earlier.

Conclusion

If a company does not profit from operating data centers, its own infrastructure should answer the question: what business problem does it solve better than an external provider? IT infrastructure is increasingly moving from private data centers to third-party sites due to several factors: rack density is increasing, outages are becoming more expensive, and there is a shortage of qualified personnel, as well as electricity in private server rooms.

Each of these limitations is well addressed by professional industry players, IT infrastructure providers, due to economies of scale:

  • supplied power is not increased on demand but planned for years, considering development and electricity availability, from the design stage of a professional data center;

  • it is easier for companies for whom it is their core business to find, train, and retain electrical engineers and operational staff;

  • the hardware upgrade cycle does not lead to warehouse expansion due to a large number of clients using IT infrastructure of vastly different performance levels.

Renting is not necessarily cheaper than owning hardware, but it is cheaper than owning the risks that come with hardware. A company gets computing power but not another complex facility to manage. Hence the Uptime Institute's forecast: the share of private data centers will not grow by 2028 and will remain around 44%, while the share of external data center and IT infrastructure providers will rise to 48%.
