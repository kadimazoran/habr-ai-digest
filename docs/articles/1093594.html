Look at what you've generated

First, open your project and go through the checklist:

  1. How does it start? There should be a single, clear command or button that brings the service up from scratch.

  2. What database is inside? SQLite is just a file next to the project; it requires nothing and lives anywhere. PostgreSQL or MySQL are already separate services that need memory, disk space, and regular backups.

  3. Is there a Dockerfile? This is a text file with a build recipe, specifying the base system, libraries to install, and the command to run. It's used to build a container – a self-sufficient box where your service resides with all its dependencies.

  4. How many people will actually use it? Answer this question honestly, perhaps just to yourself. Even if the project is super cool, it needs time for promotion.

  5. What happens if the service goes down overnight? If you can just restart it in the morning, the cheapest option in this article will suffice. If a client calls or bad reviews come in – think harder.

  6. Who will administer it? If you are the system administrator, choose an option that forgives mistakes. Otherwise, consider getting help from an experienced friend or specialized personnel.

Next, we'll go through the hosting options. I've listed them from cheapest to most expensive.

Zero rubles, but free cheese is only in a mousetrap

Option 1. Home machine and a tunnel

If you have a mini-computer, an old laptop, or a Raspberry Pi gathering dust at home, you can run your service on it. It can be exposed externally using Cloudflare Tunnel (free, but may have issues in Russia), xTunnel (free HTTP tunnel with a random address, up to 100 requests/min; paid for continuous use), or TunnelHunt (free SSH tunnel, up to 1 GB of traffic per month and sessions up to four hours; more is paid). After this, your local service on localhost:3000 becomes accessible from the outside via HTTPS.

I can't say whether it's worth paying for it, as I haven't used those paid options. If you are a user of these services, please share your experience in the comments.

It's quite simple to set up. I'll explain using Cloudflare Tunnel as an example. You install the Cloudflared program on your home machine, it establishes an encrypted channel to the Cloudflare network, and your service gets an HTTPS address even if you have a private IP.

The downsides are that if the power goes out, the service will die, and a home internet connection can struggle with outgoing traffic. In my opinion, this option is best for demos, webhooks, and testing an idea.

Option 2. Free cloud

The second free option is the free tier on foreign platforms like Render or Oracle. There are many downsides, for example, on Render, a free service goes to sleep after fifteen minutes of inactivity and takes about forty seconds to wake up, and a free PostgreSQL instance is deleted after thirty days along with its data. Oracle used to offer Always Free with four ARM cores and 24 GB of memory for years, but in June 2026, they reduced the limits to 2 OCPU and 12 GB RAM.

Registration often requires a bank card, and a Russian one won't work right now. This freebie is theoretical for users in Russia, but I couldn't skip mentioning it.

Also, I kept wondering where to remind you about PERSONAL DATA PROCESSING... I'll write it here: if your service will be used by Russians who register and provide personal data (full name, phone number, address, passport details, or email), you need to record, process, and store everything within Russia. Also, according to Federal Law 152-FZ, they must give consent, and you must register as an operator. The fines are substantial, so I wouldn't risk it.

From 170 rubles per month – PaaS

The next step up is PaaS (Platform as a Service) platforms. They take your repository, build, deploy, and restart everything themselves, and you don't see the servers at all. It's precisely for this "invisibility" that you pay several times more than for the same resources directly, although the initial costs are negligible, which is why I'm mentioning this before VPS.

Railway charges $5 per month for its Hobby plan, which is provided as consumption credits, and then the platform calculates infrastructure costs per minute – $20 per virtual core and $10 per gigabyte of RAM per month. But this is an overseas service, so user registration is immediately out of the question.

There are also such platforms in Russia. For example, Amvera offers a trial plan for 170 rubles per month, providing 0.1 GB RAM, 2 GB storage, and up to 0.1 vCPU. For 290 rubles, you get 0.5 GB RAM and 5 GB disk, and for 490 rubles – 1 GB RAM and 7 GB. They support Python, Node.js, Go, Java, .NET, and Docker. Applications are updated via Git, and PostgreSQL and other databases can be ordered separately as managed services. Payment is based on container runtime.

A similar scheme is offered by Timeweb Cloud App Platform – you can connect your repository from GitHub, GitLab, or Bitbucket, and the platform will automatically detect the environment, install dependencies, and launch the application. It includes auto-deploy, a free technical domain, and SSL. For frontend applications, 10,000 incoming requests cost 4.95 rubles, and each application gets 2 GB of NVMe. The backend is deployed on a separate cloud server.

Moving on.

From 149 to 4999 rubles – VPS

VPS/VDS is a Virtual Private Server. You get a machine with its own IP, dedicated memory and disk space, connect to it via SSH, and install your application. I'll provide specifics for RUVDS because I'm familiar with it and not trying to sell you anything. If you choose another hoster, just pay attention to their infrastructure.

From 19,845 rubles – Bare Metal

Bare Metal is a physical server in a rack that you get without a hypervisor or shared resources. Here, you can install your own OS, hypervisor, or even partition the machine into a dozen virtual machines. For pricing, I'll again refer to RUVDS – they recently added this service.

The cheapest bare metal currently starts at 19,845 rubles per month, or 661.5 rubles per day. You can rent a server for even a single day – the tariff also includes a gigabit connection and up to 10 TB of traffic per day.

You should consider moving to bare metal when a VPS is no longer sufficient. For example, when the database constantly loads the disk and predictable storage performance is required, or when 64–128 GB of RAM is needed by a single machine constantly. Generally, it's chosen when the service is already generating revenue, and the load is predictable and round-the-clock.

The most expensive are the large clouds

Cloud giants sell not just servers but an ecosystem with managed databases, object storage, queues, and auto-scaling. Accessing foreign clouds with a Russian card is impossible, so a gray payment scheme is needed – personal data processing is out of the question here. There are many options in Russia – Yandex Cloud, VK Cloud, Reg.ru Cloud, ITGLOBAL.COM, Selectel, Cloud.ru, and even MegaFon Cloud (among others).

It's important to note that in the cloud, CPU, memory, disk, public IP, and outgoing traffic are billed separately. To avoid singling out any of the listed companies, I'll provide generalized figures. With one Russian provider, two 100% vCPUs cost 1.24 rubles per hour, and a gigabyte of RAM is 0.33 rubles. If you take 2 vCPUs and 4 GB of memory for 720 hours, it comes to approximately 2736 rubles per month for CPU and RAM alone. Disk and public IP are billed additionally.

With another provider, a virtual machine with 2 vCPUs and 4 GB RAM costs 2.232 rubles per hour, meaning 1607 rubles for 30 days just for computation. Disk and external bandwidth are billed separately, so the final bill is higher.

The most critical factor in this option is traffic. If the service distributes a terabyte of images, archives, or videos in a month, your bill will increase by approximately 1500 rubles just for data transfer, excluding storage and bucket requests. This amount becomes more significant with larger volumes, so calculate your outgoing traffic costs before moving to the cloud.

How to choose

I've compiled all the options and steps into a list and a table:

  1. Verify that the service starts on a clean machine with a single command, and ask an AI to write a Dockerfile if one doesn't exist. This will eliminate half of future problems on any platform.

  2. If you have no users yet or are testing an idea, deploy locally and use a tunnel // use free-tier if you have a foreign bank card.

  3. If the project uses external AI via API, it doesn't need a dedicated GPU. Only consider a graphics card if you're running the model locally.

  4. If you get your first real users, move to a VPS for 1500-2000 rubles and install Coolify.

  5. After a few days, check `free -h`, `docker stats`, and `df -h`. If memory is constantly low, increase RAM. If the database is slow, check `iostat`, as the disk might be the bottleneck. Choose NVMe only after observing disk load.

  6. Save Bare Metal for when there's a clear reason to migrate. Until then, it's easier to upgrade your VPS as needed.

  7. Go to the cloud when you need auto-scaling and managed services, but first, calculate the cost of outgoing traffic.

Whatever you choose, set up backups on the very first evening. An AI can generate a new service in an hour, but it can't restore your user database. And here's the summary table:
