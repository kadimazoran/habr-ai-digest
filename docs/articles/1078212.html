Does anyone really dislike robots, except for businesses?

Late last year, analysts from Qualtrics' research division assessed the effectiveness of AI systems in customer support from a service quality perspective. Experts surveyed over 20,000 respondents from 14 countries, including the US, UK, France, and Germany. It turned out that almost one in five individuals who contacted AI assistants in customer service failed to get an answer to their question or resolve their issue.

The result is quite expected – in this area, chatbots and voice assistants merely act as interactive FAQs. Step left or right, and you have to call a human operator, reaching whom can be quite a challenge. Sometimes, AI systems provide inaccurate information or even misinformation, and the client has to deal with the consequences. A prime example is the Air Canada story, where the airline's bot invented a non-existent rule and promised the passenger a discount.

As for the business motives for implementing AI operators, they are prosaic – despite all their shortcomings, robots help reduce operational costs. Last year, Salesforce replaced 4,000 support employees with AI agents. According to the company's CEO, Marc Benioff, this move reduced call center costs by 17%. Now, about 50% of the company's customer inquiries are handled by neural networks: "And I don't see this as a dystopian scenario, for me, this is reality."

In 2023, Swedish fintech Klarna also transferred the work of seven hundred support operators to a new AI system. However, in their case, such an operation hardly paid off. Within six months, the quality of service in the support department deteriorated so much that the company had to involve employees from adjacent departments – engineers, designers, and marketers – to communicate with clients to somehow rectify the situation. In 2025, the fintech company began hiring specialists in technical support again, and the company's CEO changed his perspective: "It's important that the client clearly understands: if they want to, they will always have the opportunity to talk to a live employee."

However, there's a possibility that in the near future, Klarna's CEO will change his mind again: AI systems in this field are not standing still.

Agents vs. Prejudices

According to some estimates, AI voice systems handle up to 19% of incoming calls in contact centers – up from 6% in 2024. This is not surprising overall, considering the advancements made in the last year or two. Here are a couple of their recent achievements:

Works in 'noisy' conditions. Modern AI voice systems can recognize speech against background noise, regional accents, customer interruptions, and conversations from others. This is achieved through noise filtering technologies and specially trained language models. For example, the American fast-food chain Taco Bell continues to implement such a system; it is now operational in nearly 900 restaurants. Voice agents help take orders from motorists, considering the specific menu of the location, ingredient and preparation availability, as well as temporary special offers. The technology is already showing positive results. In 2025, Intouch Insight conducted a test using mystery shoppers: they evaluated 120 orders involving AI in three fast-food chains (including Taco Bell). Such orders were processed on average 21 seconds faster. Moreover, 97% of the test participants were satisfied with the AI-assisted service.

Although there are still occasional blunders in this area. Last year, one driver tried to order "eighteen thousand cups of water" at a restaurant's drive-thru, and the system froze. Voice AI assistants do not handle non-standard situations very well, but such scenarios are gradually being addressed with stricter escalation rules.

Adapts to changing conditions. Early AI voice systems were essentially FAQ services: they recognized a query and generated a response based on a limited set of information. Now, more complex solutions are being developed, built as 'constellations' of specialized models, each responsible for a different part of user interaction. An example is the American company Hippocratic AI, which is developing AI voice agents for patient interaction. The system is based not on a single universal agent, but on a group of models: one conducts the conversation with the patient, while others monitor its work, including verifying the accuracy of medical information. A special orchestrator coordinates their work: it determines which agent should join the conversation, at what moment, and for what task, considering the conversation's context and the patient's reaction. This architecture is used in various scenarios – from preventing hospital readmissions to supporting people with chronic diseases.

New solutions in the field of AI voice assistants are gradually reducing what specialists from the Wharton School of Business termed algorithm aversion back in 2015 – this is a phenomenon reflecting people's tendency to distrust the predictions and decisions of computer systems, even if they perform no worse than humans. In the hope of overcoming this effect over time, developers are advancing new audio models for designing voice interfaces. For instance, this year OpenAI introduced GPT-Realtime-2 and GPT-Realtime-Translate, capable of conducting dialogues, processing user requests, and translating speech from over seventy languages (and responding in thirteen of them). Interestingly, the open-source community is also trying not to fall behind. Independent developers are offering many interesting projects in the field of AI telephony.

VideoSDK AI Agents

Python framework under the Apache 2.0 license for multimodal voice agents. The framework manages the agent's behavior throughout the conversation: it processes incoming audio, detects when the interlocutor has finished their utterance (turn detection), monitors voice activity, and responds to interruptions. The pipeline can be assembled for a specific task using various components: speech recognition (STT) and synthesis (TTS) modules, voice activity detector (VAD), speaker change detector, language model, etc.

In a separate repository under the MIT license, you can find a step-by-step guide to building an AI agent for telephony – for receiving incoming and making outgoing calls via SIP. It includes all the necessary components with links to them, provides code examples for some steps, and demonstrates the interface operation with GIF animations. If you're interested in details, you can refer to the SDK reference, as well as the documentation.

Pipecat

Another Python framework for building real-time multimodal AI agents. The first version of the tool was released in 2024 under the BSD-2-Clause license. To date, its GitHub repository has garnered over 13,000 stars.

Pipecat acts as an orchestrator for multi-agent systems that interact within the project via a common message bus. The official website features an interactive demo that allows you to evaluate the pipeline's performance in conjunction with different components when processing speech. For example, you can use a combination of Soniox + OpenAI + Cartesia and build a voice system based on them (it also supports Russian).

The developers have also prepared numerous examples of voice applications: from a simple chatbot to an AI storyteller and a word guessing game. A guide to writing your own AI agent can also be found in the documentation. It also includes a section with code examples and instructions on how to add and implement a specific feature with a particular command.

AgentLine

This is a telephony platform that allows AI agents to obtain a phone number and make voice calls (as well as receive and read SMS). AgentLine is developed by a startup of the same name. The project code was published on GitHub under the MIT license in 2026.

The call flow looks like this. The call is received by a connected provider – for example, SignalWire – and the audio is sent to AgentLine. The platform plays a greeting, records the speech, and converts it to text to send the transcription to a specified webhook. Then, the AI agent analyzes the received fragment, forms a response, and returns it to AgentLine to be relayed to the interlocutor. AgentLine has built-in billing, as well as an event 'mailbox' that operates on a polling model. The project website features various guides – including an analysis of what applications can be built on the platform. You can read more about the structure, components, and installation in the documentation.

Thanks to these and other solutions, AI agents are becoming increasingly independent and better at handling real-world communication scenarios. However, even the most advanced models do not work in a vacuum: the quality of their interaction with the user still depends on the telecommunications infrastructure through which the call passes. Behind the conversation with an AI agent lies a whole set of technologies responsible for establishing the connection, data transmission, and communication security. One of these technologies is ePDG (Evolved Packet Data Gateway). It is used to connect devices to the mobile core network via third-party Wi-Fi networks and is an important element of Wi-Fi Calling infrastructure. For the user, this means more reliable communication in places where the mobile signal is weak or absent, for example, indoors and in parking lots.
