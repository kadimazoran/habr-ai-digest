It's Not AI That Devalued Seniority. Your Interviews Did.

It's commonly believed that LLMs devalue experience: a junior with a model produces results similar to an expert's, making expertise seem unnecessary. Data reveals a more complex picture. While models do narrow the gap on tasks they excel at, they don't provide juniors with the crucial ability to verify answers. It's not the model, but the hiring process that equates juniors with LLMs and experienced specialists, by focusing only on areas where they are similar. Let's explore how this happens and what hiring adaptations can be justified by research.

One of the main questions in the IT market in 2026 is: if a junior with AI can complete tasks almost as quickly as a senior, and an inexperienced analyst gets a ready-made SQL query and a neat dashboard from a model, then why pay for experience? Such a question has a well-reasoned basis. In a study of 5,172 support employees, an AI assistant increased productivity by an average of 15%, with less experienced individuals benefiting in both speed and quality, while the most experienced saw a slight speed increase with a minor quality drop. In an experiment with 453 professionals writing professional texts, task completion time decreased by 40%, quality increased by 18%, and inequality between workers reduced. Simply put, for typical tasks, LLMs bring the weaker performers up to the level of the stronger ones.

But what if the conclusion that "LLMs have equalized juniors and experts" is not a property of the model, but a property of how we compare them? For example, what if the IT hiring process itself has become such that a junior armed with an LLM and zero experience can easily outperform a senior in the competition for a vacant position?

What is Expertise

It turns out that LLMs equalize juniors and experienced professionals not universally, but within a specific domain: for typical tasks within the model's capabilities and where the result does not require human verification. Beyond this domain, the gap persists, and sometimes even widens. Why? Cognitive psychology provided the answer long before LLMs. Expertise is primarily the content of long-term memory. An expert differs from a novice not by having a larger working memory, but by having encountered similar tasks many times and storing ready-made solution patterns. A novice has to assemble a solution step-by-step within their limited working memory. Accumulated knowledge is the main, and possibly the only, modifiable source of differences between people.

Externally, these patterns manifest in three signs. First, an expert recognizes meaningful configurations: a chess player can memorize a position from a real game at a glance, but not a random arrangement of pieces, and the same effect has been observed in programmers. Second, an expert classifies problems by their deep structure, while a novice does so by superficial features: expert physicists grouped problems by the law needed for the solution, while students grouped them by what was depicted in the problem statement. Third, an expert immediately sees the first step of the solution. A method built on this sign correlated with a full knowledge test up to 0.92 and reduced verification time by 2.5–4.9 times.

Now it's clear why LLMs don't replace expertise. All three signs are the ability to recognize what is in front of you and understand if it is structured correctly. This is precisely what is needed to verify a model's answer and notice when a task goes beyond its capabilities. The model generates text and code, but the patterns for evaluating them remain in the human mind—or are absent. In other words, LLMs replace execution, while expertise is primarily recognition.

Hiring as an Equalizer

Let's start with the typical hiring process:

  • The job description is created using LLMs, often by someone who doesn't know the subject area;

  • Resumes are adapted for Applicant Tracking Systems (ATS), and ATS filters them based on keyword matching;

  • Screening is conducted by a non-expert: what are you looking for, why are you leaving, tell us about the company;

  • then come general interviews in the STAR format

  • and then technical interviews that test only one small piece of the candidate's expertise

Now let's go through each stage and analyze what exactly is being assessed.

Job Description and ATS. The patterns required for a role are determined by the class of tasks the person will encounter. If the job description is written by someone who doesn't know these tasks, they describe the role using superficial features: tech stack, years of experience, buzzwords. This is the same novice categorization by external features, but on the employer's side. ATS then compares the resume keywords with the job description keywords—surface to surface. A resume tailored to keywords will be written identically by an expert and a novice.

Screening. A non-expert cannot distinguish a deep answer from a superficial one and doesn't realize it. In experiments with unstructured interviews, participants formed a confident impression of the interviewee even when they answered randomly, and the interview itself worsened the prediction compared to objective data alone. Subjective confidence is generally a poor indicator of accuracy: reliable intuition arises only in a regular environment with frequent feedback. At this stage, the fluency of the narrative matters more than the underlying patterns.

Interviews. The STAR format (questions about past behavior) is not inherently weak: with scales describing response levels, its validity in meta-analyses reached 0.63. However, the candidate chooses what to talk about, so without such scales, the interview largely measures the ability to tell a story.

Technical Tasks. Algorithmic challenges, system design, and live coding introduce other noise: the result is mixed with anxiety from being observed. Furthermore, such tasks are inherently typical, meaning they are highly likely to be within the model's capabilities. Therefore, when using LLMs in interviews (legally or not), the result for a novice and a senior will be the same at best.

This creates an unpleasant loop. Each stage measures either superficial features, impressions, or results on typical tasks. This is precisely the area where a novice with an LLM and an experienced specialist are similar. Recognition, verification, and work beyond the model's capabilities are not measured by any stage. This means that at the hiring outcome, a novice with an LLM and an expert look the same not because the model equalized them, but because hiring only looks at where they are equal.

A fair caveat is needed here. If the role truly consists almost entirely of typical tasks within the model's capabilities, the devaluation of experience in that role is real, and hiring is not to blame. However, for some reason, even for writing CRUD operations and styling buttons, the market has always sought to hire innate engineers.

The Cost of Equalization

If hiring doesn't differentiate between a novice with an LLM and an expert, the rational decision is to hire the cheaper one. The consequences of this decision are twofold.

First—direct errors. A novice hired for a role requiring verification will make mistakes where the model's capabilities are exceeded and will not realize it. This follows from data on tasks beyond the model's capabilities and the illusion of competence, although direct measurements of such hiring errors are lacking.

Second—a broken pipeline. Experts are developed from novices. As far back as 1983, Lisanne Bainbridge described the "irony of automation" in industry: automation takes away the very practice from the operator that is needed to control it and substitute for it when it errs. The transfer to LLMs is analogous, but direct. A novice hired as a model operator does not build their own patterns.

Economists describe automation not as job replacement, but as task redistribution: technology displaces labor from some tasks and creates new ones where labor is needed again. If hiring assesses people solely on tasks that the model has already taken over, it systematically undervalues those capable of performing the remaining ones.

The circle closes: hiring doesn't see expertise, the market doesn't pay for it, novices grow up as operators, and expertise genuinely diminishes.

Hiring Adaptations

The good news is that personnel selection psychology has proven tools, and expertise theory suggests how to target them. Below are adaptations, categorized by the strength of their evidence base.

Adaptations with a Robust Evidence Base.

Job Analysis Instead of Keyword Lists. A job description is written from a list of role tasks compiled by an expert: 5–10 typical tasks with an indication of where each stands relative to the model—done by LLM; done by LLM, verified by human; task beyond model's capabilities.

Structured Interview with Scales. A structured interview is called the best method because, among all selection methods, it most accurately predicts how a person will perform.

How it works:

  • Identical Questions for Everyone. Candidate answers can be compared to each other, not to the interviewer's mood.

  • Questions are Based on Job Analysis. They ask about what will actually be needed in the role.

  • Scales with Described Response Levels. Raters agree more on candidate assessments.

  • Less Room for Impression. In unstructured interviews, people confidently "understand" even random answers, and predictions worsen as a result. Structure eliminates this effect.

Work Samples on Real Tasks. A work sample is an assignment that replicates a part of the actual job: the candidate performs the work, not just talks about it. Its correlation with subsequent performance is moderate but stable. A work sample can be compiled from the list of role tasks.

Why the method works:

  • It measures behavior on a task from the actual job, not self-description or impression.

  • In terms of expertise theory, the sample directly engages domain-specific patterns, not the ability to talk about them.

However, this method has caveats:

  • The sample partially overlaps with general abilities. Its correlation with general cognitive ability tests is 0.32. This means the sample measures more than just expertise.

  • The sample only captures patterns included in the assignment. A narrow sample provides a narrow slice, so it should be compiled from a list of actual role tasks.

  • A candidate might complete a take-home sample using an LLM (hello to all fans of typical test assignments within LLM competence). Then it ceases to differentiate between a novice with a model and an expert.

Adaptations Justified by Theory but Not Yet Validated in Hiring.

Recognition Tasks. Short "first step" assignments: a log fragment, a diff, an architecture diagram with the question "What is happening here and what will you do first?", with an expert-defined answer key. Or sorting 6–8 short problem descriptions with explanations, similar to studies on expert task classification. The method works well in training; it has not been validated in IT hiring.

LLM Answer Verification Tasks. Within a work sample: find an error in a plausible solution generated by the model, and solve a task beyond its capabilities. This is a direct measurement of what distinguishes an expert from a novice with a model, but data on the validity of such tasks is not yet available.

Broad Pattern Sampling. One mandatory area and one that the candidate chooses as their strongest. This reduces the risk that the interview will capture a narrow and not the strongest slice. Explaining or modifying a solution live verifies that the processing was done by the candidate themselves.

Reduced External Load from Format. If the result of live coding is mixed with anxiety from observation, the format should be changed, for example, by having the task solved without an observer and then discussing it.

The cost of these adaptations should be stated upfront. They require expert time at each stage—from the task list to scales and sample evaluation. However, the alternative—hiring that doesn't see expertise—ultimately proves more expensive.

Conclusion

The pattern we've pieced together reads as follows. LLMs do indeed narrow the gap between novices and experienced professionals, but only for typical tasks within their capabilities and only in generating results. Expertise, on the other hand, consists of recognition patterns needed to verify answers and identify when a task exceeds the model's capabilities. Typical hiring measures superficial features, impressions, and results on typical tasks—precisely where a novice with an LLM and an expert are similar. Therefore, it's not the model that equalizes them, but the hiring process.

From this, we draw three conclusions. First, the assertion that "LLMs have devalued experience" is largely an artifact of measurement, not a property of the technology. Second, personnel selection psychology offers tools with a robust evidence base—job analysis, structured interviews with scales, work samples, mechanical score aggregation—and these are sufficient for hiring to once again recognize expertise. Third, tasks for recognition and LLM answer verification are the most direct way to measure what the model cannot provide, but they still need to be validated.

For companies, the practical takeaway is: as long as hiring equates a novice with a model to an expert, saving on experience today turns into a shortage of verifiers tomorrow. For specialists, the takeaway is equally concrete: value grows where a person can recognize and verify, not where they get the answer the fastest.
