I first encountered web design in 2005. I started with crude HTML on narod.ru, continued with Joomla, WordPress, and the constantly crashing Photoshop with 200+ megabyte layouts. Then, like everyone else, I switched to Figma — in short, I went through all the stages of evolution.

I myself don't always understand

When reviewing a designer's work, I try to add at least some measurable objectivity to the review, beyond 'I'm more experienced and think this is how it should be.' And since requests like 'Can you look at the mockup?' come in an endless stream, at some point I just wanted to make my life easier and stop answering the same questions.

Recently, I created a tool for checking the quality of HMI interfaces and wrote an article about it. But people uploaded just websites to it! The check yielded meaningless results because HMI has its own rules: for example, 50 mm between emergency buttons, and the gloves used to press these buttons.

I had to create a separate version for regular websites and applications. The design analysis is based on general standards — WCAG 2.2, Apple Human Interface Guidelines, and Material Design, and the recommendations are tied to measurable violations.

Can AI be trusted?

In early 2026, NN/g released an interview with the founders of Baymard about the accuracy of AI audits. In 2023, GPT-4's accuracy was only 20%, and in the latest Baymard UX-Ray 2.0 study, the tool claims 95% accuracy compared to human auditors on 79 e-commerce websites. The results of public tests of several similar tools range from 50-75%.

As Baymard itself writes, a single wrong decision in UX can lead to astronomical business costs, so the design checker measures what can be verified numerically, and honestly labels the rest as an assessment or prediction. The algorithm highlights what to pay attention to in the interface, rather than replacing a live expert.

There's Lighthouse for code, Stark for Figma, and separate paid heatmaps on the market. But I haven't found a tool that, from a single screenshot, simultaneously calculates contrast, button sizes with distances between them, shows a heatmap, and provides ready-made prompts for edits.

Therefore, I compiled a set of rules for the most frequent errors and made it myself using Claude. The tool works without the fantasies of language models and does not evaluate the beauty of websites; it uses ordinary computer vision, as well as formulas and standard norms.

Fun fact: 'ugly' looking foreign websites like Amazon, Adobe, Lufthansa, etc., pass all checks.

What courses won't teach you

Courses sell entry through the part that has already been replaced by neural networks — the skill of implementing design mockups. How to sell these skills later, a design course is unlikely to tell you (or it's a very good course).

I always recommend all beginners to master basic knowledge for free, practice immediately with neural networks — including with similar automatic checks based on established rules.

And of course, don't forget about communities and gaining knowledge from colleagues.

What is worth learning

In the first version of the tool, the check mistook cat fur for text. I noticed the dangling prepositions, not the checker — this indicates that any errors can be turned into rules, and gradually they will be automated.

Contrast can be corrected with one click, but whether secondary buttons should be more prominent at all is decided by a person.

Regardless of market changes, designers should develop:

  1. Curiosity. A neural network won't suggest a reference you haven't asked for. To tell an artificial intelligence 'Draw as if you were Andy Warhol,' you need to understand who that is. To understand how brutalism differs from Swiss style, skeumorphism from neomorphism, grotesque from serif — and so on.

  2. Understanding of context. Who is the user, where are they, what hinders them. A factory operator, a grandmother on Gosuslugi, an investor on a phone. From the prompt 'make a job site' without specifying who we are making it for, it can turn out significantly worse.

  3. Rejection. To see that what is generated is plausible nonsense. A neural network produces both good and bad, and distinguishing them is our job.

  4. The ability to say 'no'. To remove the unnecessary, refuse a feature, convince the client. A neural network will add anything, but common sense must prevail everywhere.

  5. Responsibility. To defend the decision to the client and be accountable if it doesn't work.

But this is practice and experience; courses are almost powerless here.

Where should a beginner designer go

My opinion: into interfaces for hardware. Robots, mechanisms, transport, railways, medical equipment, construction — everything tied to the real world and physics, because a neural network can't check it from a screenshot. I saw firsthand how it works in metallurgy: human presence is still indispensable here. And operators need something to monitor the lagging robots through.

That is, globally, it's not interface design that is disappearing, but web design in its classic form (to draw and assemble a website). This is the part that neural networks will largely cover, including setting up standard promotion. But experience is needed to make the right decisions: to understand what this business needs, and to assess whether it will work — according to Baymard, neural networks err up to half the time. This means the seniors at the beginning of the article were only half right.

Advice for schools: sell product vision, not the craft of website building. For beginners, it's relevant to be able to refine the results generated by a neural network, rather than mastering constructors — which is what I mentioned above.
