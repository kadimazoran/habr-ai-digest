Then I'll discuss why, from a legal perspective, it's not a copy at all – and why, in the age of AI, this loophole has turned into an industry. The conclusion will offer insights and specific advice for authors whose content is being (or will inevitably be) rebuilt.

How I Knew It Wasn't the Original

The ear catches the similarity, but my ear isn't proof. Shazam-like fingerprints won't work here: they find exact copies, and they break when the key or arrangement changes. That's why I used chroma features – the distribution of energy across 12 pitch classes. They are indifferent to timbre and octave, but sensitive to key: transposition shifts the vector in a circle, and this needs to be compensated by iteration.

So, the first attempt was this: I "dragged" the spectrogram of the recording along the original's spectrogram and found the maximum – it looked convincing. Then I checked what the same metric yielded on non-matching segments of the same track. The average was 0.79, the maximum 0.828, and my detected peak was seven-tenths higher than the best of them. It lay within its own noise: chroma similarity is high for any music of the same genre and instrumentation. Therefore, it was clearly a false positive.

I had to build a metric that distinguishes: Pearson correlation on feature sequences instead of averaged cosine; zero measured along the curve itself, instead of an assigned threshold; iteration of transpositions and time alignment. After this, the picture improved: there was no match in the previous window at all, but there was one in another place – closer to the end of the track, and with a transposition: the recording sounds about two and a half tones lower than the original. Two independent feature families, both candidate files, the same location: r ≈ 0.31 at z ≈ 6.

Three-tenths is reliable, but not much: direct use of the recording would have yielded significantly more. This means it's not audio copying, but derivative material. The entire difference lies between these two things.

The spectrograms also don't show a direct match: genre, instrumentation, and general harmonic progressions might match, but everything else must differ – arrangement, mastering, room noise, microphone. A spectrogram doesn't work as proof; as an illustration of what was found above, it's fine, provided the bottom panel is transposed to the same key.

What I can say now is that the arrangement and performance differed, and this was confirmed – there is a match, but its magnitude is far from audio copying. It's a re-recording, transposed to a different key. What still cannot be determined from sixteen seconds is whether this is a licensed cover, a remix, an official re-recording by the rights holder, or an independent work. But if it's the same composition recorded anew, it doesn't reproduce the original sound recording – and the rights to the work itself remain in force.

Not the same sound recording – but possibly the same work.

What Copyright Protects – and What It Doesn't

There's no new loophole here. The principle is over a century old: copyright protects the expression of an idea, not the idea itself. In the Russian Civil Code, it's Art. 1259, para. 5: ideas, concepts, methods, processes, systems, and problem-solving approaches are not protected. In US law, it's the same idea-expression dichotomy doctrine, § 102(b) of the Copyright Act.

Therefore, "reproducing from scratch" is not circumventing the law, but an expected behavior. The law simply states: take the idea, leave the expression. The only question is where the line is drawn in each medium.

However, it's important not to confuse two different objects of protection. A song has two: the sound recording – a specific fixed recording of a performance, and the musical work – the original composition expressed in music and, if present, lyrics. Moreover, protection is not as straightforward as it seems: individual notes, typical chord progressions, and common rhythmic figures are not protected on their own – protection may apply to the original combination of these elements, their sequence, and development.

Re-singing a song means not copying the sound recording – and simultaneously reproducing the work. The former is indeed not a copy. The latter is grounds for a license. So, rebuilding doesn't negate the right: it changes which right applies.

Music: What Court Cases Show

In music, the line has proven so thin that courts have swung back and forth on it.

On one hand, the Blurred Lines case: in 2015, a jury found infringement of the composition rights by comparing protected, deposited notation, not sound recordings. The outcome after appeals: $5.3 million and 50% of future royalties to the Gaye family. The case became symbolic not because of the decision itself, but because of the question it raised: has this approach turned similarity of style and "vibe" into too broad an object of protection? Over 200 musicians, including Hans Zimmer, signed a petition against it, even though the "protected vibe" formula was mentioned in a concurring opinion by one of the appellate judges, not in the decision itself.

On the other hand, Katy Perry's Dark Horse: a jury in 2019 awarded $2.8 million for an eight-note ostinato, but the judge overturned the verdict, and the 2022 appeal confirmed it: the combination was "not sufficiently unique," and building blocks of music cannot be monopolized. Ed Sheeran, in 2023, prevailed in the "Thinking Out Loud" case: the jury found no infringement, and a crucial part of the defense was independent creation. Regarding chords, the court spoke separately and later, in another dispute: a four-chord progression with a typical syncopated rhythm is too common to be independently protected. The overall conclusion is that music's building blocks do not become anyone's monopoly – but the grounds for the decisions differ, and they should not be mixed.

The pendulum swings between "if you recognize it, you stole it" and "common is common." But both sides agree on one thing: direct copying of notes is infringement, but how far one can deviate from the original is decided anew each time. Changing the key and arrangement doesn't create distance – if the ear still recognizes the melody, a court might hear it too.

Code: The Clean Room as a Legal Template

In code, the same boundary has been refined into a technology. In 1983–1984, the IBM PC clone industry solved the problem literally by the book: a team of "dirty" engineers read the BIOS source code and wrote a specification, and a team of "clean" programmers – those who had never seen IBM's code – wrote the implementation from scratch based on that specification. Phoenix Technologies turned this into a business model, and Compaq built its BIOS using the same separated process – and this historically became the model for documenting independent implementation. IBM, meanwhile, pursued direct copiers: Corona Data Systems and Eagle Computer settled claims in early 1984 without waiting for a court decision.

The clean room is protected not because its code is "a different expression of the same idea," but because it was not copied: independent creation is protected in itself, as a separate basis. However, a clean room is not automatic immunity: the outcome depends on facts of access, the method of reverse engineering, licenses and agreements, patents, and applicable law. Court practice here is not "rewrote it – you're free": Sega v. Accolade allowed intermediate copying during decompilation, but in a narrow context – for access to unprotected functional elements and compatibility.

A more recent example: Oracle v. Google (2021). Google copied 11,500 lines of Java API declarative code – 0.4% of the codebase – and the US Supreme Court ruled it fair use: the interface was "functional" and "further from the core of copyright" than almost any other program. The court deliberately did not answer whether an API is copyrightable at all.

One must be cautious about generalizations. The Supreme Court did not say that the API structure is "nobody's": it suggested it might be protectable and ruled the specific use fair use – with caveats about the interface's functionality and market effect. The expression is indeed yours, the idea is not, but the structure and organization of elements is a separate issue and is decided anew each time. Rebuilding is not a loophole, but it's not a free pass either.

How AI Made Rebuilding Cheap

Previously, a clean room required a team of engineers and months of work, and "re-singing" a song so that the ear recognized it but a lawyer wouldn't, cost a studio and musicians. Now, both are done by one person with a subscription to AI agents for a couple of hundred dollars a month.

Code: Specification Instead of Source Code

In March 2026, Habr published an article "Copyright No Longer Protects Your Code" – with a diagnosis I share: one agent studies a project under a "viral" license like GPL and writes a specification, another agent writes a new implementation based on the specification. The code is different, the structure is different, the language might be different – but the system is the same. Licenses regulate code copying, but not system rebuilding from a description. The author rightly notes: AI didn't create the problem, but highlighted an old one that was simply too expensive to exploit before.

Music: Sheet Music Transcriptions Already in Court

The same is happening in music on an industrial scale. In June 2024, twelve record companies – Universal, Sony, Warner, and others – filed lawsuits against music generators Suno and Udio (RIAA coordinated them but was not the plaintiff). And in the lawsuit materials were two-column sheet music transcriptions: the model's output and the original, transposed to the same key, with color-coded matches – red where both pitch and rhythm coincided. The same logic as mine with the recording from the store – only in federal court and not on spectrograms, but on scores.

The market has since partially resolved itself: Universal settled with Udio in October 2025, Warner in November, followed by Merlin (January 2026), Kobalt (April), and NMPA (June) – licenses instead of lawsuits. Only Sony remains to sue: in early July 2026, a judge denied its motion to add 30,442 recordings, and the original case remained on 333 works. The remaining proceedings, including Sony v. Udio, may provide one of the first substantive judicial answers to the question of fair use in training music models. But there is no decision on the merits yet, and the settlements of other labels do not set a precedent.

From this, the following conclusion follows: rebuilding has become cheaper than licensing, and the market is already digesting this – some through courts, some through deals. This means it's time for authors to stop expecting copyright to do what it never could.

What This Means for Authors

If your work can be described in words, it can be rebuilt. In the age of AI, copyright still catches direct copies, but rebuilding is becoming cheaper faster than lawyers can formulate lawsuits. The question is not "how to prohibit," but "what part of my value cannot be rebuilt?"

Here's what data and practice suggest:

1. Document the expression – but don't expect it to protect against rebuilding. Depositing, committing, publishing with a date – this is protection against direct copying, and it works: IBM in 1984 forced direct BIOS copiers to rewrite the code without going to court. But copyright itself doesn't save against "made anew from a description" – not because the law is bad, but because it's a different layer.

2. Shift value to what cannot be rebuilt. The data you work with – corpora, behavioral data, catalogs – cannot be rewritten from a specification: they must be obtained. But don't be fooled: data itself is usually not protected by copyright, and a similar set can be independently assembled, purchased, or generated. What protects is not the fact of ownership, but what the competitor lacks: exclusive access, trade secret status (which must be established – it doesn't appear on its own), unique selection and annotation. Know-how and trade secrets (Art. 1465 of the Civil Code of the Russian Federation) protect processes, not text. Brand and community are the only things a clone doesn't get along with the product.

3. Stay ahead with iterations. Rebuilding is by definition one step behind: the original must appear first. If you move faster than they can rebuild, the rebuilder will always be selling yesterday's version.

4. If you are rebuilding yourself – maintain creative, not technical, distance. Changing the key and renaming variables are camouflage, not independence: they work as a rule of thumb but are not a legal test. A court looks at specific protected elements, access to the original, the purpose of use, and licenses – not at cosmetic changes. Blurred Lines showed that what's compared is not recordings, but the musical score; Dark Horse was saved not by changes, but by the recognition that eight notes are a common building block. A safe distance is when similarity is explained by a common idea, not a common expression.

5. Consider that "modified" no longer means "unrecognizable." Tools like chroma analysis and embeddings are publicly available, and the entry barrier is an evening and a phone. If today the similarity of your work and a competitor's product can be proven with a Python script, tomorrow it will be proven in court (or in the comments).

6. Check what exactly you call "my intellectual property." If it fits into a specification, consider that copying and rebuilding it is cheap, regardless of your legal status. It's worth investing in what doesn't fit into a specification.

Conclusion

Copyright is not dead – it simply protects a different layer than you thought. It doesn't save a sound recording from re-recording, but it protects the work, and that's what requires a license. AI hasn't broken this structure; it has made its cost visible: previously, "making anew" cost more than reaching an agreement, now it's the opposite.

For authors, this means a change in strategy: stop protecting what cannot be protected and enhance what cannot be rebuilt – data, know-how, community, and speed. The recording from the store is a good illustration: someone's song was rebuilt so carefully that it has become a new industry norm.
