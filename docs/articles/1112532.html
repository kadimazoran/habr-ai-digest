Hello, Habr!

My name is Anton Kabaev, and I am a Technical Lead at the Center for Natural Language Processing, MWS AI.

Recently, we released two encoders for working with texts in Russian and English into the public domain and introduced a new method for improving their performance. An encoder can be trained from scratch, or you can take a pre-trained multilingual model and replace its vocabulary. We tried both approaches. In this article, I will describe the process of our work and the conclusions we reached.

BERT is an encoder language model. It converts text into vectors, which can be used to classify texts, find similar phrases, or rank documents.

During BERT training, some tokens are masked. In the sentence 'The train departs from the [MASK] platform,' the model must restore the gap using words to the left and right. Through many such examples, the encoder learns language patterns and facts about the world. This is pre-training. But what happens if you change the exercise for an already trained encoder and continue training it?

We created two Russian-language ModernBERT models of different sizes. The Small model, with 34 million parameters, was trained from scratch, and the Base model was obtained from the multilingual mmBERT. For comparison, we took open-source models from a Russian developer of the same size, RuModernBERT-small and RuModernBERT-base. We tried post-training on both of our models and on the ready-made base model from the Russian developer. It was this one that benefited the most: the average accuracy on seven RussianSuperGLUE tasks increased from 70.88% to 74.12%. We will explain how the encoders were trained, what changed after post-training, and why the choice of model depends on how you intend to use it.

Where We Started

We aimed to achieve several goals:

  • To practically understand how to pre-train high-quality Russian-language encoders;

  • To create our own models of two sizes—small and base—and prepare useful open-source models for the community;

  • To verify whether post-training helps an encoder solve applied tasks better.

ModernBERT, like BERT, outputs a vector for each token, considering the context from the left and right. For example, in the phrases 'the castle towered over the city' and 'the lock jammed,' the word 'castle'/'lock' will receive different representations. One of the features of ModernBERT is alternating local and global attention. Most layers look at nearby tokens, while specific layers connect positions across the entire sequence.

Two Pre-training Paths

Both models were trained on the BERT task, masked language modeling (MLM). For the Base model, we used the pre-trained mmBERT, while the Small model began training from scratch, and later we involved a teacher from the Base line in its training. A shared tokenizer allowed us to compare their predictions using a single vocabulary.

Tokenizer and Embedding Transfer

To train the tokenizer, we used about 10 million documents from Russian FineWeb-2, English FineWeb, and a code corpus in an 80/10/10 proportion. We borrowed text preprocessing from ruRoberta, and trained the BPE merge rules and a vocabulary of 50,368 tokens from scratch.

From mmBERT-base, we took the Transformer layers and normalizations, and rebuilt the embedding table for the new vocabulary. If a token exists in the old vocabulary, we copy its vector. If there is no match, we decompose the new token into old ones and sum their embeddings. Special tokens were mapped by function: padding to padding, mask to mask.

Base Fine-tuned on top of mmBERT

With the new vocabulary, we continued MLM pre-training on Russian and English FineWeb and code. In the first stage, the model processed about 76 billion tokens with a peak learning rate of 5 × 10⁻⁴. Then, we reduced the learning rate to 10⁻⁴. After approximately 12 billion tokens, the results on control tasks plateaued, and we stopped. In both stages, we masked 15% of the tokens and used sequences up to 1024 tokens long.

We filtered out low-quality texts using external models. Russian texts were checked by two classifiers: RuCoLa evaluated how naturally the text was written, and RuBERT Corruption Detector looked for signs of corruption, such as omissions or word reordering. For English texts, Gibberish Detector helped remove incoherent fragments. In the second stage, we filtered more strictly, keeping only texts with higher classifier scores.

Small Learns First Alone, Then from a Teacher

For the Small model, we sought a configuration with a budget of around 35 million parameters, similar to RuModernBERT-small. In the work Scale Efficiently, the authors compared models with a similar number of parameters and showed that with an equal budget, part of the width can be exchanged for depth, leading to improved quality. We went in the same direction: our Small model has 21 layers with a hidden size of 320 and an intermediate size of 480. RuModernBERT-small has 12 layers with sizes 384 and 576, respectively.

For the first 128 billion tokens, the Small model learned to restore masked tokens on Russian FineWeb-2. In the final stage, we expanded the data mix and made our Base model a teacher. To Russian FineWeb-2, we added English FineWeb, Russian and English Wikipedia, and code. We supplemented the standard MLM loss with KL loss with a coefficient of 0.5, so that the Small model would learn to assign probabilities to possible answers similar to those of the Base model. This stage involved about another 12 billion tokens.

During the distillation stage, we also increased the maximum sequence length from 1024 to 2048 tokens and reduced the masking ratio from 30% to 15%. The new mix was filtered by the same models and with the same strictness as in the first stage of training the Base model.

In total, over two stages, the Small model processed about 140 billion tokens. The Base model had a different starting point. The original mmBERT had already been trained on approximately 3 trillion tokens in over 1800 languages, while our 88 billion tokens were used for its adaptation. For scale: the training stage tables for RuModernBERT indicate 1.6 trillion tokens for the small version and 2 trillion for the base.

What Can Be Done with an Encoder After MLM

After pre-training, the encoder can restore masked tokens. We wanted to see if it could be made more useful for applied tasks by temporarily giving it a different exercise. This approach was previously tried in the work A Causal Language Modeling Detour Improves Encoder Continued Pretraining: when adapting ModernBERT to biomedical texts, the authors first trained it to predict the next token, and then returned to MLM.

For our experiment, we used dialogues from the ZeroAgency corpus. In the assistant's responses, the reasoning process is often visible directly in the text: the assistant explains the reason, breaks down the solution into steps, or shows how to arrive at a conclusion from the problem's conditions. We used the connection between question and answer in training in different ways. In different branches, the model learned to continue the answer, restore it based on the question's representation, or detect a replaced fragment. Since the corpus itself changed along with the task, we separately fine-tuned our Base model with standard MLM on assistant responses from the same dialogues. We compared the variants with task changes against this run.

Causal Language Modeling (CLM)

In this branch, the encoder temporarily worked with causal attention: each position saw only itself and the preceding tokens. System messages and user queries went into the context, and the prediction error for the next token was calculated only for the assistant's response and the end-of-sequence token.

The trained encoder was then required to consider context from both sides again. Therefore, after 90 million target tokens from assistant responses, we re-enabled bidirectional attention and, as in this work, briefly returned to MLM. This restoration phase involved about 10 million tokens of natural text with a masking ratio of 15%.

Predicting Continuation via CLS (Future-CLS)

The second branch divides the text into context and continuation. The context goes into the main encoder, which trains on standard MLM with 15% masking. The continuation is masked by 90% and fed to a single-layer auxiliary encoder along with the [CLS] embedding.

In the figure, the main encoder only sees 'Where can I [MASK]?', while the answer 'At the next station' goes to the auxiliary encoder. The latter restores the masked tokens of the answer based on the visible part and the [CLS] embedding from the question. Since the error in restoration is passed back to the main encoder via [CLS], we expected the question vector to learn to retain more contextual information. After training, the auxiliary encoder is removed.

Detecting Replaced Fragment (Span Injection)

The third branch receives text where a fragment of the message is sometimes replaced. The model restores the masked tokens of the original text and simultaneously determines if a replacement occurred and, if so, finds its boundaries.

A random fragment might stand out too much from the text, so we selected replacements based on TF-IDF similarity among fragments from other dialogues. For a user's utterance, we looked for a fragment in other user utterances; for an assistant's response, we looked in other assistant responses. In the figure, 'The ticket is bought for tomorrow morning' is transformed into 'The ticket is bought until the end of this week.' The MLM head restores the word 'bought,' while a separate head, using [CLS], determines if a replacement occurred. Two more heads mark its start and end. MLM loss is applied only outside the inserted span. If a mask covers the entire inserted span, the example is considered clean.

Triple-Merge: Averaging Weights of Three Branches

For the causal branch, we saved two checkpoints: one immediately after CLM, and another after returning to MLM. We averaged their weights equally to obtain CLM50. Then, we combined CLM50, Future-CLS, and Span injection, taking one-third of the weights from each. This variant was named Triple-Merge. Parameters can be added element-wise because all three branches start from the same checkpoint and use the same architecture and tokenizer. We performed this averaging separately for our Small, our Base, and RuModernBERT-base.

After averaging, a single bidirectional encoder remains, which can be used like a standard ModernBERT. For classification, it is fine-tuned with a new head, and for text comparison, vectors are obtained from it directly.

How We Tested Quality

We evaluated the encoders' quality during fine-tuning and separately assessed the vectors produced by models with frozen weights. For the first comparison, we used seven tasks from RussianSuperGLUE (RSG). For example, TERRa requires determining if one statement follows from another; RUSSE checks if a word is used with the same meaning in two contexts; and PARus involves choosing one of two causes or consequences.

For each RSG task, the encoder was fine-tuned along with a head. For PARus, we duplicated the training examples, changing the order of options. In MuSeRC, each answer option was evaluated as a separate example with a 'correct' or 'incorrect' label.

For each 'model × task' pair, we iterated through learning rates (2 × 10⁻⁵, 5 × 10⁻⁵, 10⁻⁴), batch sizes (16, 32, 64), and number of epochs (5, 10, 20). This resulted in 27 combinations. Each was run with five seeds, and we took the best validation accuracy per run. Using the average of five runs, we selected a set of hyperparameters. Then, with this set, we trained the model on ten new seeds to evaluate the average and variance across runs not involved in the selection.

In Encodechka, the encoder weights remained frozen. Depending on the task, we either compared vectors directly or trained a lightweight classifier on them. This allowed us to assess how useful the features extracted by the encoder are without task-specific fine-tuning.

Triple-Merge added about half a point to the Small model, primarily due to DaNetQA, RUSSE, and TERra. The original Base models showed similar average results.

With Triple-Merge, RuModernBERT-base improved the most. On PARus, the model gave about eight more correct answers out of a hundred on average, and on DaNetQA, accuracy increased by about 4.7 percentage points. The result on MuSeRC barely changed.

We primarily focus on the combination of TERra, RUSSE, and reading comprehension tasks (DaNetQA and MuSeRC). They test different aspects: the relationship between statements, the meaning of a word in context, and working with text-based questions. In PARus, validation consists of 100 examples, so each answer shifts the score by a full point. For RCB and RWSD, the scores of different models in this series clustered quite closely.

What changes when moving from web texts to dialogues and continuing training on the same MLM task? What to Take Away from These Experiments

Pre-training gave us two working models. The Small model, which we trained from scratch, outperformed RuModernBERT-small in average scores on both benchmarks, while the adaptation of mmBERT resulted in a Base model with performance comparable to RuModernBERT-base. Post-training demonstrated that a pre-trained encoder can also be improved by changing the training task.

For our Small model, the effect of post-training depended on the application method. Triple-Merge provided a small gain on RSG after fine-tuning on labeled examples. For working with pre-trained vectors, the original Small model, which has a higher average score on Encodechka, was more suitable.

For the pre-trained RuModernBERT-base, both evaluation methods showed improvement. The average accuracy on RSG increased by 3.24 p.p., and the average score on Encodechka by approximately 3.8 points. Across the entire hyperparameter grid, RuModernBERT-base with Triple-Merge less frequently produced drastically weak results. We also observed a small but stable increase on several internal tasks.
