Bill Jones dreamed of becoming a writer his whole life. Two years ago, he retired and decided to play around with ChatGPT.

Today, he has 445 books.

He writes about everything that interests him. Sports, jazz, World War II, eccentric geniuses.

More precisely, ChatGPT writes, and Mr. Jones chooses the topic, proofreads, edits the AI text, generates the cover, and uploads it to marketplaces. Just over the summer, he churned out 70 new books. During the Christmas gift season, he sold 821 books for $6,000. He says the books don't fly off the shelves like hotcakes, but it's enough to make a living.

He doesn't work an hour a day. Jones says he spends 8-12 hours a day on books and works 6-7 days a week.

So, he's quite the guy.

At 70, he mastered a neural network, and it's not like he's just messing around, generating pictures of himself in knight's armor. No, the old man integrated this AI into his boring retirement routine and set up a decent content factory at home.

But I'm not interested in his skill here.

Look, even if Jones sells 10-30% of the total print run, and he says he gets about $7 per sale, he's earning $300-940 every month.

(A nice stable passive addition to his pension, right?)

He doesn't care if all 445 books are successful.

That's the main point.

How was it before?

You'd write one book for six months to a year, release it, promote it on social media, and only then find out if anyone needed it or not. If not, well, you gained experience, learned about the book business, but essentially, a year wasted.

But Jones tests hundreds of topics.

In the same amount of time.

Listen with sound. Not everything is so rosy. I spoke with authors who edit AI texts. According to them, it's hellish work; by evening, your brain is mush - it's hard for a neural network to stick to one topic in long dialogues

Let's go in order.

Previously, authors spent a hell of a lot of human effort to get a book out to the masses and prayed it would at least recoup the invested time (beginners earn literally a pittance, 15-30 thousand rubles).

Today, you churn out ten books a month - let them be garbage, the reader, having tried once, will curse the author, "yuck, yuck, nasty" - but still, due to the volume, some of the books will bring in a little money.

That is, the AI author simply increases the number of attempts until something starts selling.

Venture capital funds do the same: they pour money into dozens of startups, fully aware that more than half will die, two or three will tread water, and one successful project will cover the investment.

Before AI, this was a rarity with books.

Writing texts was time-consuming (if done yourself) or expensive (hiring someone).

But AI, the rascal, devalued content production.

Pay $20 and churn out a hundred books a month. If you guess the topic wrong - without ChatGPT, you lost a year, but if you generated it, it took a couple of days and a trip to the grocery store.

Since creating content has become cheap, people, naturally, have an incentive to churn it out in tons. Out of a hundred attempts, at least one will hit.

When the cost of error drops to almost zero (an unsuccessful book costs you not a year of your life, but a couple of days), people stop aiming and start firing volleys

And as we see, this story repeats far beyond books: articles, YouTube videos, music, websites, SaaS products, games. Look how the local community on Habr revolted against AI articles (deservedly, in my opinion, because AI authors kill fair competition between human authors, and generation is prohibited on the platform).

Where AI cheapens content production, the neural sludge starts to knock out the economics of human-made content.

Yes, it's unpleasant.

Despite the fact that a human writes texts/videos better than a machine, they compete with a content factory that, in the same amount of time, covers ten times more topics and search queries. And the public doesn't always understand the difference between human and AI (half can't tell).
