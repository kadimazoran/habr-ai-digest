The Cost of Mistakes: Padding, Network Drive Checkpoints, and a Watchdog That Killed Healthy Training

Mistakes happen in any real job. Usually, they are mentioned in one line somewhere between graphs with results, but I decided to do the opposite: gather our missteps into one article and analyze them from an engineering perspective, i.e., what exactly broke, how much it cost, and what we changed afterward.

In the second article of the series, I promised to tell you how I rented video cards in the cloud and burned money on them without calculating almost anything. In the third, I reminded you that the promise is still valid. Now I'm fulfilling it. Most of the article is about cloud fine-tuning of a language model, and at the end, I will talk about several measurement errors: they cost almost no money but were more dangerous.

As before, I do not name the base models, fine-tuning techniques, checkpoints, or data sources. I name the card classes, providers, hourly prices, and orders of magnitude because the discussion of cost loses meaning without them.

Why the Cloud at All

Speech recognition and voice synthesis from the second and third articles are trained at home, on a card with 24 GB of memory, and it's enough for them. But for a language model that needs to write and respond in Crimean Tatar, it's not enough: full fine-tuning requires a card with 141 GB or more. Buying such a card for a few runs a year is not worth it; they are rented by the hour. It's like a rental car: you pay for the time it's with you, and the meter doesn't care if you're driving or standing on the roadside with the hood open.

Over a month and a half, a little over $300 was spent on rent. This wasn't one failed run, but a series of iterations: train, observe, change configuration, run again. With each iteration, the run became cheaper and more reliable until we found a working recipe. Honestly, I was angry that we spent so much money. But I'm glad that in the end, we have the right "recipe" for future training: now we know how much a run costs and what not to pay for. The graph shows how the cost of a single training run changed.

Run #1: $53 for 15% of the Second Stage

The first rented card was an H200 with 141 GB, costing about $4.6 per hour. The training was two-stage, and the estimate for the entire run was 9–11 hours and $33–40. After 11.5 hours and $53, I stopped it: the second stage was 15% complete, with about 46 hours remaining. The card provided approximately 3–4% of its useful output during this entire time. When we figured it out, we found three reasons, and each of them independently consumed a significant portion of the cost.

Padding: The Estimate Counted Tokens, the Card Counted Empty Space

The main reason is that we paid for empty space. Our training dialogues are short, with a median of about 20 tokens, but in a batch, each example was padded to 1024. The attention mask and loss mask ensure that padding doesn't affect the result, but it fully affects the cost: matrices are still multiplied for the entire sequence length, but most of these numbers are later discarded. It's like having one short dialogue in each box, but carrying the whole box. The estimate was calculated based on the actual content, 59 million tokens, while the card processed 1.17 billion positions. Hence, the 20x discrepancy.

The most frustrating part is that the plan explicitly stated an assumption: padding overhead would be around 20%. No one checked it, and checking took a minute:

# Estimate the proportion of useful work with padding to max_len lengths = [len(tokenize(x)) for x in sample] # sample from the dataset useful = sum(lengths) padded = len(lengths) * max_len print(f"useful: {useful / padded:.1%}") # we got about 5%

If the median example length is fifty times less than max_len, no kernel optimization can save it. The card honestly multiplies zeros, and the bill is for hours. The correct estimate calculates not "corpus size × epochs," but the number of positions the card will actually process: steps × microbatch × sequence length, divided by actual speed and multiplied by the hourly rate.

Checkpoints on a Network Drive

The second reason was saving. The training state was saved every 400 steps, 46 GB at a time, not to a local disk but to a network volume. Saving was synchronous: until the checkpoint was fully written, the next step wouldn't start, and the card would just sit idle. During the run, about 600 GB of data was saved, and as a result, the card was busy 58–68% of the time, even without considering padding.

Saving is, of course, necessary: without it, any failure would roll back to the beginning. But their frequency should be measured in hours of operation, not steps. The question is: how much does it cost to lose an hour, and how much does one save cost? A 46 GB checkpoint is justified once every few thousand steps, not every 400, and it's better to save it to the machine's local disk, transferring it to the network volume later.

Data Loader Couldn't Keep Up

The third reason turned out to be quite simple. On a machine with 128 cores, batch preparation was handled by 2 processes. With short examples, the card processed a batch faster than the next one was prepared, and it waited again. This is classic loader starvation: a powerful card to which food is brought one plate at a time. The guideline we recorded is: the number of loader processes should be about a quarter of the number of cores, and the card's load should be monitored in the very first minutes.

Why Home Testing Didn't Catch This

Before renting, the entire pipeline was tested at home and worked. But this test proved only one thing: the code was correct. At home, time is free, so no one paid attention to throughput. The test "it works" and the test "it's profitable" turned out to be different, and we didn't do the latter. Before the first training step, about 45 minutes and $3 were spent on machine setup traps, but this is a trifle compared to the rest.

Not all was lost then. The first stage completed entirely, and we saved its weights.

Packing: The Same Work 20 Times Cheaper

We addressed the main reason first. Short dialogues were packed into dense blocks one after another, with an attention mask that prevents adjacent examples within a block from seeing each other. The box is now filled to the brim, with almost no empty space: the proportion of useful tokens increased from 5% to 99%. Recalculation showed that the second stage in its old form would have cost $331–413, but now it costs $14–17.

A disclaimer is needed here: the left column in the graph is not a fact but a calculation. We, of course, did not continue calculating in the old way to verify.

Five Machines in a Day

Encouraged, I launched the second attempt with packing, and overnight and during the day from September 1st to 2nd, we had to rent 5 machines in a row. Each died before it could finish its work, and we had to pay for each.

The first of them, for 20 minutes, stood on that very setup trap that had already cost us once. The lesson was recorded in the report, but not in the code, and a report entry is not a fix. Then this machine crashed at step 50 out of 95. A recent fix enabled an intermediate check that required about 160 GB of memory on a 141 GB card. In the first run, this check never had time to start, so no one saw it.

Another machine was ruined by an old marker file "run failed," left on the persistent volume from a previous attempt. The watchdog, which I'll discuss later, read it, believed it, and shut down a perfectly healthy training run at step 80 out of 95, 4 minutes before the stage ended.

That's when I saw the pattern: the training itself never failed. Everything around it failed, i.e., the launch scripts, checks, and watchdogs. Each of these components was tested separately, but the entire chain was never run through.

Rehearsal at Home

Thus, a full rehearsal before renting was introduced: the same chain, the same watchdogs, the same stage transitions, but with a tiny model on a home card and for free. One such rehearsal on September 5th took 4.7 hours and caught 6 defects. The most unpleasant one was in gradient accumulation: due to how the model wrapper handled it, the actual training speed in the first stage was 16 times higher than set. Gradient clipping masked this error, so the loss looked quite decent, and we wouldn't have noticed it from the graphs. In the cloud, we would have paid for each of the six defects.

The Most Expensive Habit: Shutting Down the Machine on Any Error

But the most expensive thing that day was not the code, but our habit. For any error, we did the same thing: shut down the machine, fix it, and redeploy everything. A new machine reinstalls the environment, re-downloads tens of gigabytes of weights, and the meter runs all this time. And along with the machine, the logs disappear, so the cause has to be guessed later, and sometimes you have to pay for another machine to reproduce it.

We formulated the rule that same day: don't shut down, but fix on the spot and restart the process on the same machine. Like a flat tire: you patch it by the roadside, not abandon the bike and go buy a new one. The trouble is that this rule made it into the code an hour later than it should have. The fifth machine reached 65% of the second stage, ran for 4.35 hours at $19.96, and crashed. The rescuer, who was supposed to retrieve the logs, first removed the pause before shutdown, and there was no copying program on the machine. The machine disappeared in minutes, and I still don't know why it crashed.

A similar story happened with side comparative measurements. The program saw an old result file on the disk, decided it had already downloaded the new one, and shut down the machine along with the actual result. After that, it became clear that old markers and old files on the persistent volume are a separate class of errors, and now everything that signals the run status is tied to a specific launch.

How It Looked Afterward

But then the rules started to pay off. The second version run with packing and the "fix on the spot" rule completed both stages in one go and cost about $41 in total, whereas the first time we paid $53 for just 15% of the second stage. The next, trial training became the first real test of the rule. A short test directly on the machine found 3 defects for $1.5, we fixed them on the spot, and the entire job took 2.81 hours and $12.91 without a single incident.

Watchdogs: Mistakes in Both Directions

We call a separate program that monitors training, saves results, and shuts down the machine to avoid unnecessary meter running a "watchdog." Almost every mistake it made cost money, and it erred in both directions: sometimes too lenient, sometimes too strict.

On September 6th, the third version training on B300 finished, but the watchdog didn't notice: it had quietly broken twice that day and hadn't reported it to anyone. The machine, at $7.89 per hour, sat idle for 30–40 minutes with a ready result until I checked myself what was happening, costing $4–5 in vain. After that, before each launch, we started giving the watchdog a drill: artificially trigger an event and see if the signal gets through. Towards the end of the run, we now also check the machine manually. From the same story, a separate conclusion emerged: the "run completed" marker doesn't mean the result is safe. It's safe when it's downloaded and verified.

On September 24th, the watchdog, on the contrary, overdid it. It estimated card load based on measurements every 5 minutes and in the last measurements saw 94%, 0%, 100%, and 0%. Both zeros occurred during save pauses when the card was legitimately waiting for the disk. On average, it came out to 48% with a threshold of 50%, and 28 seconds before the end of training, the watchdog saved the checkpoint and destroyed the machine. The weights themselves survived, all 51.6 GB, but the checkpoint's service files turned out to be zero length, and we had to spin up a separate machine to save everything. The error was in the logic itself: card load cannot distinguish between a freeze and a save, but step count can. Now the condition looks something like this:

# freeze = card is idle AND steps have stopped advancing hung = gpu_util_avg < 0.5 and step_now == step_at_last_check

During saving and at the finish line, the card is perfectly entitled to rest, but a real freeze is indicated by the steps not advancing.

The question of where the watchdog lives also remained. A watchdog on the rented machine itself dies with it, i.e., exactly when it's needed most. Therefore, it now lives at home and accesses the machine from the outside.

Limits: How a Healthy Run Was Almost Killed

The story with money is similar. The initial logic was simple: reach the limit, save, and shut down. On September 2nd, the watchdog with a $36 limit at step 10 out of 1875 extrapolated the run cost to $35–38 and began saving a perfectly healthy training run. We managed to stop it, but it became clear that a hard dollar limit is dangerous: at the tenth step, extrapolation knows almost nothing about the real speed, and if the limit triggers closer to the end, it will kill an almost finished job, and everything already paid for will be lost. Therefore, now, when approaching the limit, the system first saves everything that has been done and only then shuts down.

How Cost is Calculated Now

The estimate for the first run was off by a factor of 20, and it wasn't our only miscalculation. We recorded the second version in the report as $32 because we only calculated training hours. The balance showed $41. The machine ran for almost 9 hours, not 7, and all this time it also paid for disks, so the listed $3.59/h for the card actually turned into about $4.6 for the entire machine. The bill comes not for training, but for the machine's life from creation to deletion, including setup, downloading weights, and uploading results.

Therefore, the estimate is now built on the actual number of steps with the real length of packed blocks, and is verified against the actual speed within the first minutes of operation. If there's a noticeable discrepancy, we address it before the run goes into hours. For scale: the third version on B300 cost $79.31 for 123 million tokens, the fourth on MI300X about $41 for approximately 200 million, and another fine-tuning cycle now costs about $10–12. And when 4 more launches failed on October 5th, each cost about half a dollar: a failure caught at the start costs pennies, not a whole run.

Measurement Errors

These missteps cost almost no money, but they are more insidious. An error in renting is at least visible on the bill, while a measurement error gives a confident but simply incorrect figure. Some of these stories were already in the series. In the first article, the data doubled, but the error didn't shift. In the second, a repetition ban corrupted live speech. In the third, truncation ate the first word, and the breath detector found them where they weren't. In the fourth, my prediction about synthetics turned out the opposite, and dialogues that the instruments deemed clean were rejected by a native speaker. Here I'll add what I haven't written about yet.

Once, a recognizer showed an error of about 100% on a book. It looked like the data was garbage, but the reason was almost comical: the model responded in Latin script, while the book text was in Cyrillic, and WER counted every word as an error. Before measuring anything, both texts must be brought to the same alphabet, using the official transliterator, not by eye.

Another time, synthesis produced a pitch outlier twice out of two, and I based a data rework on this. When we ran not two, but 125 duplicates, there were 0 outliers. Generation is stochastic, and two samples tell us almost nothing about it. A similar trap awaited with the pronunciation of proper names, only there it wasn't about the number of repetitions, but about what to repeat. The defect wasn't found on 60 names in one phrase with 5 duplicates, although in reality, it occurred in about half of the phrases. It was worth taking 4 names in 8 different phrases, and it immediately appeared, p = 0.0013. You need to vary the factor the defect depends on, not the one that's easier to iterate through.

Automation made another mistake for us. The setting "keep only the last N checkpoints" deleted the most successful one, and two training runs lost their best moment. The checkpoint that is best on validation must be kept separate from rotation.

And the most unpleasant one was the last. 68% of the text in the old validation set was repeated in the training data, so some figures for the third version were inflated. It's like giving a student the same problems on an exam that they solved in class: the grade comes out higher than their knowledge. We assembled a clean validation set from works that are not present anywhere else in the data.

What Has Changed

Putting it all together, the picture is this. Initially, almost every launch failed somewhere, but now failures are rare and cheap.

Before renting, the entire chain undergoes a free rehearsal at home with a tiny model, and not only its functionality but also its cost is checked. The estimate is calculated based on packed tokens and actual steps, verified against the first minutes of operation, and tracked by the machine's life, not training hours. Checkpoints are saved by the hour, not by steps, and to local disk, and the loader is matched to the number of cores. The watchdog lives outside the rented machine, undergoes a drill before launch, and distinguishes between a freeze and a save pause, and the system at the limit first saves the result and only then shuts down. We no longer shut down the machine on error but fix it on the spot, so a failure now costs a couple of dollars, not a whole run. And we only trust the price list after we've managed to get the card.

A cheap cycle doesn't mean a successful one, though. The version with another portion of data in a blind comparison (I explained how it works in the first article) performed 6:5 against the previous one, meaning no difference, and it wasn't put into production. But such a failure now costs $10, not $53, and after it, you have a complete report with logs, not a machine that disappeared along with the cause.

What's Next

We are not abandoning cloud training. We are currently in the data collection phase, and we will continue to train on rented machines, only now each run is significantly cheaper and doesn't break down for no reason. And the latest version of the recognizer has already turned out better than the previous one: it's more accurate on test transcriptions and won more often in the same blind comparison.

From now on, everything hinges not on hardware or money, but on live conversational speech transcribed by humans. We've learned to count video cards, but models still lack live language.
