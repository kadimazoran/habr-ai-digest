And now, to the point. At my last medical check-up, my blood sugar was found to be noticeably elevated. A follow-up test a couple of months later showed an absolutely similar excess, meaning an unhealthy (in the literal sense of the word) trend had emerged. Not that I was particularly surprised, considering I've loved sweets my entire conscious life, and in recent years, I've often used sweets to cope with stress (as you can imagine, the last 6–7 years have been nothing but sources of stress). However, I want to preserve what's left of my health, so to avoid irreversible consequences (in the form of diabetes), I started to drastically reduce my sugar intake. In itself, giving up sugar is not a problem. Fortunately, there are plenty of substitutes available now – choose one you like and that is scientifically approved, and eat to your heart's content. But there's a nuance – hardly anyone eats pure sugar by the spoonful. It usually enters our bodies through other products.

And this is where things get interesting. Facing the need to completely give up sugar for the first time in my life, I quickly realized that doing so without breaking old habits (especially snacking) wasn't that easy. There are dozens of sugar substitutes, but many of them are actually quite harmful, capable of ruining health no worse than "white death" (and perhaps even faster). The problem was exacerbated by the fact that manufacturers like to disguise all sorts of junk under fancy marketing names, hiding the real composition of what they sell us. So, simply buying a "sugar-free" product is completely insufficient; you need to dig deeper.

At first, I took the brute-force approach: for the first six months, I actively bought products on marketplaces that seemed safe (in terms of sugar content) and literally tested them on myself. Some turned out to be quite good, others clearly had a negative impact on my health. In short, I gradually came to realize that "not all yogurts are equally healthy," and I needed to delve deeper into the topic of sugar "substitutes."

Well, what are neural networks for, if not for processing large amounts of information? Then I took a not-so-easy (but most reliable) path. I started searching marketplaces for various products labeled "sugar-free" – candies, cookies, even condensed milk. Abbreviations used: GI – Glycemic Index (the lower it is, the better; sugar GI = 100), ADI – Acceptable Daily Intake (indicates how much of a product can be consumed daily without harm to health).

High-Risk Zone: Carcinogenicity and Toxicity

22. Sodium Cyclamate (E952). GI 0. Banned in the USA/EU due to old data on carcinogenicity in animals (Fitzhugh, Science, 1965), although modern EFSA/IARC assessments do not confirm the risk to humans when adhering to ADI. But the reputation of this outdated sweetener is questionable, so – last place in the ranking.

21. Saccharin (E954). GI 0. As far back as the last century, it was suspected of being oncogenic. Removed from the NTP (1993) list of carcinogens, but has a negative impact on the microbiome and glucose tolerance (Suez et al., Nature, 2014). Bitter aftertaste. Best avoided.

20. Sucralose (E955). Considered a safe sweetener in most countries, but there are recent studies (Nature Communications, 2023) showing genotoxicity of its metabolites (DNA damage), and destruction of the gut microbiome. EFSA (European Food Safety Authority) has drastically reduced the ADI (Acceptable Daily Intake) to 0.2 mg/kg (2023), which sucralose manufacturers vehemently dispute. The decision is yours, of course, but it's one of the last places in the ranking.

19. Aspartame (E951). GI 0. IARC (International Agency for Research on Cancer) classifies it as a possible carcinogen of group 2B (2023). When heated, it breaks down into phenylalanine, aspartic acid, and methanol (not formaldehyde in significant quantities). Contraindicated for phenylketonuria.

Zone of Deception and Hidden Health Threats: Sugar Spikes and Liver Strikes

18. Maltodextrin. GI: 85–105+ (higher than regular sugar!). This is not a sweetener in the usual sense, but a starch hydrolysate. It is absorbed instantly, causing a sharp (!) spike in glucose and insulin. Regular consumption contributes to the development of insulin resistance, visceral obesity, and inflammation. Often disguised in "sports" or "low sugar" products, where it's added for volume, ignoring the glycemic response. Side effects: rapid hunger after eating (due to sugar crash), bloating (if residual enzyme activity exists), microbiome imbalance (rapid fermentation in the upper intestines). In short, the worst choice among carbohydrates. If your goal is weight or diabetes control, avoid it completely. It's even worse than regular sugar substitutes because it doesn't provide sweetness proportional to its energy but causes significant metabolic harm.

17. Concentrated Fructose Syrups. This includes: high-fructose corn syrup (HFCS), agave nectar/syrup, carob syrup, industrial Jerusalem artichoke syrup. All these products have a low GI (15–30), which creates a false sense of security, but their biochemical impact on the body is identical. The main component is free fructose (up to 70–90%). Fructose is metabolized exclusively in the liver, bypassing insulin regulation. Excess fructose is converted into triglycerides directly in liver cells, forming adipose tissue even in thin people ("skinny fat"). This is a direct path to non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). Fructose metabolism in the liver also depletes ATP stores and increases uric acid levels, blocking purine excretion. This, in turn, can lead to the development of gout. Moreover, fructose does not suppress ghrelin (hunger hormone) and does not stimulate leptin (satiety hormone) effectively, so people overeat unnoticed.
Regarding individual fructose syrups, the picture is as follows. Corn syrup is the cheapest and most common source of health problems from soda and fast food. Agave is positioned as "healthy" but contains more fructose than corn syrup. Carob: rich in polyphenols and fiber, which slightly softens the blow, but the sugar concentration remains high. Jerusalem artichoke (industrial): inulin is hydrolyzed to fructose/glucose, and it loses the prebiotic properties of the pure root.
In short, fructose syrups, when consumed regularly, are worse for the liver and metabolism than white sugar. Use only occasionally and in small doses (<10 g/day).

16. Trehalose. GI ≈65–70. Essentially, another type of sugar (a disaccharide of two glucose molecules). A Nature (2018) study linked it to increased virulence of C. difficile. This bacterium can cause severe colon inflammation (colitis) and diarrhea. It induces autophagy (a plus), but is useless as a substitute due to its high GI.

15. Isomaltooligosaccharide (IMO). GI ≈25–35. The FDA removed it from the "dietary fiber" category due to its actual glycemic response. According to marketers, it's a good sugar substitute. But in practice, it's readily broken down in the small intestine into glucose, causing a spike in blood sugar and insulin comparable to maltitol. It also causes significant gas, bloating, rumbling, and a mild laxative effect (especially at doses over 15–20 g at once, which is about one protein bar).

14. Maltitol (E965). GI ≈35–52. High insulin response + severe osmotic diarrhea ("bomb" for the gastrointestinal tract). A trap in "sugar-free" chocolates.

13. Sorbitol (E420). GI ≈9. Polyol pathway accumulation in diabetes (cataracts, neuropathy). Pronounced laxative effect.

12. Xylitol (E967). GI ≈7. Anti-cavity plus, but a European Heart Journal (2024, Hazen) study linked high levels to an increased risk of thrombosis. Toxic to dogs.

11. Acesulfame Potassium (E950). GI 0. Recent studies have shown impaired endothelial function of blood vessels in mice (Perry et al., Nature, 2022). Similar studies have not yet been conducted on humans, but the risk cannot be ruled out. Bitter aftertaste.

Zone of Compromise Deception: Conditionally Safe (Require Moderation)

10. Potassium Sorbate (E202). Not a sweetener in terms of taste, but often added to confectionery (e.g., candies) and healthy products as a preservative, as it inhibits mold growth. However, with daily consumption, it suppresses beneficial gut microbiota. Relatively safe in small doses, but if possible, it's best to avoid.

9. Isomalt (E953). [Do not confuse with isomaltooligosaccharide, see above!] GI ≈2–9. Metabolically inert, but causes diarrhea at doses >20–30 g. Good for lozenges, bad for daily consumption in large quantities.

8. Molasses. High GI (55–65), comparable to regular sugar. Essentially a concentrated byproduct of sugar production, a kind of liquid sugar with minerals. Causes a sharp spike in glucose and insulin. High calorie content and liquid form contribute to unnoticed overeating. In short, molasses is better than white sugar (as it contains a significant amount of minerals – iron, calcium, magnesium, potassium, manganese, and vitamin B6), but as a sugar substitute, it loses even to date paste (which has fiber) and honey (with its antibacterial properties). Suitable for occasional use in baking or marinades, but not for daily consumption (e.g., with tea).

7. Date Paste (whole product). GI: 42–55. Mashed dates. Contains fiber, which slows down sugar absorption. An excellent option, but overconsumption acts like regular sugar, with the "bonus" of a laxative effect.

6. Natural Honey. GI: 35–55 (highly dependent on variety: acacia ~32 due to fructose predominance, buckwheat and floral up to 55). From a biochemical standpoint, honey is a concentrated solution of natural sugars. With immoderate consumption, it puts the same strain on the pancreas and liver as regular sugar, contributing to insulin resistance. Critically important rule: when heated above 40–50°C (in hot tea, baking), honey loses all enzymes and antioxidants, turning into ordinary flavored sugar syrup. Strictly forbidden for children under 1 year old due to the risk of infant botulism (natural honey can contain Clostridium botulinum spores, which an infant's immature digestive system cannot handle). Allergic reactions are possible (to pollen). High calorie content and liquid form easily lead to an unnoticed calorie surplus.

5. Erythritol (E968 / Erythritol). GI 0. No diarrhea (absorbed in the small intestine). There have been studies showing a link between erythritol consumption and an increased risk of thrombosis, but causal relationships have not yet been reliably proven; more data is needed (Witkowski, Nature Medicine, 2023). Still one of the best options.

4. Thaumatin (E957). GI 0. Protein extract. Safe. Downside – a long licorice aftertaste.

Safety Zone: Natural and Most Harmless

3. Stevia (Stevioside). GI 0. A long-known natural sweetener. Does not affect insulin/microbiome. Mild hypotensive effect (lowers blood pressure). Downside – a specific aftertaste in cheap versions. Choose highly purified extracts without bitterness.

2. Allulose (D-psicose). GI: 0 (practically not metabolized in the body). Calorie content: ~0.2–0.4 kcal/g (10 times less than sugar). An absolutely safe sugar substitute. Numerous studies (including clinical trials on humans) show that allulose does not affect glucose and insulin levels, does not feed Candida, and does not disrupt the microbiome. Moreover, there is evidence that it can help control weight – it slows down glucose absorption from other foods and enhances fat oxidation. Unlike stevia and erythritol, allulose caramelizes when heated and behaves almost identically to sugar in baking (provides volume, texture, a brown crust). The taste is as close to sugar as possible without a bitter or menthol aftertaste. Side effects: at very high doses (over 30–40 g at once), it can cause mild abdominal discomfort, but the laxative effect is significantly weaker than with polyols (maltitol, sorbitol).

1. Monk Fruit Extract (Luo Han Guo). GI: 0. The absolute champion. Mogrosides in its composition act as powerful antioxidants. Does not cause insulin spikes, does not feed Candida, has no side effects, and its taste is most similar to sugar.

And this is where the "second act of the Merleson ballet" begins. In marketplace listings, the composition can be hidden at the very end of the description, or it can be placed in the "characteristics" section. Manufacturers use different names for the same substances. It's not just that it's hard to remember, it's even tedious to compare with a cheat sheet. And at some point, I decided to give the neural network a new task – to write a browser extension that would analyze the composition and provide a verdict. In fact, the idea of such analysis is in the air – recently, Habr featured a scanner called "Etiketka," which does something very similar, but in a slightly different direction (in offline stores and not just about sugar).

The technical specification was simple – take the above ranking as a basis, parse product pages belonging to the "Food Products" category, analyze the composition, and provide a "verdict" in the form of a badge in a prominent place.
