Who Will Profit from AI Development — and Who Will Pay for It All?

Coatue ($67 billion under management, 25 years in tech investing) released a comprehensive report on the state of the stock market and how AI is shifting the balance of power. They believe we are at the dawn of a new era.

My team and I, as is our tradition, have analyzed the entire report and added our brief comments to the slides. From this analysis, you will understand:

— Why even betting on AI winners can lead to sharp losses?
— What new ways to earn and attract money does the AI era open up?
— How much does it cost to build AI infrastructure, and why loans alone won't suffice?

Public Markets Update (Oct 2026)

To start, an overview of the market: in July 2026, the Momentum strategy—buying recent leaders and betting against laggards—lost about 55% in 5 weeks. This is the deepest drawdown since 2000. Leveraged money and the fact that too many investors made the same bet only amplified the decline.

AI Winners, a selection of stocks of companies that Goldman Sachs estimates will benefit from AI development, have grown by 405% since November 2022 (the launch date of ChatGPT), while AI Losers, stocks of companies whose businesses may be negatively impacted by AI, have grown by only 2%.

Buying AI Winners and simultaneously betting against AI Losers has been a nerve-wracking strategy: it periodically lost between 12% and 23% in short periods, and once showed a 46% loss in 49 days. Enduring such drawdowns is psychologically challenging, although the strategy ultimately yielded +330%.

The reasons for the decline are understandable: high oil prices, high interest rates, and political conflicts continue. Significant risks are seen in AI, ranging from job losses to problems with infrastructure financing (more on this below).

However, there are also reasons for growth, primarily AI agents and rapidly increasing corporate profits.

Previously, technological advancements were depicted as a series of waves: early computers, personal computers, the internet, mobile internet, and cloud services, each with its own leaders. In this narrative, AI appeared as the next big wave.

Now, Coatue proposes viewing AI as the beginning of a new era, comparable in scale to the Industrial Revolution. Just as machines took over physical labor, AI will gradually assume more and more tasks currently performed by humans.

Coatue envisions the future as follows: CPUs (Central Processing Units) execute commands and coordinate the system, while GPUs (Graphics Processing Units) handle AI computations. GPUs are adept at performing numerous calculations simultaneously, which are essential for training and running AI models.

It is expected that both will be important.

The phone will become a remote control for agents that perform necessary tasks on a computer or in the cloud. The primary control method will be voice: we currently type, but we will soon speak commands into our phones.

Tokens are small pieces of data that a model processes or generates. Coatue sees their production as the foundation of the AI economy: it requires land, energy, and chips, and the result is AI's work—answers to questions, code, research, etc.

Since land, electricity, and chips are becoming the primary resources for AI operations, Coatue proposes measuring the scale of AI infrastructure in gigawatts. The available electrical capacity determines how much equipment we can deploy and how much work it can perform.

Chip manufacturers are expected to profit from AI development similarly to how railroads and the oil industry profited from economic growth in the 20th century, as the new infrastructure simply cannot function without them.

To grasp the scale, Coatue compares these industries to GDP: at their peak, railroad revenues accounted for about 9% of US GDP, and expenditures on oil products were 8%. For semiconductors (from which chips are made), this figure has already reached 2%.

The stock market is more volatile than ever: if the current pace continues, there will be approximately 120 days with swings of over 3% and 81 days with swings of over 5% in either direction within a year. This is even more than during the dot-com bubble in the early 2000s!

Coatue studied 38 instances of similar drawdowns in the Momentum strategy since 2000 and examined what happened next.

In approximately 13% of cases, previous market leaders ceded ground to new ones—and the current drawdown most closely resembles such a scenario.

Typically, over 4 years, about a quarter of the top 15 S&P 500 companies change. In Coatue's 2026 scenario, nearly half, 47%, change. However, they have hypothetically included private companies OpenAI and Anthropic with estimated valuations.

Among the giants, chip manufacturers and AI companies have become significantly more prominent: before the launch of ChatGPT, NVIDIA held the 12th position with a market capitalization of $0.4 trillion. By October 2026, it rose to first place with $5.8 trillion.

The question remains: can chips continue to grow faster than the market? Chip manufacturers and their production equipment already account for about 19% of the total value of S&P 500 companies. For comparison, the peak for energy was 26%, for computer hardware manufacturers—15%, and for financial companies—22%.

Memory manufacturers' margins reached 76%, a record high since 2006. This is higher than that of exchanges, payment systems, and major software developers.

During the development of cloud services, infrastructure investments were accompanied by revenue growth: on average from 2014-2019, for every $1 of capital expenditure, there was about $0.60 in annual revenue increase.

In AI, the revenue increase is currently significantly lower relative to infrastructure investments: about $0.21 for every $1 of capital expenditure, compared to approximately $0.60 for cloud services previously. Even in a hypothetical scenario by 2030, the figure only reaches $0.30—hence the question of whether the emergence of AGI (Artificial General Intelligence) can narrow this gap.

The construction of data centers for AI involves investments comparable in scale to railroads and oil and gas infrastructure: the figure has already reached 2.9% of US GDP, compared to 2.1% and 2% at the peaks of previous cycles.

Imagine: by 2028, investments in AI infrastructure will reach $2.8 trillion—which is more than the total annual mortgage lending in the US!

To attract such sums, organizations similar to Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac will be needed. They buy mortgage loans from banks, attracting funds from investors. Banks receive funds back and can issue new loans. According to Coatue, something similar will be required for AI.

The industry definitely needs new financing methods.

Previously, giants paid for data centers and GPUs themselves, but now they attract partners, investors, and creditors. Some finance the buildings, others the equipment, and the giants commit to leasing capacity or provide financial guarantees. This distributes costs among several participants, but the scheme becomes more complex, and their obligations become more tightly intertwined.

Data center - a physical building containing servers, power, cooling, and internet connectivity.
