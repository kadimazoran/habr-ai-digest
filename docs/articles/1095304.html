What Mascot?

I have a business card website on Rust (Leptos + axum), and in the hero section, there's a terminal — the only way to chat with the assistant. The mascot sits on the top edge of the "basement" of this terminal and reacts to the request state: base when idle, read while the request is in flight, thinking while the answer is being typed, smirk when the answer is finished, dissatisfied when the request failed. That is, it's not a decoration, but a state indicator — just in the form of a witch.

Her name is Kiki, and the name is taken from Miyazaki's "Kiki's Delivery Service." This is a reference to the name, not a copy of the character: there's no cat, no red bow, no recognizable dress from it — the heroine is drawn from scratch. There are twelve frames in the set: five went to the site in a 144x192 canvas, the rest are in reserve for new chat reactions.

Experiment: Asking the Model to Create Pixel Art

Before writing the pipeline, I tested the most obvious solution — what if I just ask for it? I had the image_qwen_image_2_1_image_edit template: it takes two images, one as a canvas, the other as a style source. The illustration generated in image_qwen_image_2_1_t2i for this article went to the canvas, and the style was a ready-made pixel art draft from the set.

Density is calculated by drawn pixels: for full-frame images, it's the entire frame; for a sprite, it's only the opaque pixels. If you take all RGB triplets consecutively for a sprite, another one will be added to the count, which is not in the palette: these are zeros under fully transparent pixels. A trifle, but it's on such trifles that they get caught in the section about correct measurements.

The model, which was explicitly asked to remove anti-aliasing, produced more colors than were present in the input: 62,513 versus 46,304. The draft satisfied from the 2.1 set is even poorer — 36,256. The color density of the result is 26 times higher than that of the sprite from the pipeline.

I'll clarify the nature of this comparison, and it's important not to attribute someone else's work to the model here. The sprite has a limited palette by construction, so "26 times" is the scale of the difference, not a competitive result. The substantive part of the measurement is different: the number of colors is not specified by the request. This is not something the generator controls — it draws an image based on a description, and "make exactly 61 colors" is not a prompt parameter. The model limits the palette roughly the same way a camera without color correction filters limits the number of colors in a frame — that is, not at all. Therefore, there's nothing to fix with a prompt: the palette limitation lives in post-processing, and in this set, it's a separate step.

The request "make pixel art", measured numerically

This doesn't mean the model is bad. It does exactly what it's asked, in terms it understands: "flat blocks" for it is a technique within an image, not a storage format. The difference between "looks like pixel art" and "is pixel art" is the difference between an image and data, and it's not covered by a prompt. In short, a sprite differs from such an image not by resolution, but by three properties, and each is verifiable:

  1. One color per pixel. Not "almost one": there can be no intermediate shade between two adjacent pixels.

  2. An integer grid. All pixels lie on a grid with a constant step, not "somewhere a block is 3 pixels, somewhere 5".

  3. Binary alpha. A pixel either exists or it doesn't: a semi-transparent edge falls apart with image-rendering: pixelated.

It is the third property that kills the idea of "generate and just downscale": when downscaling with anti-aliasing, each edge gets its own shade, and the number of colors increases, not decreases. One detail in the drafts is correct, and it wasn't fixed but preserved: their blocks are indeed of different sizes. Large squares on the hat, stripes one sprite pixel wide on the cheek. Pixel art without scale variation looks like downsampling, not drawing.

The Same Thing with Ready-Made Nodes

Before defending my own work, I must acknowledge others'. This machine has five packages for this task, and two of them — comfyui-pixelization and comfy_pixelization — are different projects that register a node with the same name, Pixelization. The two most obvious ones, PixelOE and Krea2 Pixel Art Refiner, were run on the same draft.

Ready-made pixelation nodes vs. pipeline

The result is more honest than I expected: Krea2 Pixel Art Refiner with colors=61 produced exactly 61 unique colors. Half of the task — "make few colors" — is solved by a ready-made node in one go and without my code. The common palette is a separate measurement, and it shows a limitation of the interface, not a discovery. Krea2PixelArtRefiner has six input fields: image, width, height, colors, color_reduction_first, and scale_to_original. There's no way to input a palette — only the number of colors, an integer from 2 to 256. That is, the node cannot accept "these 61" by its design, and a common palette for the set is not expressed in it: it recalculates colors for each frame anew. Therefore, the refiner with colors=61 on two frames of the same character yielded 3 common colors out of 61, the union being 119. "Palette per frame — palette per frame" is not a discovery in itself, it's a definition; what's interesting here is that two frames of the same character diverged so much that only three shades remained common. On the site, this would look like this: the character changes emotion and, along with it, the dress color changes.

It was here that I realized I needed not a pixel filter, but a format: a palette common to the set, and a grid by which the sprite could be redrawn entirely. Palette limitation is a consequence, not a goal. This is the boundary where the filter ends: it will process one image, but not a set.

Five Techniques

Next is what was actually needed. Each technique is described the same way: why, how, what it yielded in numbers, and where it breaks. This is not an architecture, but a list of places where the obvious solution didn't work, and their order is the same as in the pipeline.

The pipeline in its entirety: seven steps from draft to block map

1. Median Block Instead of Average

Why. Need to reduce 1344x1344 to a grid. The obvious approach is to average a 4x4 block, and on a smoothed edge, it gives exactly the dirt we're trying to avoid: the average between a black hat and a light background is gray. How. First, the frame is cropped to the figure's dimensions: pixels whose color distance to the background is greater than 40 are taken, and a frame plus a two-pixel margin for future pixels is built around them. Otherwise, the grid would be calculated on an empty 1344x1344 field. For the read frame, the crop was 887x1188 — after dividing by four, this is the 222x297 sprite. Then, each 4x4 block is collapsed into one sprite pixel using the median separately for each channel: if a block has 12 source pixels belonging to the hat and 4 to the background, the median will remain the hat's color, while the average will shift towards gray. Here and further, "block" refers to a square of the source image, and "pixel" refers to the sprite pixel, the one visible on the screen.

Effect. Exactly fourfold reduction (3.99–4.00 across frames), canvas 217–286 x 235–318. At this stage, a grid appears that was not present in the draft. Where it breaks. Four is a compromise. The finest real detail in the draft — the edge of a buckle and blush stripes — occupies 5–6 source pixels, meaning it falls into one or two sprite pixels at cell=4. cell=2 would give twice the detail, but the sprite would be twice as large, and cell=6 would already eat them.

2. One Palette for the Entire Set

Why. Twelve frames of the same character must look like a single set. If the palette is calculated separately for each frame, the shades will shift: the hat in one frame will be slightly cooler than in another, and the dress will flicker when the emotion changes. How real this is can be seen from the measurement above — the ready-made node produced 3 common colors out of 61 for two frames of the same character.

How. The palette is built using median cut on the combined histogram of all frames and then only expands: a new frame can add a color, but it cannot overwrite existing ones. I also tried K-means and got a characteristic failure: the golden buckle — tens of pixels out of a million, the centroid doesn't notice it, and the buckle becomes gray. Median cut divides by the widest box and preserves such small but distant clusters. Effect. One palette for exactly twelve frames, and a 1.0 match for each: any opaque pixel is exactly equal to one of the entries.

Where it breaks. The limit is not in the palette, but in the format: a sprite is a text grid where a character means a color index, and the alphabet has 62 characters. More than 62 colors don't fit into the sprite, and 61 slots are used in the main set. The closest pair is separated by 25 units, meaning they cannot be merged unnoticed: the next frame with new shades would require removing a shade or expanding the alphabet.

A good example is the frame with the book: the pages turned out gray. Cream #fceacc with chrome 48 is light neutral, which wasn't in the palette: the nearest shade was 45 units away. It had to be expanded: old colors remain verbatim, only accents that the base cannot express are added. Thus, #f5e6cc appeared, and the distance to it is 14.5 units. Both numbers are calculated based on the frozen palette from the repository: the first is if the added color is removed from it, the second is if it's kept.

3. Glow and Shadow: Recolor, Don't Cut

Why. The generator adds a light halo around the silhouette and a soft shadow under the figure. The shadow is not part of the character, but the halo turns into a dirty border along the contour. How. "Glow" is a light and low-saturation pixel: the average of the three channels is above 112, and the difference between the maximum and minimum channel is less than 30. The threshold was not chosen by eye: at 120, gray stripes #747476 (average 117) remained in the hair, and at 112 they disappear, while the highlight on the hat #64636b (average 102) remains.

Next is the main part. The first version made such pixels transparent and created holes in the hair: a stripe trapped between strands disappeared, and the background showed through. Therefore, the solution is split: a pixel on an open edge, where at least 75% of surrounding pixels are free, becomes transparent, while one trapped inside the figure is recolored to the color of the adjacent hair.

Effect. 7,894 pixels of glow and shadow removed across twelve frames. Separately, I checked that the cleaning doesn't create small holes: I went through all transparent areas not reachable from the frame edge and counted those occupying one or two pixels. Across twelve frames and five thresholds from 0.5 to 0.9, there are 0 to 7 such punctures — all seven at the most cautious threshold of 0.9. And if you remove all light pixels indiscriminately, the punctures increase from 6 to 30 per frame, and the largest hole grows to 318 pixels. The difference between "recolor" and "cut" is here.

4. Eyes are Protected by the Only Saturated Green

Why. The rule "remove all light and low-saturation pixels" erases the whites of the eyes and highlights — on a chibi face, this means losing the character. How. The iris is the only saturated green in the frame, and this allows it to be found without markup: a pixel is iridescent if g - max(r, b) > 25. The iris border is expanded by three pixels, and this area is excluded from all cleaning rules — on the satisfied frame, this is 560 pixels. At the top, the brightest warm area is always added: it's needed in case the eye is closed and there's no green in the frame at all (the smirk frame, where it covers the entire face — 4690 pixels).

Effect. 20,388 pixels of glow and shadow protected across twelve frames — from 708 to 5,751 per frame. Of these, light ones, i.e., those that the rule would have eaten without protection, are 848, from 17 to 110 per frame. I re-ran the check to avoid relying on memory: each frame is counted twice, with and without glow cleaning, and pixels within the protected area are compared. Across all twelve frames, there are zero differences. That is, the protection doesn't "reduce damage" but completely covers these 848 pixels. Breakdown of one frame: on satisfied, 960 protected pixels — 560 from the iris border, 400 from the second part of the rule, and 86 light ones on the iris and one in the skin area.

Eye protection: iris mask, skin area, and the result without and with protection

Where it breaks. The rule relies on two adjustments: the iris must be green, and the skin must be light and warm. An emerald brooch or a green background — and the border will cover the wrong thing; dark skin, an animal, or a robot — and the second part of the rule will miss the face entirely. On the satisfied frame, it already misses: 400 out of 960 protected pixels are the golden buckle on the hat because it passes the skin test under all conditions. Its usefulness is one pixel out of four hundred. The numbers here are modest: 848 pixels across twelve frames is not much, and the difference is only visible on the pupil and highlight by eye. But these are exactly the pixels that make a chibi face look alive.

5. Block Map Instead of "By Eye"

Why. The requirement from the task remains: pixels should be of different sizes. I'll clarify the inaccuracy here right away, because any practitioner will latch onto it. The pixels themselves in the sprite are the same size — it has a single grid. What's different in size are the flat areas: sometimes a stripe one pixel wide, sometimes a fill of eight. This is what needs to be measured, otherwise, it's a pointless discussion. How. The frame is broken down into the largest homogeneous squares: if an 8x8 block is entirely one color, it's marked as 8x8 and the algorithm doesn't go inside, the remainder is recursively divided in half down to 1x1. The histogram of these blocks is the answer.

Effect. The proportion of canvas area on average across twelve frames: 8x8 — 41.3%, 1x1 — 25.8%, 2x2 — 21.8%, 4x4 — 11.1%. The variation across frames is from 35 to 54% for large blocks. If the map had degenerated into a single size, this histogram would show it immediately.

Block histogram: 1x1, 2x2, 4x4, 8x8

Where it breaks. The first version degenerated into only 1x1 blocks: the canvas was not a multiple of the block size, and any 2x2 square touched the border at the seams. I had to pad the canvas to a whole number of powers of two with a sentinel and place blocks edge-to-edge. The second trap was that the histogram counted a non-existent size of 0.

What Didn't Work

Downscaling without a palette doesn't work. The first version of the pipeline simply downscaled the image by the grid and got thousands of colors at the output: the smoothed edge breaks down into dozens of shades. Until the step of linking to a common palette appeared, the "sprite" remained an image. cell=6 doesn't save the situation, it ruins it. The hypothesis was that a larger block would give more "honest" pixel art. In practice, facial details blur on a six, and the palette hit the same 62 slots. The set at six was kept as an alternative, but it didn't go on the site.

How to Verify

Verification doesn't require looking at the image. Three conditions are run as tests, and all three are about format, not drawing quality. Each prints a line per assertion and exits with a non-zero return code.

  1. Every opaque pixel equals a palette entry. If even one doesn't match, the pipeline is broken. Currently, 1.0 for all twelve frames.

  2. Alpha takes exactly two values. The appearance of any third value means anti-aliasing has returned somewhere.

  3. The block histogram contains more than one size. If everything degenerated into 1x1 or 8x8, it means either the block map is calculated incorrectly, or the sprite has ceased to be pixel art.

Additionally: within the protected eye area, the frame does not change when glow cleaning is enabled or disabled. This is a check that the cleaning rules do not touch what is protected. It's the only one here that compares two runs of the same frame, not the properties of one run.

How to Replicate

The core of the pipeline is two operations on a numpy array, and both fit into five lines. Below they are in full, without the wrapper. Exactly these two lines turn an image into data.

# 1. 4x4 block -> one pixel. Median, not average: 1. the smoothed edge doesn't pull color into grayness. blocks = arr.reshape(h // 4, 4, w // 4, 4, 3).transpose(0, 2, 1, 3, 4) cells = np.median(blocks.reshape(-1, 16, 3), axis=1) # 2. One color per pixel: the closest palette entry. idx = np.stack([redmean(cells, c) for c in palette]).argmin(0)

redmean is a cheap substitute for Lab distance: sqrt((2 + rm/256)·dr² + 4·dg² + (2 + (255−rm)/256)·db²), where rm is the average red component of the pair. It's calculated without converting to another color space and behaves noticeably closer to human perception than Euclidean distance in RGB. Everything else — cleaning single pixels, silhouette, glow, block map — is an overlay on these two steps. It works in pure Python with Pillow and numpy, no graphics editor needed.

The entire pipeline is in an open repository — github.com/aigen31/deepseek-pixel-art-generation. It contains the seven steps from the techniques above, two drivers (by reference and by drafts), a frozen set palette, and 34 checks that print one line per assertion and exit with a non-zero return code. There are two dependencies: Pillow and numpy.

Where is the Measurement, and Where is the Explanation

Measured: number of unique colors in drafts and sprites, canvas sizes, palette match, alpha binarity, silhouette IoU, edge recall and precision, block histogram, number of pixels removed, number of small punctures at five cleaning thresholds, number of punctures with naive removal, size of the largest closed transparent area at each threshold, number of light pixels under eye protection, zero differences in the protected eye area, palettes of ready-made nodes for two frames. Explanation, not measurement: that the median "holds the edge better than the average," that the rule about 75% free pixels separates the halo from the stripe in the hair, and that the brightness threshold of 112 is the correct boundary for this model. All this is consistent with the measurements, but it's not isolated by separate experiments. Thresholds were chosen by trial and error on four out of twelve frames.

Not tested: how the pipeline will behave on a different model and a different character. The thresholds in the code are arguments with default values chosen for this set, so they will have to be re-selected. But two places are rigidly tied to Kiki herself: the iris is sought as the only saturated green, and the face as light warm skin. A character with blue or brown eyes, an animal, a robot, or a person with dark skin will receive no protection at all: the mask will return empty, and the glow rule will eat the whites of the eyes along with the highlights. This is the first thing that will need to be rewritten, and both functions are marked in the repository.

On the Site: 144x192

A separate story is how the sprite gets onto the page, and here I must honestly admit one place where my integer scaling rule is not met. The first solution is a common canvas of 144x192, frames aligned at the bottom. If the scale is calculated for each frame separately, the character will change size when the emotion changes, so the canvas, scale, and alignment are calculated by one script, not by a person in a graphics editor. The pivot point for the frames is different because the poses are different: for base, the silhouette falls from the 126th pixel of the canvas to the 46th, and the bottom edge runs along the hips. Therefore, each frame has its own --seat-nudge offset — without it, frames with raised knees appear to be floating in the air, and when the emotion changes, the witch jumps a few pixels.

The second is sizes on the page: 144, 120, and 96 px wide, and this is the only place where the integer scaling rule is not met. Only the first is a multiple of the canvas: 120 is 5/6, and 96 is 2/3. The intention was for the file to match the display area: then the browser wouldn't recalculate anything, and Pagespeed wouldn't see "image larger than needed." This only happened for one size out of three. I measured on a live page: with a 1280 px window, the file is 144 px and the area is 144 px, i.e., one-to-one; at 1100 px, the area is already 120x160, at 900 px — 96x128, and the file is still 144x192. For two out of three sizes, the browser scales the image itself, and does so exactly as it shouldn't: 144 → 120 and 144 → 96 are fractional scales, at which image-rendering: pixelated discards some pixels, and adjacent ones become of different widths. Pagespeed on these same two sizes gets exactly what it penalizes: an image 20% and 50% larger than the area.

This can be fixed simply: three files and srcset — 144x192, 120x160, and 96x128, each exactly the size it's displayed at, and each a multiple of the same grid. The common divisor of 144, 120, and 96 is 24, meaning it should be drawn on a 24x32 grid. The cost is facial detail: the sprite is now nine times wider. The second option is cheaper: keep the grid large and remove 120 from the list, showing the frame at 144 and 96. Neither has been done yet, and I won't write "all sizes are multiples of the canvas."

And about the files themselves, because two different solutions are merged here. The 144x192 size is the first, and it's conscious: for the widest breakpoint, the file is matched exactly to the display area. Averaging during downscaling is the second, and this is worse. The pipeline outputs a canvas of 219–237 pixels wide, and the assembly script fits the frame into the canvas with a scale of min(192 / height, 144 / width) — this is about 0.61, — and each canvas pixel becomes the average of the source pixels that fell into it. It is this filter, not the chosen size, that eats the palette and grid: in the uploaded base.png, there are 3,113 unique colors instead of 58. The alpha, however, remains binary; the script fixes it separately. I checked that the problem is with the filter, not the size, on the same code: if the canvas matches the dimensions of the widest and tallest figure in the set, the scale is exactly 1, and the script copies pixels one-to-one — 58 colors at input, 58 at output. Currently, the site displays an image, not a sprite, and this is a consequence of the frame not being scaled by an integer factor, not because the file is small.

Who Assembled This

The workflow is as follows. I launch DeepSeek Harness and state the task in words; inside, the DeepSeek Flash V4.1 model runs on thinking low. Then a cycle: the agent deploys the environment, finds the necessary skills via the find-skills skill, reads them, writes code, and shows it to me. I look at the result and say what's wrong — the cycle repeats. I haven't written a single line of the pipeline: my part is reconnaissance, task setting, and acceptance; its part is implementation.

Skills here are not prompts for images, but instructions for the agent. pixel-art-redraw describes how to read a reference and what constitutes real pixel art; pixel-art-sprites describes how sprite sheets and palettes are structured. From these, the agent takes criteria by which it then checks its own work. The difference is fundamental: without these criteria, the agent considers the task complete as soon as the image looks similar.

What this changes in practice. The agent performs well on the part that can be verified by code; it makes mistakes precisely where there is no verification. Holes in the hair from the first version of glow cleaning were not found by tests, but by eye: palette match and alpha binarity were fine because both checks look at the format, not at whether it improved. Automation doesn't catch this. This is exactly where I was required not to write code, but to look — and there turned out to be more such places in the project than I would have liked.

Therefore, the only thing that truly maintains quality here is that the result is data: a grid of characters, a palette, and the three checks above. They can be run as tests. An image cannot be tested.

Results

A real pixel art differs from a "pixelated" image not by style, but by representability: if a sprite is stored as a grid of indices plus a palette, any requirement for it — "the same witch, night palette, twice as large" — becomes a deterministic operation that can be verified pixel by pixel. An image doesn't allow this, and no resolution will fix it. What is valuable and noteworthy here: the palette is limited by the grid's alphabet, not by the generator's capabilities, and this ceiling is set by the storage format I chose, not by pixel art as a discipline: if you write an index with two characters instead of one, the limit goes up to thousands of colors. In the current alphabet, 61 out of 62 slots are occupied, and the closest pair of colors is too far apart to merge them unnoticed — so a new emotion with new shades cannot be added in one go: you'll have to decide what to sacrifice.

The neural network, meanwhile, hasn't gone anywhere — it remains a draft. A good draft: twelve frames of the same character, from a smile to flying on a broom, I wouldn't have done this by hand. But between "drawing a draft" and "getting a sprite" lies this entire article.

Live wallpapers for Wallpaper Engine: 640x360 logical grid, integer scaling

They are assembled according to the same rules as the sprites, and this is evident in the solutions. The entire scene is drawn on one logical grid of 640x360 and scaled by a single integer transformation: 2x for 720p, 3x for 1080p, 4x for 1440p, 6x for 4K. The logical pixel always remains an integer number of screen pixels; no layer is resampled. This is exactly the same prohibition on fractional scaling that is only met for one out of three sizes on the website — here it is always met.

The wallpapers are 16 PNG layers, not a finished image: background, glass, halo, light on the floor, shadow, girl, highlight on the hat, and an eyelid patch the size of two eyes. Five animations are changes in brightness and transparency of these layers, not video. There are two palettes, and they are switched by the wallpaper's properties: one taken from the reference and reduced to 128 colors. In addition to the palette, the properties include glow brightness, terminal speed, blinking, and shadow trembling.

The assembly is deterministic: no model output, no randomness, no timestamps. Therefore, honest verification after editing is to reassemble everything and see that git status remains empty. A non-empty diff is a change. A separate script runs 87 checks in about a second. The wallpapers are located separately: github.com/aigen31/wallpaper-engine-kiki-character.
