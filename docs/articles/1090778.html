We have been involved in complex analytics for over 25 years, where neural networks and machine learning were already common working tools, and for several years now, we have been implementing AI in real client business tasks – from data cleaning to support automation. Therefore, I know that what remains behind the scenes is what determines the real cost: AI generation costs pennies compared to the budget of traditional development, but a single hallucination can undermine client trust and add work for lawyers; the model generates a response very quickly, and the business process it "optimized" will have to be restored, possibly for several days. Something tells me that if such advertising generation were turned into a technical specification, all that would be left would be to calculate how many times the project budget has increased.

Let me preface this by saying that I am not a skeptic. AI is an excellent tool that we use in our product and in client projects. But AI has its limitations, and we all seem to try not to think about the fact that even if we don't account for costs in the demo, we have to account for them in production.

Consider this article an invitation to a conversation for everyone who has gone from "let's just connect AI" to "why is everything crashing on Tuesdays?" And for everyone who is not in a hurry to release such solutions into production because they clearly see the hidden costs and know the price of error.

Costs That Aren't in the Demo

It occurred to me that AI generation can be compared to the ISS, whose modules, in principle, can be of any shape, because in a vacuum there is no air resistance – hence, no costs for aerodynamics. But an airplane in the shape of the ISS would fall apart or burn up in the dense layers of the atmosphere.

Apologists of "pure" AI suggest we fly above the Earth as if in a vacuum. They wonder why their solutions don't work in production. They don't work because in the real atmosphere, with environmental resistance in the form of Federal Law 152, strict business processes, timeouts, and clients who leave after a series of errors, our "airplane" starts to burn.

Let's break down what this "atmosphere" consists of.

When I write about environmental "resistance" and associated costs, I don't mean the cost of tokens or subscription fees, but the costs generated by AI generation itself, which are known to everyone who uses AI.

  • The same prompt that returns valid JSON today might return something else tomorrow. The format fluctuates, the parser crashes, and as a result, engineers spend hours writing strict validation schemes.

  • The model can accidentally "blurt out" personal data. This risk is far from theoretical: Roskomnadzor is already issuing orders for the transfer of personal data to public neural networks. Fines under Federal Law 152 for leaks can reach 10–15 million rubles, and since May 2025, turnover fines of up to 3% of revenue will be in effect.

  • The model doesn't know the regulations and can therefore "optimize" a solution and easily miss a mandatory step, such as writing to a database or sending a notification to security, because for it, the condition "send notification" is just text, not a mandatory action with legal consequences. And given local optimization, mandatory steps outside the prompt do not exist for the model. The result is not only inconsistent data and the inability to conduct an audit but also violations of compliance and associated fines, and even disruptions of business processes because missed steps are not recorded.

  • The model strives to maximize the given KPI, ignoring common sense, for example, sending dozens of push notifications a day for "engagement." As a result, you lose not pennies for sending, but a client who simply "uninstalls" or blocks the annoying application.

  • The model can "optimize" a contract and remove a force majeure clause because it has no concept of the cost of error, and lawyers will have to deal with the consequences.

In each of these cases, the model worked *technically*, but there's nothing around it to make it consider the associated costs.

We recently completed a project to clean client data.

It would seem like an ideal task for AI.

First, we tried to get by with regular expressions and strict rules, but we couldn't handle it due to the large volume of unstructured text. Then we decided: to hell with rules, let's do it all with AI. We ran it on a real volume and measured the speed: at the pace of a "pure" model, a process that took one day stretched to two months. 50 times slower! Of course, no one waited two months – we shut down the experiment.

We rolled back and divided responsibilities. Everything that can be clearly described by rules – field validation, routing, handling standard cases – was left to strict rules. Only what the rules couldn't handle was given to the models: reading non-standard text and extracting meaning from it.

We expected the rules to do their job, and the model to do its. But it turned out that the parts *reinforce* each other.

As soon as we removed the model, the rules got bogged down in non-standard text: everything that didn't fit the template fell out of processing. If we removed the rules, the model started to "drift": missing cases, producing unstable results, and the process became unmanageable. The system only worked properly when both parts performed their tasks: the rules filtered out the model's errors, and the model handled cases for which a template couldn't be written.

That's when we became finally convinced that "pure" AI in corporate tasks is a "ship" designed for "vacuum" that will fall apart upon entering the "atmosphere."

We tried to formulate a rule for ourselves for using AI: we try to *do strictly everything that can be formalized and where the cost of error is high, and give everything that requires interpretation, language, and variability to the model*. I am convinced that the boundary should be drawn precisely this way – not based on "what's trendier," but on the cost of error and the degree of variability. It turns out that AI is not removed from the system but occupies *its* place – where the creative component is needed. A strict wrapper (often built on Low-Code or classic pipelines) is built around the AI, which "slaps the AI's hands" where the "hands" reach out to disrupt the process.

It should be noted that the wrapper also incurs costs. We have to consider engineers' working time, maintenance, and slower performance. One might even think that we are trading a needle for a bobbin, and creative chaos for bureaucracy.

Yes, exactly. And the costs, it seems, are not a bug but a feature. They cannot be eliminated, but they can be moved. Where? I found the answer in economics – more on that below.

Why Costs Don't Disappear

By the end of the 19th century, neoclassical economists – Walras, Marshall, Pareto – brought the ideal market models to mathematical perfection, within which all transactions went smoothly: find, agree, receive, without incurring any additional resources (frictionless transaction). But in reality, they didn't work perfectly because *homo economicus* lived in such models – a person who makes decisions without costs. By the way, it was these models that collapsed in the 20th century.

They knew about production costs – raw materials, labor, capital, of course. But about the costs of the transaction itself – that finding a counterparty, agreeing, and monitoring execution also costs money – no one thought. Until Ronald Coase, in 1937, asked the question: **why do companies exist at all if the market is so efficient?** His answer: because market transactions are not free, and every deal has a price – search, negotiation, contract, control, protection of rights. He called this **transaction costs**.

In 1991, 54 years later, he received the Nobel Prize for this idea. Because it turned out that ignoring transaction costs is like ignoring friction in physics.

Later, economists Douglas North and John Wallis showed that transaction costs are in principle unavoidable: they don't disappear, they *move*.

And here, I admit, I looked at our projects in a new light. It turns out that when we build a wrapper around a model, we are not fighting costs – we are *moving* them. From a probabilistic model, which by its nature cannot be made predictable, to a deterministic framework where they know how to work with them: visual business processes, validators, limits, routing, i.e., the very "specially trained managers," only in the form of code and rules.

This is how our Loginom platform came about in complex projects. We didn't create it specifically for LLMs, but in practice, it all happened naturally: whatever we launched, it ended up being a combined solution. We shifted the costs of control and compliance to a strict, visually designed scenario, and left the models only with what they do best.

And only later did I read Coase and learned that we had been doing exactly what he described all along: not eliminating the total damage, but looking for a structural alternative where it could be controlled and minimized.

Hard and Soft: "Composite" AI

This search for a balance between a strict framework and a variable model is what we eventually called the composite approach. By then, I had seen enough of our systems to notice a recurring pattern: inside, there are always two fundamentally different parts: a *hard* one, where you put in a "sheep" at the input and get "sausage" at the output (and never a "motorcycle"), and a *soft* one, where the AI works.

But I couldn't reconcile these two parts in my head for a long time. They contradict each other, and I constantly stumbled over this contradiction. It looked like a dead end: you can't strengthen both sides at once, nor can you abandon either of them. I thought about it for a long time and couldn't find a word for what emerged at the intersection. I called it "wrapper," "framework," "the thing that slaps hands" – but these were names of parts, not the whole.

And then I started noticing that we weren't alone with this idea.

I read a paper by researchers from Intel Labs, presented at ICML 2025. They approach it from a completely different angle – studying model safety. And they arrive at the same conclusion: no single safety tool provides sufficient guarantees in isolation. A composition of heterogeneous methods that cover each other's weaknesses is needed. They call this *Composite AI Safety*. And although they are thinking about model safety, and we are thinking about business process architecture, the principle rhymes: one tool doesn't solve it.

To mathematically prove the effectiveness of their approach, the authors took a classic computer vision task – object detection. And look at the results:

  • YOLOv8: 0.068 billion parameters, 71.5 FPS, AP on LVIS = 7.1

  • Florence 2: 0.822 billion parameters, 2.9 FPS, AP on LVIS = 2.3

  • Gemini 2.0 Flash: AP on LVIS = 4.9

  • GenerateU: 0.896 billion parameters, 1.5 FPS, AP = 25.5

(FPS – frames per second, speed: higher = better; AP – Average Precision, average precision across masks on LVIS: higher = better; parameters: lower = better; LVIS – segmentation dataset with ~1200 classes.)

A compact specialized detector (YOLO) – 12 times smaller and 25 times faster – outperforms large generative models on a specific task. And it does so not because it's "smarter," but because it doesn't try to be everything to everyone.

But GenerateU shows the real breakthrough: a model where a detection network is connected to a language one (i.e., strict specialization plus language) – a hybrid.

I understood this for myself this way: a universal "brain" is good in demos, but in a specific task, a pipeline with one creative insert wins, i.e., rigidity where rigidity is needed, and variability where it's impossible without it.

It turns out that the composite principle works not only in our analytics. Model safety, computer vision, business processes – the logic is the same everywhere: no single tool solves the problem completely and cannot "ideally" cover all risks. There will always be costs, but some ways of solving the problem are more expensive than possible alternatives – economists like to talk about opportunity cost, as it is the foundation of their science. Therefore, we need to find a combination of tools that is cheaper and more reliable.

And if something can be done deterministically, there's no point in offloading it to AI and hoping the model will "guess" correctly. Why, for example, explain to the model the rule that minors cannot be given loans (for which you can get in trouble) if it can be explicitly written? In one case, we hope the model understands correctly, and in the other, we are completely sure that everything is correct. If we are to consider costs, why increase the risk?

Looking at how this works in practice, I am increasingly convinced that composite AI is precisely the most alive and working technology, and very soon, when we simply say "AI," we will automatically mean it. Pure models will remain in beautiful demos, and only composites will work in real tasks. But this composite, like any real system, has its price. And here we come to the main question: *who exactly pays this price* and how are the inevitable costs distributed?

The Price of an Architectural Compromise, or Who Pays for a Hallucination?

In AI projects, a model error is not a local but a systemic problem. Everyone suffers from a hallucinating system: the client who receives an incorrect answer, the lawyer who deals with the consequences, the business losing trust, and so on. But the task in designing the architecture is not to find a "scapegoat" to dump this burden on, but to find a compromise where the total losses (time to create a strict framework, risk of errors, support costs) are minimized. There are always many options, and they all cost something.

There is no ideal option where everything is free and risk-free. But we can choose between chaotic damage and cleaning up "tails" or a conscious architecture that will minimize damage to a reasonable minimum and increase efficiency.

If we consider the popularity of AI from the perspective of economic theory, it is undoubtedly a new structural alternative for solving many problems. They are performed faster and cheaper. Yes, there are questions about quality, nuances may not be taken into account, but even considering the costs of checks and verification, the benefit is still obvious. However, we must not forget the costs that appear where, at first glance, there seemed to be none. In reality, they have always been there, but the previous methods of solving them have stopped working.

Why are there so many questions about AI safety, especially when it comes to agents? Haven't humans made mistakes? Of course, they have! But the point is that before, a *employee*, along with the right to make decisions, *received* *responsibility* for them as a package. There are specific ways of influence, *up to criminal ones*. And in the case of AI agents, the ability to perform actions has appeared, but the responsibility for the consequences has hung in the air.

And as always, costs, in this case related to responsibility, cannot be eliminated. The only option is to move them somewhere. All the way back: strict rules, isolation of work, restrictions. The idea of composite AI is precisely to minimize the losses inherent in each method by combining deterministic algorithms and AI. Not to eliminate costs, but to move them in such a way as to increase the overall benefit.
