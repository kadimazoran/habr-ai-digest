To start, a little context: my team and I are building a service for businesses, and I sometimes write here on Habr about successes, failures, or just my experiences. A few years ago, I wrote about how our PIM system was structured internally.

It's still structured the same way, but something has irreversibly changed. We actively developed the product, but it became outdated faster than we developed it. That's how I felt. I didn't know what to do about it. And then I figured it out. Over several months, we changed how users interact with the system, and it benefited both us and our clients. But don't worry, I still don't understand many things.

We've integrated an AI agent into our platform and taught it to work independently. For example, it actually searches the internet on its own and creates product cards on marketplaces.

But we know that under the hood, it involves API calls, running Python scripts (which call C libraries), and so on. That's what we'll be talking about.

We'll also discuss why it took us four attempts to "implement AI".

PIM is a rather niche topic; not every developer has encountered it. Essentially, it's a system for managing product data, and its task is to collect, process, and distribute this data. For instance, a vacuum cleaner card on a marketplace is ideally the result of an export from a PIM (not manual entry in a personal account). A list of available motor oils with RN17 approval is also a PIM matter. Just as CRM relates to processes involving customers, PIM relates to processes involving products.

We'll refer to token predictors as artificial intelligence. It turns out that complex and very useful things can be built on the basis of this mechanism. For example, it can already program better than me, despite my 20 years of experience. In general, this is a topic for a separate article; I'm still better in some aspects.

The story of integrating specific AI functions into the product began long ago, about 5 years ago. At that time, out of all the things we wanted to do, models could only generate product descriptions based on known characteristics.

The rest didn't work well: for example, models lacked the intelligence to understand that if a product specification listed a Bluetooth version, it meant wireless connectivity was possible. Later, expensive models learned to understand this, and this year, relatively inexpensive ones can. GPT-6 Luna handles this task quite well.

The third separate task we offloaded to AI was the distribution of new products into the catalog's category tree.

But all of this was solving individual, isolated problems without any long-term strategy or vision.

Starting Point

So, we have a PIM system. It can do a lot, from collecting supplier price lists in various formats to integrating with all major marketplaces. It handles catalogs with millions of products well, gives clients access to a library of 100 million product cards, and provides algorithms for working with them.

This means if a client wants to sell a Kärcher vacuum cleaner on Ozon, they don't need to find photos or download specifications. The data is available in the system from the official website, and a product card can be created almost automatically.

Integrations with Ozon, Wildberries, and other marketplaces are implemented. There's a module for Bitrix. There's an API. But still, you need to configure this, figure out that, invent and write a formula here. You still have to do something manually, and keep an eye on other things.

On the other hand, there are large language models that can write code, answer questions, have broad knowledge, and understand context.

Thirdly, we use Claude Code, Codex, and OpenCode, which can effectively use models and have access to local tools for working with code and more. For example, they add new features to a project and do it well. This is no longer a chat window where you copy the file content and ask to fix a bug or add a button; it's a genuinely new level of working with code and projects.

Claude Code, even without documentation, can understand code and add new functionality or fix a bug in old code. With documentation, it does even better. By the way, we try to document not the implementation details, but the tasks that we solve with a particular function, and explain why it's necessary for our users. Since Claude Code can do that, it can also figure out settings.

And there's also MCP in the world – a protocol built on top of JSON-RPC. It's a simple thing: the server just needs to accept and return JSON of a specific structure. Agents like Claude Code understand it well. In principle, they understand other APIs too, but they are optimized specifically for working with MCP.

Plan

Claude Code became publicly available in May 2025. I started actively using it in December. Then it took another six months to go from automating routine tasks (writing code with Claude Code) to something fundamentally new (Claude Code or another agent independently working on client tasks). It seems like an obvious idea, but it took a long time to sink in for me.

We realized we could build something valuable from this when a prototype developed in an evening assigned HS codes to hundreds of thousands of products in an hour. We checked it (we had several thousand products for verification, for which a specialist had manually selected codes earlier) – and it turned out there were few discrepancies, tenths of a percent, and those that existed favored Claude Code (it was under the hood).

By the way, if you just give Claude Code a list of products and assign this task without tools, it won't cope. The prototype provided it with effective methods for searching product data and filtering HS codes. Tools are half the battle.

When we saw this, we started developing the actual agent.

The plan was as follows: for the user, it looks like a chat within the system where they can type "create Ozon cards for products from the file," upload a file containing only the names, and expect the cards to actually appear on Ozon. Our task is to make sure they actually appear there.

But this is only the first step, reacting to a user's request. The second step is autonomous operation. Technically, implementing autonomous operation isn't that difficult when you have a foundation. But before clients start trusting the agent to do things independently, they test its capabilities and adequacy in chat mode.

How It Works

The magic for the user happens somewhere in the chat window. The magic for the developer happens in exactly one place – somewhere on the remote servers of the LLM provider. Everything else is quite deterministic and predictable.

Let's look at each block in detail.

User

The agent is used by different people with different skills, permissions, and responsibilities. Some write "Fill out this product card" and then check it visually, while others set up a scenario like "Mass creation of Ozon cards for new products from Merlion" and trust the agent to work in the background with tens of thousands of products directly in their active Ozon account. For reference, Merlion is a major electronics distributor. Readers are likely more familiar with the brands "Citilink" and "Positronica."

Chat Window

Here, on one hand, everything is simple: JavaScript that adds a chat to all pages. Communication with the server is via standard HTTP requests (we'll switch to WebSockets later).

On the other hand, it's not so simple: for the agent not to look foolish, it needs user context. Essentially, the chat must gather information about what the user sees on the page, which pages they viewed earlier, which products or brands are checked, and where on which page a specific button or label is located. Part of this complex work of immersing the agent in the user's context is implemented by the chat script.

I'm drawing attention to this because there was a period when top models couldn't navigate their own interfaces.

— How do I enable voice input?

— I don't know, my data is only up to February 2025, this feature didn't exist then, and I can't search the internet.

Web Server

Requests are processed by a web server on ASP.NET MVC. It's been around for many years, existing since the project's inception. We've simply added methods for working with the chat and integrated an MCP server. From a code organization perspective, the MCP server is a separate project and is simply connected via dependency injection.

Agent Runner

This is a separate process on a separate server. Kestrel, the built-in server for ASP.NET Core, is responsible for handling HTTP requests. Its task is to launch the agent with the correct settings and in the right environment. Settings, keys, the request, session ID – everything comes within the request from the web server. The runner's job is to prepare the environment, launch the required agent, pass the request for execution, and then read the result. We write to stdin and read from stdout.

If the user uploaded a file, it needs to be placed in the folder where the relevant session resides. If the agent generated a file for the user, it needs to be able to read it and provide it to the user when they download it. (The request from the browser goes to the web server, and from there to the runner; users do not interact directly with the runner).

The runner doesn't make decisions itself; it receives commands from the Web Application. This allows specifying in the admin panel which model and which agent to use for a specific client, and what data to use for accessing the LLM API.

Another task of the runner is to ensure a unified interface for working with any agent. Regardless of whether Claude Code, Cursor, OpenCode, or Qwen Code does the actual work, the interface for interacting with the runner remains the same.

Agent

The agent itself is not even our code. The agent's purpose is to work effectively with the LLM API, firstly, and to have access to the computer and tools, secondly. Currently, Claude Code and OpenCode are supported, but architecturally, other executors can be easily added.

The agent receives a command from the user and constructs a request to the LLM API: a system prompt (OS, installed software, rules, and limitations), conversation history, and the user's request. It marks the request prefix as cacheable; resending the same prefix will cost 10 times less because the model doesn't recalculate them.

For example, the user types "What time is it?". The agent constructs the request and sends it to the LLM API. The response isn't the answer text but a request to call a tool: "execute date +%s". The agent runs the command, gets 1783874849, appends the result to the conversation, and sends the entire context again. Now the model responds with text – "It's five to five" – and the agent prints it to the user.

But on a blank operating system, the agent's capabilities are limited. For effective work, ready-made programs are needed for frequently occurring tasks.

First – working with code. We use Python with a set of pre-installed libraries. If large data arrays need processing, it's done not through context and LLM, but by generating code for their processing. This is more economical, accurate, and faster.

Second – working with files. The user asks to create an Excel table or extract product characteristics from a PDF, and the agent does it. If the task is simple, built-in grep, sed, or cat are sufficient. If it's more complex, the same Python and suitable libraries are used, such as pandas and openpyxl. If these are not provided to the agent, it writes XML files and assembles an XLSX file from them. It's surprising to me that it can do this.

Third – working with the network. After all, you still need to find that PDF with characteristics.

And fourth – the platform's MCP methods. The agent calls them just like `date`, but the request goes to our platform.

As a result, the agent doesn't just communicate; it can actually do work.

MCP Server

This is a way to provide the agent with access to the platform's capabilities. And the platform knows how to do everything else: process price lists, recalculate prices, exchange data with marketplaces or other systems like Bitrix or 1C, search for products by filters, and much more (the number of tools is in the hundreds).

Through MCP, the agent also receives all project documentation. Furthermore, it can view any page through the user's eyes. For example, a user writes, "I can't find the red chair with the duck," and the agent replies, "The open page displays a list of products, and the red chair with the duck is in the fifth position on this list. The duck is not visible in the main photo; it's in the third photo." To achieve this, we had to create our own ViewEngine – to provide not raw HTML, but a more compact version of the page data.

There are also nuances with user interface documentation. If it's all provided at once, the context grows to hundreds of thousands of tokens. Firstly, it's expensive; secondly, a large context with data unrelated to the task can confuse the agent. Therefore, we had to build something similar to Wikipedia, but via MCP. At the top level is a description of the service as a whole: what tasks it solves, how the user interface is structured at the section level, and what integrations are implemented. At this level, there's also an instruction on how to view documentation for a specific section.

At the second level, it's the same principle, but for a section. And at the third and fourth levels, there's documentation for a specific page or function. They link to each other if functions are related. Additionally, the agent can view any page itself if the documentation is insufficient.

This allows the agent to save context while adequately answering any questions about the system's interface.

During development, each tool was tested for performance at different scales, including analyzing all database query plans that the tool might generate. We measured the number of reads and writes on a test database with 10 million products. This is important because agents can create significant load on the database if any of the MCP API methods are inefficient (e.g., perform a full table index scan).

LLM API

— Is this your machine learning system?

— Yes! You feed data into this big pile of linear algebra, and then you collect answers from the other side.

— And if the answers are wrong?

— Just shuffle the pile until they start looking right.

I swear, it works something like that inside. For us, it just looks like an API call to a remote server.

Can't We Do Without the Platform?

A limited task can be solved without a platform and MCP. But a full-fledged data management process – no, you can't build that.

Marketplace exchanges, for example, involve tens of thousands of lines of code. The nuance is that marketplace APIs are not documented as well as we'd like. For instance, the documentation lists 20 possible values for the seller's expense type enumeration. In reality, we encounter 50. Not everything is immediate, and different clients have different sets. And this data can only be accumulated through real-world usage and converted into code.

And so it is with all integrations. Code developed according to documentation either doesn't work or breaks at some point with unexpected data.

But even without considering integrations, the platform provides what the agent would have to invent itself – efficient algorithms, frequently used functions, rarely used functions, plus tens of terabytes of collected and structured data.

And also – hundreds of pages that provide a picture of what's happening. That is, the system's state is not hidden; everything can be done manually if you don't trust the agent.

For a month, I actively read user chats with the agent and looked for incorrect answers, erroneous actions, and suboptimal task solutions. Sometimes a tool was missing – so I added it; sometimes documentation gaps were found – I updated the documentation. In that month alone, there were several hundred corrections and improvements. Now I continue to do so, but less actively.

Autonomous Operation

Even without the agent, the platform provided the ability to run tasks on a schedule. For example, every business day at 10:00 AM, new supplier prices need to be fetched, and every 15 minutes, current stock levels need to be uploaded to the marketplace. Autonomous operation is built on this mechanism.

For repetitive actions, the client creates a scenario for the agent and can refer to it in the chat or set up a schedule, and then the platform will run it at the specified time.

Does It Work?

We've dealt with the technical part, and it really works as intended. But what about the business benefits?

Here, we'll take a step back and return to the days of our prototype. At that time, there were two viewpoints in the team: release the agent's functionality as early as possible and give it to users, or refine it to a state where it could perform almost all user scenarios, navigate the interface independently, and understand the user's current context – see what the user sees. We chose the second path, and it delayed the release by a month or two.

But it was worth it, in my opinion. Users didn't immediately start trusting the agent. Initially, there were requests that were like tests of adequacy and capabilities. I think if a raw version had responded, the agent would have failed these tests and wouldn't have gained credibility. And persuading them to try a version after improvements would have been very difficult.

Why persuade? The service's goal is to solve client problems, and the agent makes it easier and faster. If a service doesn't deliver the expected benefits, it's easier to abandon it.

What if the agent does something wrong? For many operations, it's possible to roll back. You tell the agent "revert it," and it reverts. Session data is stored for several weeks.

Price management on marketplaces is a risky operation. It can also be rolled back, but you can get unfavorable orders in a short time. But, firstly, you can deny the platform overall the rights to do this. Secondly, even if the platform has the rights, the agent cannot manage prices directly, only through the settings of the pricing algorithm. Of course, the algorithm should be checked before launching it in a live account; for this, there's an option to perform test runs without sending data to marketplaces.

Another significant effect is that we've received about three times fewer support requests. However, by studying the chats, I understand that the number of questions has increased. People ask the agent things they wouldn't have written to support about.

The three previous attempts to integrate AI into the product failed. Well, they didn't fail entirely; from a technical standpoint, they worked as intended and performed the stated tasks. Clients used these functions (descriptions – rarely, but filling out product cards and finding the right categories for new products – more often).

But conceptually, they were just useful features, not a paradigm shift. They weren't a defining characteristic of the product. I expected more from these attempts and saw them as a full integration of AI into the product – although in reality, they didn't become that.

The fourth attempt, as far as I can see, has become that defining characteristic of the product that makes people want to implement it, and quickly. I didn't invent this – since the release, I've met with clients over 50 times to discuss it. Everyone finds it useful, but I also regularly receive lists of things that still need work. For example, something clearly needs to be done about the agent's access to pages protected by captchas. We're thinking about it.

I continue to monitor the agent's performance; thousands of dialogues per week are now being processed. I don't know how many cards are created or edited; I haven't counted. More important was whether the agent could handle the stated tasks. I can't assess the effect from the perspective of client business metrics; I don't have access to those metrics. I only have feedback from meetings and access to the dialogues.

Conclusions

I started using AI regularly and noticing its effect on my work when the chat for questions and answers was replaced by a chat for commands and reports.

Our platform's users – when a cohesive concept replaced individual functions, in which the AI agent has the ability to do everything a user can do (and even more; users don't very often write Python scripts).

By the time of the fourth attempt, ready-made agents were available, and models became smarter and cheaper.

Without ready-made agents, we wouldn't have been able to implement all the capabilities on our own. Dumb models couldn't solve complex tasks, and expensive ones wouldn't have made economic sense.

It turns out that attempt number four was successful because the world changed. Our merit is that we noticed this and changed the product's paradigm in accordance with the new possibilities.

Perhaps this is the most difficult thing: in a situation where a product has been around for many years, where it works and actually solves tasks – to look at it from a different perspective, to look at the world around, and to admit that it was good five years ago, and now it's outdated, even though it has been developing for all five years.

It seems the agent is here to stay; we can no longer turn it off – it has proven useful, users have gotten used to it, and if we turn it off, the product will objectively become worse.
