Of the 6,157 reports that Anthropic has provided to open source project maintainers, 516 have been fixed: this is 8.4%, not 516 out of 29,000, as The New Stack headline reads.

The headline from The New Stack (hereinafter TNS) reads: "Claude found 29,000 possible bugs in open source. Only 516 have been fixed." It combines numbers from different stages of the funnel, so I opened Anthropic's dashboard and recalculated the proportions. The figures are as of October 2, 2026, the date of the snapshot. I'll preface this by saying they describe six months of Anthropic's work before the launch of OSS Scanner (October 8). This funnel is not related to the scanner itself; it shows the process from which the scanner evolved.

What launched on October 8

On October 8, Anthropic launched OSS Scanner, a voluntary service that scans open source projects with its most powerful models (named Claude Mythos in the announcement) and sends reports to maintainers via email. The company took the idea from OSS-Fuzz, a Google project where open source code is run through fuzzers.

The main difference from the company's previous process is that the reports are entirely generated by the model; a human does not review them. Anthropic states this directly and admits that some reports may be incorrect. A report should include a self-contained reproducer, a bisect to the commit where the error occurred if possible, and a patch candidate if the model wrote one.

The service is free, but not for everyone. A project applies via a pull request to the anthropics/oss-scanner repository. The criteria are the same as for OSS-Fuzz: critical impact on infrastructure and user security, with a separate decision for each project. Scanning occurs in an isolated virtual machine without internet access, and you can opt out at any time.

The company itself states the reason for the launch. Over six months, the models found over 29,000 potential vulnerabilities, but only about 6,000 could be manually reviewed: Anthropic writes that it is limited by human resources. Maintainers, after receiving the first verified reports, increasingly asked to receive everything else, even unverified ones. Almost 5,000 reports have already been sent directly. The fast channel of OSS Scanner responds to this demand. The company itself rounds these two numbers from the October 8 announcement; the October 2 dashboard provides exact figures.

Hence the difference in dates: the October 2 dashboard accumulated over six months of the previous process, and OSS Scanner is not yet included in its figures. It does not provide a breakdown by channel. Everything below regarding the funnel, including the 78% of reports without independent verification, relates to that process; these numbers will not show how the scanner itself performs.

The second table is not nested within the first: on the dashboard, "verified" and "sent" are two separate counters, and how they intersect is not stated. Anthropic accompanies the fix count with a disclaimer "to our knowledge," meaning the actual number of fixes might be higher.

From the tables, three things follow, and the first is about the denominator. 516 should be divided by the 6,157 sent: this gives 8.4%, because the dashboard counts as fixed those vulnerabilities sent for which maintainers have released a patch. Dividing by 29,439 is not advisable: it gives 1.8%, but this proportion mixes fixes with candidates that did not pass independent verification and, in most cases, did not reach maintainers, so it is not a good measure of fixability.

Second, the word "sent" combines two different channels. About 22% of reports went through firms that reproduce the error and assess its criticality. The remaining 78% were sent without the same independent verification, some at the request of the maintainers themselves, and Anthropic warns that there may be false positives among them. Third, the 92.7% figure shows the proportion of confirmed findings among those verified by firms, which is about one-fifth of the candidates. This cannot be extrapolated to all candidates and direct reports. The remaining 23,316 candidates have not yet been verified by firms; TNS mentions a similar figure of approximately 23,000. This is the queue for verification and has no relation to fixes.

Previously, maintainers received mostly garbage from models, but now they receive quality reports, and they rarely call a high or critical finding incorrect. In the announcement, the company provides results on the academic benchmark CyberGym: the proportion of found vulnerabilities increased from less than 20% at the beginning of last year to over 85% this year. This is a significant jump, but it is Anthropic's own figure on a single benchmark, and there is no independent verification in the announcement.

On an early version of the scanner, pentesters who reviewed Anthropic's CVD reports assessed 97 high and critical severity findings in 48 projects. 85 (88%) met the disclosure process threshold, 11 of the remaining 12 were real but duplicated known issues or other findings from the same scan, and only one was a false positive. 88% sounds impressive, but the sample size is small and consists only of major findings: these 97 reports do not show how the scanner will perform on low severity issues and with a large volume.

Why there are fewer fixes than findings

Anthropic itself names the reasons in the dashboard explanations, and most of them are mundane.

Fixes lag in time. The company considers the number of patches a more reliable indicator of impact than the number of confirmed findings, but it is "lagging": patches take a long time to be made. For example, a report from September might not be released until November, and the October 2 snapshot will not see this.

Response does not equal fix. Maintainers responded to 5,103 reports, and fixes were released for 516. These are two independent counters: on the dashboard, "responded" is counted for any response, and "fixed" is counted for a released patch, even if there was no response. The data does not explain why there are approximately ten times fewer fixes than responses.

Confirmed does not necessarily mean fixable. The proportion of true positive findings, 92.7%, includes real errors that have been reported before, and "won't fix" cases: the bug exists, but the project does not intend to fix it, for example, because it is outside the threat model or the code is unreachable in real scenarios.

Finally, confirmed reports are waiting in the queue. Anthropic writes that there are many real errors verified by it and by firms that have not yet been sent to maintainers due to a lack of capacity. The company does not state how many there are. This explains the gap with the total number of candidates, not the proportion of fixes among those sent. Another factor explains the proportion, and TNS writes about it: even with an accepted report, there is still work to be done on the project's side. The maintainer needs to review the patch, make backports, and release it, sometimes realizing that the fix will break behavior that users rely on.

What maintainers say and who chose the quotes

The announcement includes five testimonials. Noah Misch from PostgreSQL writes that some reports came with fixes that the project could adopt almost without changes, and the fast channel allowed them to close recent bugs before the release. Anton Arapov from OpenSSL Corporation writes that the raw output of the model is no worse, and sometimes better, than what comes from humans, whereas he called early AI reports from a year and a half ago terrible.

Todd Ouska from wolfSSL received 74 reports, of which all but two turned out to be valid, five became CVEs. Eddie Kohler from HotCRP praises the understanding of complex access control models. Daniel Stenberg from curl, who, according to TNS, publicly complained about the flood of AI garbage in bug bounties all last year, writes that the scanner found several issues in curl, one of which was among the worst in recent years.

The testimonials were selected by Anthropic itself and published in its own announcement; this is not a survey. The secondary source, the TNS article, is not neutral: at the end of the text, the publication states that its owner, Insight Partners, is investing in Anthropic. I am taking the quotes from the announcement as they are. Five satisfied projects out of 591 is an illustration, and it's still far from statistical significance.

Anthropic itself has less pleasant data. Some maintainers report that the criticality in the reports is overestimated or that the scanner did not understand the project's threat model. The dashboard presents a similar, but not identical, picture: Claude's and external firms' criticality assessments matched exactly in 83.0% of cases and within one level in 97.1% (sample size: about 1,300 reports that went through triage). Firms usually rate lower because they know the specific project's rules, while Claude does not have them at the time of work.

Checklist for a Model-Generated Report

If you are a maintainer and have already received or will receive such a report, the reproducer and patch significantly shorten the path, but the decision remains yours. I have compiled a verification order based on the issues Anthropic mentions: duplicates, overestimated criticality, external threat model.

  1. Reproducibility. Does the reproducer run on your version and on a clean build, not just in the scanner's environment? A report without a working reproducer remains a hypothesis.

  2. Reachability. Can external input reach this point without violating the API contract? An error in code that cannot be reached in real-world operation might be a "won't fix".

  3. Threat Model. Who is the attacker here, and what do they control? Does this align with what the project considers a threat (SECURITY.md, security policy)?

  4. Criticality. Recalculate according to project rules, do not accept the assessment from the report: external firms, as seen above, usually rate lower than Claude.

  5. Duplicates. Check the tracker and past CVEs, as well as other reports from the same batch: in the early scan, 11 out of 12 findings that did not meet the threshold turned out to be duplicates.

  6. Patch. Does it fix the cause or the symptom? What tests are needed? What will break for those who rely on the current behavior? Are backports needed?

  7. Deadlines. According to the repository's README, Anthropic does not currently set a 90-day disclosure deadline for OSS Scanner reports and does not publish findings: there is no external deadline, you set the fix deadlines. TNS writes that for some high-severity findings, a deadline may be introduced later, after notifying projects and allowing them to opt out. If Anthropic later verifies the report under the standard program, then according to TNS, it can be disclosed 90 days after maintainers become aware of human confirmation; I have not analyzed the conditions of this program.

To analyze such a flow, a model can be used as a second opinion, but not as a replacement for a decision. This should be done where the report is already public or the model runs locally: do not send non-public vulnerabilities and closed-source code to external cloud services. My unverified hypothesis: if several models assess reachability or criticality differently, the report may lack data, and this is a reason to investigate manually.

If you are not maintaining a project but using someone else's, look at the lag: a finding, confirmation, and released patch are different events, and the dashboard counts CVEs and GHSAs regardless of whether a fix has been released. The appearance of an identifier for your dependency does not mean that a fix is already available in your version. If you are writing your own scanner or calculating metrics from reports, divide by the correct denominator and count verified and direct findings separately, as in the tables above.

My Opinion, Separate from the Facts

The headline about "29,000 vs. 516" is inaccurate but highlights a real issue: the deficit is not in the ability to find errors, but in the attention of a human who decides what to do with them. Anthropic addresses this deficit with six external firms and still verified about one-fifth of the candidates. A project receiving a stream of direct reports has fewer resources than such a company. Accepting everything is rational for a maintainer; some maintainers themselves asked to receive everything, but the queue of reports may grow faster than the queue of patches, and the dashboard does not show the number of vulnerabilities missed by the scanner.

What I might have missed. I looked at a single dashboard snapshot and did not compare it with previous ones, so I cannot assess the dynamics: 516 fixes in a month might turn out to be a completely different number. I don't know how many of the 516 are from verified reports and how many are from direct ones, and without this, it's impossible to say how they differ in terms of the proportion of fixes. I have not run the scanner myself and do not have access to Claude Mythos, so my conclusions are based on publications from Anthropic and TNS.
