You've probably recognized the quote I used for the title. It belongs to Arthur Barbidge, a young idealist who died on his way to the Golden Sphere in the Strugatskys' "Roadside Picnic."

I can't promise to play the role of the Golden Sphere, but the conclusion I've reached after many years of reflection, I believe, can lead to such a result.

Where it all began

In 2013, the paper "Causal Entropic Forces" by Dr. Alex D. Wissner-Gross and C. E. Freer was published. I stumbled upon a review of this article in Russian. The general idea of this paper is that it proves, through computer modeling, that systems driven solely by the requirement to maximize future options space spontaneously develop adaptive, intelligent, and protective behavior.

It might not sound impressive. However, it hooked me at the time. "Future options space" is, in principle, "degrees of freedom." And this is a fairly formalizable concept. Furthermore, it might be difficult to determine the absolute numerical value of the "degrees of freedom" of an element or system. But the paper talks about "maximizing future options space," i.e., about dynamics – about the change in this value. And this is easier to work with.

For over 10 years, I've been pondering this idea and came to the conclusion that "increasing degrees of freedom" can be a fairly objective numerical characteristic that defines such a subjective concept as "utility."

So what? What's the point?

And here we can recall how rapidly neural networks and artificial intelligence are developing in our world right now.

After a long time working with AI as a tool for software development and collaborative analysis of various ideas, I realized one important thing. The result of AI's work depends 90% on the accuracy of the task formulation.

And what task is currently causing the most concern and anxiety worldwide? That's right – "AI safety."

Isaac Asimov, in many of his works, very well showed that even seemingly correctly formulated three laws of robotics would not work if there were various interpretations of the terms included in them. The main problem is the concepts of "benefit" and "harm," which are cornerstones in the formulation of these laws. They can be interpreted quite arbitrarily. The results of which the author very successfully shows in his works.

And here we come to the most important part. Using the concept of "degrees of freedom" as a quantitative measure of the concepts of "benefit" and "harm" is objective and independent of interpretation.

What did I do next?

About a couple of months ago, I realized that I now have access to pretty good LLMs. And that I could test situations using such a definition on them. After that, the most interesting part began...

The first "runs" yielded very interesting results. But, at the same time, they determined that the single key concept was not enough for the adequate functioning of the "new laws of robotics." And then, together with the LLM, I began to develop them "from scratch."

As a result, 7 axioms were obtained.

However, upon further analysis, it turned out that, in reality, all these "axioms" can be reduced to a single formula, proven by Dr. John Nash (remember "A Beautiful Mind"?) back in 1950 in his work "The Bargaining Problem" (Dr. John F. Nash, Jr.). This simple formula shows that the only solution that maximizes structural fairness and mutual utility is the product of the individual utilities of the participants (Nash product).

That is, using this formula to calculate the total "utility" leads to the fact that in a system operating on this principle (increasing the total degrees of freedom of the system), the algorithm will lead to all participants receiving utility. And this also correctly leads to the fact that the "zeroing" of any participant in the system nullifies the overall result.

If a decision-making neural network is configured to use this principle, then all predicted options will pass through a filter that calculates the change in the system's degrees of freedom using this formula. And no option that leads to the destruction of at least one of the elements will be allowed for implementation.

If this rule is applied to a real situation, then in the "trolley problem," such a neural network will not sacrifice any of the people. It will look for other solutions to the problem that should lead to the salvation of everyone. And, in my opinion, this is a much more "moral" approach than trying to solve the "dilemma."

Is this only applicable to neural networks?

And here comes the most interesting part.

This principle is not tied to any implementation at all. And its use by humans (miraculously!) leads them to very "moral" behavior. And here we make a discovery! It turns out that "morality" is not something ethereal, given from above, or even a law of the existence of society. :) It turns out that "morality" is just a way of behavior to achieve general well-being using pure mathematics. :)

Summary

So far, the result of my work on this topic is the DOF-CORE repository. It contains a general description of the principle in the form of a skill for LLM. In addition, the repository contains a DOF-SPEC document, which details various aspects of using the principle.

Such a set of documentation arises from a two-level system of its use in AI. The first level is a skill that gives the AI general rules of behavior. For the most part, an AI with such a skill will only form behavioral options. The second level is a regular application in which the DOF-CORE principles will be embedded. And this application will act as a filter that selects from the behavioral options of the first level those that actually preserve the principle.

I also want to add, of course, that the current version is not the final solution. If you find any logical loopholes or special cases in it, I would be glad if you share them with me.
