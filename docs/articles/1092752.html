Quarterly budget approved and quarterly budget not approved. For embeddings, these are almost the same phrase: the words are the same, and the particle not carries little weight in the vector. For a meeting, this means the opposite was decided.

This is why an NLI model lives in Charoite. Below is where it has been working for three months, where I removed it and why, and what it cost.

Why Did I Bother?

Not because of the feed. It started with the minutes.

In July, I processed the minutes of one and the same meeting four times with production settings. I got 39 different statements. 32 of them appeared in only one of the four runs. None were repeated in all. The task number, which wasn't mentioned in the conversation at all. The date, which was there but didn't make it into any version. I wanted a machine that could take four sets of minutes, put them side-by-side, and say: here, the runs contradict each other, look here.

I took mDeBERTa-v3-base-xnli-multilingual-nli-2mil7 — multilingual, trained on over two million pairs. Converted it to ONNX, resulting in 1.1 GB. I didn't bring Torch: just onnxruntime and the tokenizer, nothing else. Loading takes 1.5 seconds.

It could already merge paraphrases. Deadline breach due to delay in rolling out improvements and deadline breach due to dependent tasks conveyed the same meaning. But it was bad at detecting contradictions. Speaker 5 clarifies task status versus Speaker 2 checks rollout — a contradiction, the model says. No, they are simply about different things. It was trained on pairs about one situation, and it distinguishes about something else from the opposite poorly.

So I did what everyone does: I created my own dataset. Sixteen pairs from real minutes, a manual ground truth. I added a filter: first, a common subject, then the model. 16 out of 16. At the same time, I compared it with the only active Russian NLI model on Hugging Face, rubert-base-cased-nli-threeway. Without the filter, 13 out of 16 compared to 14 for the multilingual model; with the filter, 15 versus 16. The Russian model stumbled where it mattered most to me: replication is done by Speaker 1 and replication is done by Speaker 3 it considered simply different phrases, but it's a change of performer.

Then I ran it through the archive: 14 meetings, 51 protocol items, 1192 pairs. Of the 76 pairs that passed the filter, the model identified 30 as contradictions. Almost all were false positives.

The solution lay in the length. The average protocol item is 138 characters, 69% longer than 120. An item like It is necessary to initiate direct contact with the responsible person to clarify the general principles of connection, bypassing intermediate approval stages — that's three thoughts in one paragraph. Any two such paragraphs will have a common word and something that resembles a conflict. My sixteen pairs gave 100% because I wrote them short myself. A custom dataset is not proof.

Ultimately, NLI doesn't detect contradictions in minutes. Minutes should be written as short statements, up to sixty characters, with a named object, and numbers and dates should be checked directly against the transcript by searching. This is two orders of magnitude cheaper. In that run, such a check caught one fabrication out of ten statements: the release is scheduled for 17.08, where August seventeenth was not mentioned by number or word.

The model remained on disk. Two days later, it came in handy.

Place One: Duplicates in the Live Feed

Here, you need to understand where duplicates come from. A model that writes theses during a call only sees the recent part, about 800 characters. It doesn't know what was said half an hour ago. In a long meeting, people return to a topic, and the model opens the same thing again, in different words. By the end of the hour, the feed is half repetitions.

Word-for-word comparison doesn't see paraphrases. Embeddings see paraphrases, but along with them, they swallow negations — that's where I started. Bidirectional entailment worked. A duplicate is when A follows from B and B follows from A. A paraphrase goes both ways. A clarification goes only one way: budget approved follows from budget approved by year-end, but not vice versa, and the clarification remains in the feed. A contradiction doesn't go either way.

It's set up like this. I keep the last sixteen theses in a window. First, I compare a new one with all of them by words, using cheap difflib. If the word overlap is less than 0.3, I don't touch the pair at all: the model doesn't need completely different theses. The 0.3 threshold is intentionally low. For the bare phrases 2 RPS and 2 requests per second, the only common word is '2', ratio 0.33, and a greedy threshold would have cut them off even before the model check. Those that pass go to the model, in both directions. If both probabilities are not lower than 0.8, the thesis is discarded, and the log writes thesis-duplicate filtered out.

Why 0.8, not 0.75, as by default. Errors are not equivalent here. A repeat in the feed is annoying. A discarded thesis with new information is a lost fact. Better to repeat sometimes.

This is the only place where NLI works for me every day without issues. Tens of milliseconds per call, and the pre-filter prevents most pairs from reaching the model.

It's not perfect. In the meeting on September 29th, auto-suggestions returned to the same topic five times in eight minutes without adding anything new. The thesis filter isn't to blame here: that's a different feed, and deduplication hasn't reached it yet. The mechanism is already there, the model is already in memory — that's the next task.

Place Two: Nightly Core Revision

I wrote about the meeting graph in the second part. In short: after each meeting, Charoite breaks it down into nodes — people, systems, topics. Topics that live longer than one meeting, I call cores. Each core has its own essence, history, people, and decisions.

The problem with the graph is that one topic is referred to differently. An example from an architectural document in my own repository: API access configuration and token retrieval — one job, two cores, two histories, and neither is complete. By words, the match is zero, difflib gives 0.0. All hope is on meaning. Spoiler alert: the measurement below didn't confirm this example, but the revision is set up precisely for such pairs.

Therefore, core revision is done in two steps. First, bge-m3 collects candidates: pairs with a cosine similarity of 0.55 or higher. Then, NLI judges each pair based on the essence of the cores, in both directions. There are four outcomes:

  • duplicate — both sides >= 0.8; candidate for merging;

  • possible duplicate — 0.72 to 0.8; a tag is added, which is easy to remove;

  • nesting — one side >= 0.85, the other < 0.5; a narrow topic is included in a broader one;

  • boundary — both < 0.45; topics are adjacent, the connection is worth noting.

A core with three nested items already attached is considered a hub, and its revision doesn't touch it. Merging is only done with a backup and only if enabled by a separate flag. Without the flag, the revision adds tags to the graph.

In August, everything looked good. 284 active cores, 40,186 pairs. Through the embedding pre-filter, 1806 passed, NLI spent 106 ms per call, two calls per pair, a full revision took six minutes. I was even happy that I didn't have to tweak the thresholds.

By October, there were over a thousand cores. The full revision on September 27th hit the nightly limit after three and a half hours and left 824 cores for subsequent nights. Regular nightly runs also went up to the limit. And dossiers — summaries on a topic that suggestions provide first — were not compiled in eight out of twelve nights at all. It later turned out that the dossiers were hindered not by the revision: the set of topics for the night was limited to twelve and selected by file modification date, so slots went to the wrong topics. The revision ate up the night separately, and I started looking at what it actually yielded.

I looked at ten random confident pairs, those that the revision was ready to merge. There were zero actual duplicates among them. One was debatable. The confidence for false positives was 0.93–1.00.

Not all cores have their own 'Essence' section. If it's missing, the revision took an automatic status: Discussion of requirements and features. Or just the name itself, when the status was empty. Two different work topics with the same template status — and NLI says: these phrases follow from each other. Yes, they do. It's the same phrase. The model worked correctly.

I had set a higher threshold for the auto-status beforehand, 0.92, and didn't merge if two cores shared more than half of their meetings. It didn't help: false positives had 0.93 and higher.

It got worse. There were 239 possible duplicate tags in the graph. Zero were processed. Tags are shown by a single service note, and it turned out no one opened it: it's not among the last 48 files opened in Obsidian. Nested items — 1028 in 462 files out of 1047 cores. Almost every second core has some nesting that no one has read.

On October 5th, I disabled the revision myself with a single line in the config. I never enabled auto-merging, and now I understand why it was right: it would have merged different work topics, and the suggestion during a meeting would have shown me someone else's task status. The merge backup lasts a week, and no one looks at it. In reality, it's irreversible.

What I lost. Among the thirty cases checked, four or five were debatable pairs that perhaps should have been merged. They remained unmerged. As before: no one was going to merge them anyway.

The revision can be reinstated when the judgment is based only on cores with their own essence and when 90% of twenty confident pairs are genuine. In production, it is currently enabled by default, and this is a separate task — to rework it before someone other than me accumulates a thousand cores.

Place Three: Gate Before Lightning

In Charoite, there's a ⚡ button — a quick answer to a question just asked on a call. A structural filter finds a candidate in the feed, and a local model writes an answer in seconds. Sometimes the candidate isn't a question: someone is rephrasing, thinking aloud, or interrupting themselves. Then the model replies no question detected, and time has been wasted.

I wanted a cheaper judge before the model. Without retraining, with two hypotheses, both affirmative: Speaker is asking a question and expects an answer and Speaker is rephrasing, thinking aloud, or interrupting. Affirmative because models work worse with negations in hypotheses. A cascade: if confident, it decides itself; if not confident, it passes to the model.

First, in shadow mode: the judge runs in parallel and doesn't decide anything, just logs what it would have done. Four meetings, 117 utterances.

The model answered 115. It refused 2. That's 1.7% — the maximum that the gate could have saved, even an ideal one. Caveat: I considered a refusal by the model itself as empty. If it answers non-questions, this measurement doesn't see them.

And NLI wasn't ideal. Accuracy against this label was 55.6%, almost a coin toss. Calibration was poor: it's confident significantly more often than it's right. Now for the thresholds:

What Broke

The most unpleasant thing about NLI for me is a silent failure.

First time. Charoite's models are data, they reside in the data root. But the code looked for the NLI model next to itself, in the code root. In my repository, both roots coincide, and the defect is not visible. In an installation where data is stored separately, the model is not found at this path. And in deduplication, there's a rule: no model on disk means deduplication is off. No error, no warning, the feed simply repeats again. I didn't notice it from the feed, but from a review when they were discussing who takes paths from where. And twice: the first time it was fixed, a few days later the same class appeared in another place — the path was cached during module import, before the program could say where the root was. Now the path to the model is calculated at the moment of call.

Second time — in core revision, and also twice in a row. The judge was assembled but couldn't judge, and the revision considered the run complete: it found no pairs, moved the tag, and fresh cores were completely excluded from verification. Zero for the revision means not similar, not nothing to judge. Now a judge's refusal is an exception, not zero, and before processing, the model undergoes a canary test: the same phrase about the payment gateway against itself. Entailment less than 0.5 means the model is considered not ready, and this is visible in the status, not just in the lack of results.

Third — speed. My nightly runs have lower priority so as not to interfere if I'm using the laptop. Under such a restriction, one call in revision cost 1346 ms. Without restriction — 189. On eight threads — 65. The bottleneck is not the model, but the priority. During the day, while recording is in progress, I won't give the revision the entire processor: recording is more important. And sometimes the laptop sleeps on battery at night, and the run starts when I wake it up.

And one more, minor thing. I tried converting the model to CoreML to run on neural cores. It crashes. I stayed with onnxruntime and the CPU.

Embeddings confirmed their behavior. A threshold of 0.85 would have merged five pairs: two actual duplicates, but along with them, both clarifications and the budget with the 'not' particle. They would have let Ivanov's performer for Petrov pass by a hair's breadth, 0.845.

NLI — 7 out of 10. All three contradictions and both clarifications are correct. The misses are instructive. 2 RPS versus two requests per second it doesn't consider a duplicate: 0.60 one way, 0.03 the other. In this form, my filter in the feed would have already discarded the pair by words, with zero overlap. And if it had reached the model, it still wouldn't have been a duplicate. Paraphrases with abbreviations remain duplicates in the feed. It calls database rollback and token retrieval a contradiction — this is the same July disease, about something else versus the opposite. And the pair about API and token, which I cited above as one job: cosine similarity 0.50, below the candidate threshold, the revision wouldn't have even seen it. NLI calls it a contradiction, Qwen a clarification. The example from my own document turned out to be debatable for all three judges.

Qwen — 8 out of 10. And here I was wrong. I was sure that a large model judges a pair in seconds. Measurement: 0.3 s per pair, maximum 0.47 s. NLI — 40 ms per call, two calls per pair. A difference of four times, not tens.

The sample is small, the ground truth is mine, I wrote the pairs myself, and my dataset, as it turned out back in July, is not proof. But one conclusion holds: embeddings confuse duplicates with negations and clarifications.

Why NLI After All

One might ask: if out of three places, one works, one is disabled, and one is closed, why keep a gigabyte model.

Because what truly bothers me in the graph and the feed is not similarity. Embeddings provide similarity well and cheaply. What bothers me is different: is it the same thing or the opposite. Did a new fact override an old one or clarify it. Does the model's answer rely on a found transcript snippet or is it generated next to it. These are all questions about entailment and contradiction. You can ask a large model. On ten pairs, it judged no worse, and in speed, it lagged by a factor of several, not orders of magnitude: 0.3 s versus 0.08 s. What keeps me is different: I have only one model, and during calls, it writes suggestions and answers ⚡. Each pair for judgment means a queue before the suggestion. NLI calculates on the nearby CPU, in its own queue, and doesn't interfere with suggestions. I haven't measured this argument yet: the measurement was done without calls. At night, it's weaker, and for core revision, I will reconsider it.

At the same time, I realized where NLI is not suitable. Names: Petrov and Petrov A. — these are the same entity, but not entailment. Echo of a speaker in my microphone: they decide time and volume there. Long paragraphs: as it turned out back in July, any two will have a conflict.

Next in the queue are three places, all addressing the same pain point.

Auto-suggestion repeats. The same mechanism as in the thesis feed, the model is already loaded. Should be cheap.

Staleness in memory. Currently, when asked what is the latest on topic X, graph search sometimes returns old information. I want to mark a fact as outdated if a newer one contradicts it. But only after each fact has a meeting date, not a file modification date. And with a pre-filter: for people in the graph, the median is nine items, but sometimes it's 464, and without selection, one such node would take minutes to judge.

Answer grounding. When Charoite answers from memory, check in the background if the answer follows from the fragments it referenced. First, the confidence threshold needs to be calibrated: currently, the answer often starts with this is not in memory, followed by found facts.

I'm not writing new hypotheses, by the way. The judge is the same: bidirectional entailment, affirmative formulations, pre-filter by meaning or by words. Only what is fed into it changes. In core revision, the error was in the input: the judge received a template status.

What's the Result

NLI in Charoite is a gigabyte on disk, onnxruntime without torch, forty milliseconds per call. It activates itself if the model is in the models folder. If not, feed deduplication and core revision are disabled, but everything else works.

Every day, thesis deduplication in the live feed works. Core revision is disabled for me until the judge is reworked; in production, it's still enabled. The gate before ⚡ is closed by the 15% rule.

In the minutes, I fed the model paragraphs instead of statements; in revision, a template status instead of essence. About minutes, I learned from an archive run, although my dataset of 16 pairs showed 100%. About cores — when I manually checked ten random pairs: in August, I only measured time. And about the speed of the large model — today, when I finally measured it.
