Neural Network by Recipe: Calculating Weights Instead of Storing Matrices

Neural network weights are a lot of digits in very large matrices. What if, instead of storing them, we stored a small recipe to calculate the necessary weights on the fly? Such approaches already exist, from a small network generating another network to formulas and actual fractals. Let's explore what works and what trade-offs are made for compactness. We'll also see how far LLMs are from the fate of the first Doom – being runnable on every teapot. Read on.

I want to talk about an interesting approach to building ML models. As you know, practically any neural network is, roughly speaking, some architecture + some set of weights. The weights here, again, roughly speaking, are digits in a matrix. A lot of digits in a very large matrix. Usually, the rule is that the more complex and "smarter" the model, the larger its "matrices with digits" are. LLMs are particularly notable in this regard: Kimi-K3, for example, has 2.8 trillion parameters. Typically, to use a model, you need

a) to have its weights on disk, from where you will load them

b) to load the weights needed for calculations into memory. Usually, this is video memory; whether all weights are loaded at once or only the current block depends on the implementation.

These two conditions largely determine the storage and memory requirements. For instance, the open-source Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct has 7.61 billion parameters, and its BF16 weight files occupy about 15.2 GB. And this is one of the smaller LLM models; the notorious Kimi-K3 already requires something around 1.4 TB if we allocate four bits for each of the 2.8 trillion parameters.

That's enough about common knowledge. This article was written to discuss an unusual way of building and compressing ML models, although it's intended for an amateur level of understanding. Some of the approaches discussed are well-known, while others have only recently appeared as preprints on arXiv. I've tried to include everything relevant to the topic.

So, the question is: what if we could use a model without storing its weights? The answer seems obvious: you can't use what you don't have – and many people primarily equate a model with its weights. But if you look closer, you'll find that sometimes a "key drawing" is enough. Although it would be more accurate to call it a recipe – a rule that allows you to assemble the model's weights "on the fly" without requiring them to be stored. A careful reader might notice that the recipe, in this case, would be the "model weights," but I hope you'll forgive me this minor simplification and allow me to distinguish these concepts for ease of exposition.

I'll preface this by saying that this article is not about some invention of mine; it's simply a review of several published studies with links to the original sources. We will simply analyze what kinds of recipe-making approaches the authors have tried, what they achieved, and how much closer it brings large language models to the fate of the first "Doom" – being runnable on every teapot.

Three Different Budgets

As mentioned, we need to consider several properties of the model that change when its weights are replaced by a recipe. We will look at three parameters that we will track. Let's call them "budgets".

Disk Size – the amount of memory occupied by the model "in storage." It mainly consists of the recipe – the generator and all data needed to reconstruct the model's weights: latents, indices, scales, and any remaining regular weights if the architecture involves replacing only a part of the model's weights with a generator. It's important to note that we cannot equate a reduction in the number of trainable parameters with an equivalent reduction in the "model" file size on disk. This budget, however, like the subsequent ones, depends on the specific model architecture.

Working Memory – the amount of memory (RAM, video memory) required by the model during operation. The composition depends on the implementation: you can reconstruct the entire matrix at once, or generate its blocks piece by piece: get a block, multiply it by the corresponding part of the input, add the result to the output, and free the block. In the latter case, we don't necessarily need to keep the entire matrix in memory simultaneously, but we still need the generator itself, the current block, inputs and outputs of the layer, and intermediate activations. For LLMs, KV-cache is added – stored keys and values for attention for previous tokens.

Computation Cost – the time the model spends preparing for operation and performing the computations themselves. Reconstructing weights can sometimes be done once at load time, and sometimes it's part of every pass. Therefore, preparation time and operation time will be indicated separately. If the authors measure throughput – how many requests or tokens are processed per second – we will also indicate the conditions of such a measurement. Reconstructing weights also requires computation; whether its cost can be hidden behind other operations depends on the implementation.

The nature of the recipe determines which matrices it can describe. Therefore, different methods have their limitations. One of them can be seen by simple calculation: arbitrary weights with bits yield possible matrices. With a fixed decoder, a description of numbers with bits selects no more than variants. If , it's impossible to describe all possible weight matrices without loss.

Finally, let's move on to the most interesting part and examine specific generator architectures.

Trainable Generator: From Hypernetwork to Coordinates

Perhaps the most obvious way to solve the problem of "generating some seemingly random nonsense" is to use a neural network. This approach is called a hypernetwork: a network that generates the weights of another network. In HyperNetworks, its input is a trainable description of the layer, and its output is the required filters. In the static version, the hypernetwork reconstructs dense convolution kernels. They can be unfolded in advance and then used with a regular network. Layer-wise reconstruction would allow not keeping them all simultaneously – this is one implementation option.

For Wide ResNet 40-2 – a convolutional network with skip connections between layers and an increased number of channels – on CIFAR-10, a dataset of small color images of ten classes, the number of parameters decreases from 2.236 million to 0.148 million, but the classification error increases from 5.66% to 7.23%. In this experiment, the economy came at the cost of quality: fewer parameters, but higher error.

It's worth mentioning the existence of dynamic hypernetworks – their peculiarity is that they change the generated weights of the main network depending on the input and state. But their task is usually slightly different – greater expressiveness by adjusting weights to the input and state; such networks are not necessarily smaller than the original network. In the same work, this variant is tested on RNN and LSTM for sequences. For example, on Penn Treebank texts, the network predicts the next character. A regular LSTM has an error of 1.312 bits per character, while HyperLSTM has 1.265; here, lower means better.

In the static hypernetwork, there was a layer description above the input. We can go further and feed the generator the coordinates of two neurons – and get the weight of the connection between them at the output. Such an approach was tested back in 2009 in the work HyperNEAT. Here, a small network defines the connection pattern through the geometry of neuron placement. This allows describing symmetries and repeating motifs, and the search is conducted over the parameters of this description. However, the task here is to evolutionarily construct a suitable network, not to pack the weights of an already trained one.

This was tested, for example, on a simple visual task: finding the center of a large square next to a small one. The quality was assessed by the distance to the correct center. The generator found on an 11x11 grid was then used for a 55x55 grid without a new evolutionary search. The resulting networks had an average of 8.3 million connections; the authors found no statistically significant drop in quality with such an increase in resolution. §4–5, Figure 10

The coordinate-based approach can also be used for an already trained network. In NeRN, the coordinates are the layer, filter, and channel numbers: a five-layer fully connected network takes them as input and outputs the convolution kernel. Here, there is already a trained CNN, which the authors want to represent through a small predictor.

But similarity of individual digits alone was not enough: a small error in a weight can significantly change the network's output. Therefore, the predictor is trained to reproduce not only weights but also outputs and intermediate features of the original model. Seed and Latent: From Kilobyte Networks to LLM Blocks and Adapters

So far, our recipe has been another neural network, which also had to be stored. Can we make it even shorter? In Kilobyte Models, the authors keep the seed – a number by which a pseudorandom number generator reproduces the same sequence – and a small trainable vector. This vector is called a latent: its values define the model's position within a pre-selected family. The generator here is fixed: the random basis and initial weights are restored from the seed, and then a pre-defined non-linear formula is applied. There is no need to store a separate trained network for this.

Here, it's easy to imagine a magic archiver: feed it any model, get a couple of kilobytes. But compactness is built in during training – the authors train the model within a limited family that the generator can reproduce. The seed itself does not contain knowledge about the task; it must fit into the latent. In the mode described below, the recipe consists of the seed, scale, and 4,096 four-bit latent values; such a short representation is achieved by training with quantization in mind.

The idea is tested on small convolutional networks, fully connected networks for tabular data, and recurrent LSTM. For example, a CNN with 105,866 parameters on MNIST, a dataset of handwritten digits, achieves 98.60% accuracy compared to 98.99% for the full FP32 model. On Fashion-MNIST, where objects of clothing need to be distinguished instead of digits, the result is 89.11% versus 91.06%. Still not bad, but the limited capacity of the recipe becomes more noticeable.

budgets

Disk – a compact description of the selected CNN occupies 2,060 bytes.

Memory – full peak with generation is not specified; after reconstruction, the network operates with regular dense weights.

Time – no measurement with reconstruction; the computations of the forward pass itself are not reduced. The authors explicitly state: "do not compress inference compute." Limitations

Codebook, Trajectory, and Block Computation

Now let's look at another way to approximate an already trained large model. One short latent might be too restrictive, but no one forbids storing many small codes and a general method for decoding them. It's like replacing repeated words in a text with numbers from a dictionary – only here, the reconstructed weight pieces might differ slightly from the original.

In PocketLLM, such a dictionary is called a codebook. It's a table of latent vectors: an index selects a row, and a small neural decoder turns it into a group of weights. The entire recipe includes indices, the codebook, and the decoder. After loading, the decoded groups are reassembled into layer matrices.

The method is tested on Llama2-7B and Qwen3-14B language transformers. For Llama2-7B, with 2.98 bits per compressed weight, the average accuracy on five tasks after fine-tuning is 64.07% compared to the original 64.29%. Without fine-tuning, it's 59.53%; at 1.60 bits, even with fine-tuning – 55.45%. This means the small file size is bought not only by the choice of code but also by additional training.

Disk – 2.98 bits per compressed weight in the given mode; the full file size is not specified. Memory – peak not specified; reconstructed weights, activations, and KV-cache are needed.

A general decoder, however, doesn't have to be a neural network. In Hyper-Compression, the authors use the trajectory of a dynamic system – an irrational torus winding. If we remove the geometric name, the recipe looks quite simple. For each group of weights, a numerical code is selected. Multiplying it by given coefficients and taking the fractional parts of the results allows approximating this group. For different groups, their own codes of limited length, scales, and auxiliary parameters are preserved.

In the mathematical model, the trajectory can approach the desired points arbitrarily closely. But the number of the suitable point on it will also have to be recorded, and a short code might not be enough for a more precise approximation. There is no fractal function in this mechanism. To avoid waiting for the entire model to unpack, the authors propose reconstructing the next layers in parallel with the operation of the previous ones. The decoding cost remains, but part of it can be hidden behind computations.

However, there is work where compact code has already been tested for the speed of an entire LLM. In QTIP, instead of independently selecting a codebook row, a trellis code is used. Its state shifts as bits are read, and the sequence of states defines the reconstructed weights. Here, a long sequence is encoded at once: this allows considering its structure without needing a huge table of all possible combinations.

QTIP, left panel Figure 1. A weight block is encoded by a path on the state trellis; the weight value is defined by the decoder of the selected state. p. 2

The value corresponding to a state does not necessarily need to be stored in a table. QTIP has variants where it is calculated by a few simple instructions, and a hybrid variant where a formula selects a row from a small table. Before encoding, the authors also change the weight basis with a randomized Hadamard transform. The pseudorandom decoder itself does not recognize weights: the required sequence of states is selected during compression. §3

How Close Are LLMs to Weak Devices

So, the key drawing indeed works in some cases. Weights can be stored as recipes, blocks can be reconstructed directly within computation, and a single set of parameters can be used multiple times. But, as you've noticed, similar words hide quite different achievements: kilobytes for small networks, fractal functions for approximation, many local codes for LLMs, spectral coefficients and simple factors, common layers for repeated passes.

But, as we see from the approaches discussed, we are still very far from the "teapot" stage. The works reviewed so far do not present a technology that would allow running complex neural networks, especially LLMs, on weak devices. Optimizing the size of the "cold" model is not enough – we still need to address memory and computational complexity. Even if we are willing to wait a long time for computation results, intermediate data must be stored somewhere or recomputed, which, in my opinion, imposes more serious limitations at the moment.

I believe the direction from the somewhat bleak assessment above does not make the goal less interesting. There are many directions that bring the desired result of "pocket AI" closer, and sooner or later, this goal will be achieved.
