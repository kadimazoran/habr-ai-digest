Sam Altman said something that caught my attention. On the podcast “Sources,” he stated: “I’m seeing the first signs that this is getting sillier than silly: new neoclowds appearing out of nowhere, people saying they’re going to build massive amounts of compute next year—for which, I think, they have neither revenue nor a buyer.”

This statement was made by a person whose company is the world’s largest consumer of GPU compute. OpenAI has contracts with CoreWeave, Nebius, Lambda, and IREN, and their skepticism is directed at young companies that have neither revenue nor a buyer.

The largest consumer is drawing attention to an oversupply. What does he mean? I’ve gathered financial reports and deal chronologies for these companies from open sources. Next, we’ll break down the mechanics: how a neocloud makes money, why it borrows, what’s happening with GPU rental rates, and under what conditions this entire architecture could collapse? If you prefer a local perspective, the second part of this series covers the same topics from the viewpoint of those building in Russia, Kazakhstan, and Belarus.

Disclaimer: This is an engineering analysis, not investment advice.

Everyone is in the Bubble Except Us

First, some context. In August 2025, Altman already spoke about a bubble—and it sounded carefree then: “Do I think there’s a bubble? Yes. Are people overexcited? I think the world as a whole, yes.” He considered everyone overexcited except himself: OpenAI, according to him, would emerge unscathed.

The September phrasing is different: now it’s specifically about plans to increase computing power worldwide. He clearly defended his plans: “I’m not worried about our plans. We’ll be able to profitably use all the compute we planned to build.” And he added a new note: “I definitely feel fear about what the world is doing as a whole—though we are very happy with what we’ve agreed to ourselves.”

I don’t know if this is sincerity or narrative management. Most likely, it’s both. But the conflict of interest here is structural, and we need to discuss it. According to Ed Zitron’s calculations, OpenAI owes approximately $748 billion to Microsoft, Oracle, and Amazon—these are their remaining performance obligations (future revenue tied to OpenAI)—plus tens of billions more to smaller neoclowds. Every word Altman utters against “overbuilding” increases rental prices for everyone but him. The market’s largest debtor is telling the market that the foolishness lies in others’ construction, not his plans.

But the thesis has a basis. Let’s look at the numbers.

The “Insider” Was Late

Altman said this on September 1st, in the debut episode of Alex Heath’s “Sources.” Two weeks prior, investors had already begun selling neocloud stocks.

On August 14th, WSJ reported that NVIDIA was scaling back its planned guarantee for OpenAI’s data center from $250 billion to less than $120 billion—alleviating investor concerns about risk. On August 18th, CoreWeave lost 7%, Nebius—5%, TeraWulf—6%. Attributing this decline solely to sentiment would be a stretch, even without Altman’s words: three catalysts were at play that day. Anthropic reported annual revenue of $65 billion—the market whisper had expected $80+ billion. WSJ calculated that the nine largest tech companies hold about $3 trillion in off-balance-sheet obligations, growing faster than traditional capital expenditures. And the yield on 30-year Treasuries reached 5.33%—a nineteen-year high.

Altman’s remarks came out when the sell-off was already underway, confirming that the market had begun re-evaluating neoclowds before he did. And it got worse: in early September, Nebius plunged 14% intraday amid a $4.5 billion convertible bond issuance.

The “insider” whom the market listens to turned out to be behind the market. This doesn’t negate his thesis—on the contrary, it strengthens it: he came to the podcast not with a forecast, but with a report.

Neocloud Anatomy: How the Debt Machine Works

Let’s start with the term. A neocloud (“new cloud”) is a specialized provider built entirely around one task: buying thousands of GPUs, connecting them with a high-speed network, and leasing them to those training and servicing AI models. Amazon, Microsoft, and Google have clouds that are universal environments for thousands of services; such giants are called hyperscalers. Their new GPUs are allocated to internal services and long-term corporate contracts, so a large block of new chips there means a queue and long commitments. A neocloud, however, is built for a single product—pure compute: it will offer the latest chips more and faster than any such queue.

CoreWeave is the largest public neocloud and the best case for dissection. In Q2 2026, it reported as follows (Capacity):

Revenue growth of double in a year is a fact. A loss of $626 million for the quarter with interest payments of a billion for the half-year is also a fact. These two facts do not negate each other—both are in the same report: revenue is growing while the company breathes borrowed oxygen.

The financing scheme is simple but risky. A neocloud takes out loans secured by the GPUs themselves and by the future cash flows of specific contracts. A bank issues a loan of 60–70 cents for every dollar of the fleet’s value—according to analysts; financiers call the loan-to-value ratio the loan-to-value. Delayed-draw term loans are credit lines that are disbursed in tranches as chips are delivered. You buy a fleet of H100s for a contract with a major client—take the contract to the bank, get money for the next fleet.

The algorithm works as long as the backlog—the total volume of signed but not yet fulfilled contracts—grows: new signatures become collateral for new loans.

The problem lies in the timing: CoreWeave’s main obligations are due in a narrow window of 2026–2028. Repaying them from operating revenue is unrealistic—old loans will have to be closed with new ones; in finance, this is called refinancing, and when repayment deadlines converge into one period, the “refinancing wall” occurs—in Russian financial practice, this is called the debt repayment peak. Analysts are already applying this term to the entire neocloud class.

The fate of the “wall” depends on two questions: will the contracts survive their term, and how much will GPUs be worth as collateral when the repayment dates arrive.

A separate category consists of former Bitcoin miners: IREN, Hut 8, Bitdeer, Cipher Mining. CoreWeave, by the way, comes from the same lineage—it started as an Ethereum mining farm. The others are pouring their own balance sheets into GPU campuses, and their first long-term contracts have already appeared (24/7 Wall St): IREN reported $4 billion ARR (annual recurring revenue) for 2026 capacity, Hut 8—949 megawatts with an expected contract value of $26.6 billion. But the risk structure remains the worst in the industry. Miners’ debt is their own and expensive, their contract history is short, and energy for their campuses is a separate headache. These are the ones Altman was referring to when he spoke of “no revenue, no buyer”—the most speculative layer of the market.

Physics vs. Accounting: GPUs as a Melting Ice Cube

For me, as an engineer, this is where it gets interesting. GPUs are depreciating assets, and their economic life is shorter than their accounting life.

The first time I saw this gap was not with GPUs, but at a nuclear power plant heat exchanger factory where I worked as a programmer. The plant was preparing to implement an ERP system, which required an upgrade of servers and user machines. IT submitted a budget request—the finance department rejected it: the equipment had only been replaced two years ago, depreciation was accounted for over seven years, why were we paying again? The question was legitimate. The answer, unfortunately, was also: two years ago, during the purchase, they looked at the retrospective—how long the old hardware had worked—and copied that period into the future. No one analytically calculated the impact of the upcoming ERP: neither the risks nor the potential. The equipment, which had worked normally for two years, became obsolete the day the requirements were approved. Since then, I’ve kept a simple rule in mind: hardware purchased for today’s goals may become obsolete in two years—goals and technologies change faster than the depreciation schedule.

CoreWeave depreciates GPU equipment linearly over six years. On paper, the chip evenly loses value for six years. In reality, the curve is different: as long as the current generation is the frontier, the rental rate holds; as soon as the next generation comes out at a comparable price per flop, the old chip slides into cheap tiers—inference, batch tasks, small clients. Hopper gave way to Blackwell, Blackwell to Blackwell Ultra, Vera Rubin is already on the horizon. The chip doesn’t break—it stops earning the same money. On paper, its price falls in a straight line; in reality, it plummets. Debt gets stuck in the gap between them: the chip is already worth less than what remains to be paid off.

And rental rates are indeed falling. According to independent trackers, an hour of H100 in mid-2026 costs from $1.50 on discount marketplaces to ~$7 from premium providers, with a median of about $3.30. In early 2024, it was over $7. A chip for which a loan was taken out, calculated at $7 per hour, now brings in $3.30 on average.

But there’s a counterargument. Jensen Huang said differently at the Davos Forum: try to rent NVIDIA GPUs—it’s incredibly difficult, and spot rental prices are rising, even for chips from the previous two generations. Huang’s logic: scarcity is universal, everything is getting more expensive—including old hardware.

The trackers’ data for H100 says the opposite: this chip is getting cheaper, not more expensive. The discrepancy is explained by us looking at different segments. The frontier B200 and B300 are indeed scarce and expensive—here, Huang is right. And a used H100 is getting cheaper along the classic obsolescence curve—and here, the trackers are right. For the borrower, it’s a matter of survival: if rates rise, they live; if they fall, they go bankrupt.

NVIDIA's Backstop: Compute Becomes a Financial Instrument

The industry’s response to the refinancing wall emerged in July 2026, and it’s discussed far less than it deserves. The mechanism is called a “backstop”—in finance, this denotes a lender of last resort guarantee: if the primary payer defaults, the guarantor pays.

NVIDIA introduced its version: the company agrees to lease back unused neocloud GPU capacity at a fixed rate. For the lender, this guarantees a minimum utilization: the borrower’s default risk decreases, and loans are issued more readily. The first deal is Sharon AI, six years, 40,000 GPUs. The mechanism sits under a $500 billion platform that NVIDIA has assembled with institutional partners—Apollo, BlackRock, Goldman Sachs; NVIDIA’s own backstop potential within the platform is up to $125 billion.

The stress test didn’t pass. On July 27th, WSJ wrote about plans for a $250 billion guarantee for OpenAI and SoftBank’s 10-gigawatt data center in Ohio. On August 14th, the same WSJ reported: the plan was scaled back to less than $120 billion—to alleviate investor concerns about risk. On August 17th, Reuters confirmed the deal: a guarantee of up to $105 billion for the first 4.25 gigawatts of the same campus. It took two weeks for the guarantor to become fearful of its own risk—and by scaling back the plan, it still signed.

Huang already calls AI compute an “investable asset class.” It sounds good, but read it twice: the chip manufacturer provides guarantees for the debts of its customers, who take out these debts to buy its chips.

The question is: does the backstop eliminate refinancing risk or simply transfer it to NVIDIA’s balance sheet? Formally, it transfers it.

And the exposure is growing horizontally, not just vertically. NVIDIA has been an investor in Texas developer Lancium since 2022: its Clean Campus in Abilene is building Stargate Abilene. The bottleneck in AI construction today is not GPUs, but electricity and sites; a stake in the energy layer insures the chip sales channel. Plus, the credit risk of the entire lower layer of the market through backstops.

If the residual value of GPUs between generations holds—the structure amortizes as planned. If not—the same company will be simultaneously the collateral, the guarantor, and the seller of the collateral.

This is how schemes that were called vendor financing in 1999 worked: Lucent and Nortel provided credit to telecom operators to purchase their own equipment. Lucent alone accumulated $8.1 billion in customer loan obligations—and wrote off $4.2 billion in unrecoverable debt. Nortel in 2001 recorded a record loss of $27.3 billion with a write-off of $3.1 billion in vendor financing and went bankrupt in 2009.

The Construction Relies on Two Whales

Now, the figure around which the entire bubble debate revolves.

Hyperscalers do not disclose AI revenue separately. But analysts calculate it from indirect data, and their estimates converge in one place:

  • Amazon (Barclays, Ross Sandler): OpenAI and Anthropic account for approximately 73% of all AI revenue in 2026–2027 and 75% in 2028.

  • Google Cloud (UBS, Stephen Ju): the same two clients account for 28% of revenue in 2026 and about 48% in 2027.

  • Microsoft (Wells Fargo): 70%+ of AI revenue; the share of the two labs in Azure is growing from 23% to 35% over a year.

Now let’s put these shares into perspective with the scale of construction. Amazon plans $220 billion in capital expenditures in 2026 (CNBC)—and Amazon’s total AI revenue estimate for this year, according to Barclays, is $9.6 billion (The AI Demand Bubble). The construction is gigantic, but there is almost no broad demand for it.

Bain estimates that by 2030, the industry will need $2 trillion in annual revenue to cover everything that is currently being built. According to their calculations, there is a shortfall of about $800 billion.

Disclaimer: all these figures are analyst estimates, not company disclosures. But the conclusion is stark: the “insatiable demand for compute” that justifies this construction boom is measured primarily by two clients. Two companies that do not earn enough themselves to pay for computing power, and who are funded by the same investors who are building data centers for them. I call these labs model developers—OpenAI, Anthropic, and the like. The circle closes like this: a hyperscaler invests in a lab → the lab pays the hyperscaler for rent → revenue grows → the market demands more construction.

Money circulates, and with each turn, the circle demands expansion.

Here is this circle in action. In September 2025, NVIDIA announced “up to $100 billion” for OpenAI for 10 gigawatts—the chip seller promised money to its buyer. In January, WSJ reported: the deal is “on ice”—NVIDIA had doubts. There wasn’t $100 billion: in March, OpenAI received $30 billion from NVIDIA in a $110 billion round—a third of what was promised.

And then Huang publicly limited his appetite: this might be the last time, and $100 billion is probably not in the cards anymore, because “they will go public.” The chip seller reduced support for its largest client—and warned that there might not be a new one.

But Microsoft has an objection. CFO Amy Hood said on the earnings call on July 29th that almost 90% of the company’s cloud revenue comes from clients outside the two main labs. However, Microsoft’s term “cloud” encompasses a lot: Office subscriptions (M365), LinkedIn, CRM services (Dynamics)—all bundled together with AI capacity rentals. Because of this, the denial doesn’t deny anything: even if 90% is true, almost all of the company’s AI business could be in the remaining 10%. Microsoft does not publish a breakdown that would clarify everything—and that in itself is an answer.

What the Optimists Say—and They Aren’t Foolish

To avoid a one-sided view—here’s the other side.

Demand growth is real. Anthropic reported $65 billion ARR (annual recurring revenue) as of the end of July 2026—in February, it was $14 billion. OpenAI has a $40 billion ARR. ChatGPT has 800 million weekly users and a million business clients. 71% of organizations use generative AI in at least one function. This is not the dot-com lunacy of the late nineties, when companies burned money on homepages without revenue—this is mass adoption.

The difference from the dot-com era is one thing: there is profit now. At the peak in 2000, only 14% of internet companies were profitable. Today’s leaders are earning real money—NVIDIA alone has revenue of $215.9 billion and a net margin of 55.6% (NVIDIA report). The core of the bubble, if it is a bubble, consists of profitable companies with real cash flows, not Pets.com with its sock puppet mascot—that dot-com symbol that burned out in a couple of years (old-timers will understand).

The strongest argument against a direct analogy with 2000 is made by Jitu Patel from Cisco: in the late nineties, supply preceded demand—operators buried fiber optic cables in advance, and they lay unused for years. Now, according to him, it’s the opposite: there is demand, but supply is scarce in terms of electricity, sites, memory, and networks. Data centers really take years to build, and capacity is genuinely lacking. In 1999, fiber optics lay in the ground unused; today, GPUs are turned on the day they are delivered.

Altman isn’t the only one who can talk about risks. Franklin Templeton: “Circular financing will end badly. You are not just living on borrowed time—you are living on leverage.” However, the system’s leverage (borrowed money), according to their assessment, is not yet at alarming levels. Even Torsten Sløk from Apollo, the loudest of the skeptical economists (“top 10 S&P are more overvalued than in the 1990s”), frames the risk as a matter of time, not a death sentence: “Capital can bridge the gap for some time, but not indefinitely. Will the ROI for end customers materialize quickly enough?”

And one more thing—not an argument, but a warning. In July 2026, hedge fund Situational Awareness collapsed—its manager, a former OpenAI employee, was right about the direction (AI is growing) but ruined by leverage and timing. Similarly, in March 2000, Julian Robertson’s Tiger Management closed—he correctly identified the bubble a few months before the crash. In the market, the one who is right in time is right.

Three Scenarios

I cannot predict. But I see several scenarios.

Scenario 1: Not everyone will survive, but the market will hold. The market will split in two. Those with long-term contracts—CoreWeave with a $104 billion backlog, Nebius—will survive the expensive money. The speculative layer—miners building on their own debt and thin contracts—will disappear through defaults and acquisitions. It will be painful: refinancing will have to happen at the peak, and private credit dries up mid-construction. The bottom cards of the house of cards are pulled out first. But the market won’t collapse—the demand from the two labs and hyperscalers will sustain the core. The first signs are already there: the gap between the strong and the weak is widening.

Scenario 2: Correction through rates. A milder version of the same: with 30-year bond yields above 5.3%, the economics of every new data center worsen, hyperscalers’ capital expenditure growth slows by 20–30%, NVIDIA loses its growth momentum, a year and a half to two years of sideways movement with isolated defaults. Infrastructure is completed, demand catches up—like the fiber optics of the 2000s, consumed by Web 2.0. A bubble in terms of overvaluation, but not a lost decade.

Scenario 3: Collapse through contract breach. The worst-case scenario is built from specific details, not an abstract “bubble.” The trigger is the breach or restructuring of one of the anchor contracts—pulling the supporting card from the house of cards. OpenAI cannot meet Oracle’s payment schedule ($300 billion over five years with current revenue an order of magnitude less). Oracle—already a step away from junk rating—drags Stargate project finance with it. Private credit freezes, neoclowds without long-term contracts default, NVIDIA discovers its backstops are more expensive than it seemed. I cannot estimate the probability of this scenario—but unlike 1999, the chains here are shorter and tighter: contracts are signed, ratings are being downgraded, not just “sentiments are changing.”

I won’t place my bet—I’m an engineer, albeit a former financier. But honestly, I expect not a crash, but acquisitions. Neoclouds with large contracts will survive the expensive money; smaller ones built “for the future” without contracts will be bought by those who already have clients and money. And a side effect of this outcome—one that engineers should watch—is that token prices are currently held up by hype, but as the newly built capacity is utilized, they will go down. A bubble that ends in acquisitions is a favorable outcome for those who consume compute, not those who sell it.

What to watch to understand which scenario will unfold:

  • the share of retail in trading volumes (currently 20%, with no days of net sales in July);

  • yields on 30-year Treasuries;

  • the first public default or restructuring among renegade miners;

  • and most importantly—the hyperscalers’ Q4 2026 earnings report: will capital expenditure growth slow down.

Instead of a Conclusion

Let’s return to the quote. “Sillier than silly”—that’s how I would translate “unsustainable silliness”: Altman chose the word “silliness,” not “madness,” but with one clarification he omitted: silliness grows where he himself knocked with contracts worth tens of billions. The “silliness” here is not based on the neocloud class—it’s based on debt taken out against a depreciating asset whose rental price is falling faster than the repayment schedule. Some players have a hundred billion backlog under this debt, others have faith that “someone will pay even more.”

I build systems in this market, and I have one conclusion: ultimately, the one chosen by the user will survive—they vote with their rubles. Anthropic and OpenAI are competitors, and both neoclowds and hyperscalers depend on their demand. This entire structure is a multi-story house of cards: if users leave one, the debts of the other will drag down the debts of neoclowds and clouds, and that part will collapse. And the same in reverse. The infrastructure will likely remain and feed the next wave. Shareholders of the leveraged layer will not survive this. Engineers will survive if they design in advance so that changing providers is not a catastrophe.

If I were asked, I would say this: build only with a contract in hand or at least a letter of intent, and only for specific workloads. Buying hardware at current memory prices in the hope of future prospects is almost suicide.

While Altman warns of silliness, the market votes with money. Anthropic confidentially filed for an IPO back in June and, according to the FT, is targeting October with a valuation of around $2 trillion—exceeding SpaceX’s record. OpenAI is waiting until 2027 for a trillion-dollar valuation—but SoftBank, with a $40 billion bridge loan due in March 2027, cannot wait.

What do you think—is this 1999 with fiber optics that Web 2.0 later consumed, or 2000 a month before the turn? And most importantly: have you checked whose balance sheet your production compute capacity is on?
