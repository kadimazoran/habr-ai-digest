I find myself too often debating the cyber-risks of open models with people who start from one of two assumptions. First: if we ban open models here, it will somehow stop bad actors, as if the ban operates in a vacuum. Second: China is a threat, and AI safety doesn't concern them. After such conversations, it feels like we're heading towards policy decisions that will hurt American AI competitiveness and also significantly increase cyber-risks for a long time. We're stuck on a path where everyone loses, and we can only get off it by admitting that things are more complex than they seem, that compromises are unavoidable, and that the reality is quite frightening.

Much has been written about the positions of the participants in this debate. I categorize them into three camps:

  1. Open-weight models pose an unacceptable risk to the normal functioning of society. This is the view held by leaders of frontier labs and people in US national security.

  2. Open weights are necessary for defense, and banning them will make the world less safe. This is the stance of Western proponents of a moderate view on AI risks, though moderation varies. I count myself among them, as do Hugging Face with their position after the OpenAI incident, and people like Joshua Saxe.

  3. Chinese companies that continue to release open models with strong cyber capabilities. They make decisions based on how their society and government assess risks.

The order in the list also reflects how loudly and clearly each camp is heard in the Western AI ecosystem. Discussions about risks are disproportionately prominent, and among them are works that, in my opinion, significantly hinder dialogue between the camps, even though the boundaries between them are largely imaginary. However, few try to understand in substance how Chinese companies assess risks. Instead, arguments verge on personal attacks like "Chinese labs don't care about safety."
