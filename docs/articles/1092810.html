A child simply sees hundreds of falling objects, and an image forms in their mind: things fall, hard objects break on impact, liquids spill. They "absorbed" physics from observations, not from a textbook.

Now imagine that a neural network can be taught the same way. Not by showing it thousands of hours of the same task, but by letting it watch a huge amount of video so it can understand how the world works on its own. And then attach arms, wheels, or propellers to it and explain what is expected of it with literally tens of hours of data.

This is precisely what Odyssey is betting on. On September 15, 2026, they announced Odyssey-3, and on October 8, they opened a research preview for everyone. In this article, we will break down what this model is, how it was trained, where the record-breaking figures come from, and where facts end and marketing begins.

▍ What is a World Model

Large language models predict the next word. A world model predicts the next state of the world: it takes what was before, adds an action (a step forward, a turn of the wheel, hitting an object), and outputs how everything will look next.

Odyssey describes Odyssey-3 as a trainable dynamic system that predicts how objects move and interact in space and how situations evolve over time. The model gained knowledge of physics, dynamics, and cause-and-effect relationships from a broad set of visual observations. This knowledge can then be used in two ways: as an environment simulator and as a foundation for training control policies for various machines.

The idea is not entirely new. Back in 2025, Odyssey demonstrated interactive video that reacted to actions in real-time. At the time, it was more about a new form of media. Now, the claim is more serious: general intelligence for physical and virtual systems.

▍ Who is Behind This

Odyssey's founders, Oliver Cameron and Jeff Hawk, spent about a decade working on self-driving cars. Their logic is understandable: for a car to decide what to do next, it needs to predict what will happen around it. The company was founded in 2023 with the idea of taking this concept far beyond roads.

They also honestly describe the industry's problem. Robotics has been progressing through increasingly narrow systems, each tailored to a specific task and requiring a vast amount of data for that task alone. The authors call this brute force: there's a lack of general understanding of the world, and this deficiency is compensated for by mountains of demonstrations. A person, by their analogy, can sit at a dangerous machine at eighteen and learn to operate it because they have spent their entire life observing how objects move, react to force, and how dangerous collisions are.

▍ How the Model Works

The overall architecture is an autoregressive diffusion transformer. Let's break it down.

Data. The training dataset consists of three sources:

  • internet videos with temporally annotated and schema-verified events;

  • gameplay recordings with synchronized key presses and mouse movements;

  • simulations of rigid body interactions with captions and metadata.

The first provides breadth, the second connects action to consequence, and the third provides precise physics where one can know exactly what happened and why.

Training. First, Odyssey builds a multi-step video diffusion transformer that can accept prompts and control signals that change over time. Then, the model is made autoregressive using teacher forcing and causal masks. Simply put, during training, it is shown the actual previous frames and forbidden from peeking into the future, and it learns to continue the world considering the actions.

Distillation. A diffusion model typically takes many steps per frame, which is too slow for real-time gameplay. Therefore, Odyssey applied post-training combining distribution-matching and adversarial distillation to obtain a lightweight version that operates in a few steps. This is a "student" that imitates the "teacher" but faster. This is the version you can interact with in the browser.

Sizes. There are two models: Odyssey-3 with a resolution of 832x480 and Odyssey-3 Pro with 1280x720. In the preview, you can navigate the generated world from a first-person or third-person perspective, move the camera separately, and inject events during generation to see how the world reacts. The number of parameters is not disclosed in the materials I read.

▍ From Pixels to Hardware

The most interesting part begins when the world model is turned into the brain for machines. The scheme is as follows: the base remains frozen, and a small trainable component is added to it, which translates the model's internal representations into commands for a specific device. This requires "observation, action" pairs, and only a small amount of them are needed.

Robotic Manipulators. With tens of hours of demonstrations, the model performs tasks like "pour cereal into a bowl" or "close a box of screws." The authors specifically note the recovery behavior that was not present in the demonstrations: the robot reorients its gripper after a miss and picks up an object that has fallen in an awkward position. This is exactly what usually requires demonstrations of all possible failures. To test on different bodies and environments, Odyssey is collaborating with Poke & Wiggle.

Humanoids. Flexion has built humanoid control policies based on Odyssey-3. According to the authors, they generalize better to environmental changes than tested VLA-based models and continue to work under lighting changes that cause the base models to fail. The specific base models compared are not specified in the announcement.

Cars. A policy trained on 20 hours of simulated driving, with the base frozen, drives in a closed loop on Indian streets, predicting route points ahead. An interesting figure: policies trained entirely in simulation drove about 77% of the distance of policies trained on real recordings between safety driver interventions.

Drones. A navigation policy trained on tens of hours of simulated flights showed stable flight with obstacle avoidance, but in a simulated environment.

Games. Policies based on Odyssey-3 played GTA V. There's also a hint of transfer learning: a model trained on about two hours of GTA could move while riding a horse in Red Dead Redemption 2 and drive a motorcycle in Sleeping Dogs without further training on these games.

Multi-camera data. After only 100 training steps, a checkpoint produced video from three front cameras of a car simultaneously.

Notice how much "simulation" is mentioned here. This is not a criticism, but a way to read the announcement: some demonstrations show that the approach works, but the real world with its noise and rare situations remains the main test.

▍ The World as a Training Ground for Other AI

A separate line of development: if the model can generate environments, other agents can be run and trained within them. In a demonstration, an agent receives a goal in natural language and tries to achieve it by observing the generated world.

Behind this lies the idea of recursive learning, which Odyssey is developing in the PROWL project: agents find weaknesses in the world model, which helps improve it, and reliable simulated worlds, in turn, train agents. The better the world, the more complex the experience; the stronger the agents, the more hidden flaws they find. Nearby are Agora-2, where multiple humans or AIs act simultaneously in one world, and Starchild-1, which attempts to go beyond training solely on visual observations.

There's also a safety aspect. The authors write that such worlds allow studying how increasingly capable agents behave and identifying dangerous behavior before it manifests in the physical world.

▍ A Skeptical View

Let's add a dose of realism, because the announcement is quite polished.

  • The record 66.1 was achieved in best-of-8 mode, and the WorldMark evaluations were conducted by Odyssey itself.

  • Cherry-picking: we see successful videos, but we don't know how many unsuccessful ones there were.

  • Video physics does not equal control quality. Physics-IQ checks how well the model continues an experiment, not how well a machine drives based on it.

  • Many demonstrations (cars, drones) are based on simulators, and "tens of hours of data" still represent a significant amount, albeit less than before.

  • A third-party review notes that public pricing, licenses, and API documentation were not available at the time of its release. Access for physical system developers, according to the website, is still by request.

  • Phrases like "the most physically accurate world model in the world" remain social media buzzwords. Only a specific benchmark confirms them.

In defense of the authors: they themselves write that world models are still about two orders of magnitude behind language models in scale, and they admit that the question of result stability across different robots and environments remains open.

▍ Conclusion

Odyssey-3 is an attempt to do for the physical world what large language models did for text: a single pre-trained foundation onto which a steering wheel, a manipulator, or a propeller can be attached with a small amount of data. For now, it's a mix of real achievements and promises that still need to be verified in the real world.

It's very interesting to follow, and you can try the preview today.
