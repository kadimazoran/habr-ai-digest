What the task was

When we first launched the Avito Ads cabinet in June 2025, we regularly read all user comments from beginning to end. This greatly helped us prioritize the backlog more accurately and improve the service faster.

A year later, the number of users increased significantly, and the product team grew and divided responsibilities. Reading, systematizing, and distributing the entire flow manually became difficult.

Avito Ads is a tool that helps businesses attract potential customers' attention to their goods and services.

With the help of advertising banners and videos, companies can:

  • increase reach and maintain brand image;

  • prompt the user to take a target action: make a purchase, leave an application, or participate in a promotion;

  • attract interested customers to their website, social media, or app.

We needed to find duplicates (messages from the same user on the same topic), extract and save multiple meanings within a single comment, assess the weight of topics, distribute feedback among teams, and prepare meaningful summaries.

What is more important: the same problem for several users who launch hundreds of campaigns, or for a hundred users with one or two campaigns? This is what is meant by weights.

At the same time, the labeling had to strictly follow the rules formulated by UX researchers, and the user's original text had to be preserved for subsequent analysis.

How we arrived at the pipeline

First, we tried to solve the problem with a single agent: we gave it the comments, a large list of rules, and asked it to return the finished labels. It worked for short examples, but the quality decreased with a long queue. A single model had to simultaneously understand the text, remember hundreds of similar rules, check its own choices, adhere to the format, and work with data.

Therefore, we further divided the process into independent mini-roles. The semantic decisions were left to the AI agents, and the repeatable and verifiable operations were handed over to Python. This way, the knowledge of UX researchers is applied consistently to the entire queue, and the output is structured feedback suitable for product work.

Glossary

  • Semantic topic — a single independent thought of the user that may require a separate product reaction.

  • Concern — the name of a specific pain point, question, or product request.

  • Tag — a broader product area: moderation, finance, targeting, support, and others.

  • Taxonomy — a general directory of concerns, tags, and conditions for their application.

  • Sub-agent — a separate AI executor with one role, a short instruction, and a clean context.

  • Bucket — a group of up to 50 comments that goes through the entire pipeline as an independent part of the run.

  • Manifest — a service list of buckets, their statuses, and processing results.

One comment is not always one problem

Users provide feedback in free form and may simultaneously mention moderation, statistics, support, and budget. If you immediately look for only one suitable label, some information will inevitably be lost.

For example, the phrase "The ad was rejected without a clear explanation, support isn't responding, and there's not enough statistics on creatives" contains three topics: unclear rejection reason, slow support response, and insufficient creative statistics.

The unit of analysis is not the entire comment, but the semantic topic within it. The system first identifies all independent topics, then classifies each, and only after that combines the results into the labeling of the original comment. This approach is close to the principle of atomicity in research knowledge bases: an observation is broken down into minimal independent units that can be reused in different analytical slices.

Why one agent is not enough

Because different types of work are mixed in one task!

Finding all thoughts, choosing the correct rule, performing a check, adhering to the format, and recording data are all different tasks. When a model performs them simultaneously, it results in a mess that a human has to sort out. But we strive for increased efficiency, not the opposite.

If you entrust AI with feedback analysis, you need to remember three axioms:

  1. Self-checking is never fully independent. After selecting a concern, the model "remembers" how it made the decision and tends to confirm it during re-checking. A separate checker receives the result in a new context and is therefore able to notice a discrepancy with the text or rule.

  2. Large context blurs attention. Hundreds of comments, similar categories, and long instructions compete for the model's attention. The broader the context, the higher the risk of combining different topics, missing a rare rule, or mistaking the completion of one stage for the end of the entire task.

  3. Agents also get tired and lazy! After working for a long time, the model starts to simplify decisions more often. The reason usually lies in an overloaded context, multiple competing tasks, or an insufficiently clear completion criterion.

For tasks like ours, something like a microservices architecture is required, but applied to AI agents.

How the agent team is structured

Our system includes a managing agent, a Python assistant, a run controller, and several semantic sub-agents. Each receives only the data necessary for its specific role.

The Python assistant forms the queue: it retrieves unlabeled comments from a single source, records the sample composition and the current version of the rules, divides the queue into buckets, and creates a manifest. Already labeled lines are not mixed into the process.

The Segmenter identifies semantic topics: it reads the full comment, finds all independent thoughts, and saves a short text fragment that confirms each topic. At this stage, we do not show the taxonomy, so existing categories do not limit the search for meanings.

The Completeness Checker (Completeness Critic) evaluates the distribution by segments, i.e., it compares topics with the original comments: adds missing ones, separates merged ones, and removes duplicates or unconfirmed conclusions.

The Classifier applies the taxonomy (finally!). For each confirmed topic, it selects a category and a signal type. The decision is made not by similarity of names, but by extended conditions of application and restrictions for similar rules.

The Independent Checker (Rule Critic) verifies the labeling against the text and compares the selected concern with plausible alternatives. An acceptable but too broad decision receives a warning; only a clear contradiction is rejected.

The Corrector (re-classifier) fixes obvious errors, i.e., rejected decisions. This happens once. There is no repeated critique to prevent the process from turning into an endless cycle.

The Python assistant checks the response structure, extracts tags from the selected concerns, and safely records the result. A local error in one line does not delay the others.

The Run Controller takes the next bucket. The cycle continues until each bucket in the manifest receives a final status. If the controller finishes ahead of schedule, the managing agent launches a new one and continues the same fixed run.

Fantastic rules and where they live

Product logic is stored in a separate directory maintained by UX researchers and the product team. For each concern, we specify its exact name, product area, conditions of application, limitations, and a short, unambiguous example. Changing the taxonomy does not require transferring product knowledge into Python code.

Example rule

If a user writes "The ad was rejected, but it's unclear what to fix," the category "Unclear rejection reason" applies. If they ask how long a review usually takes, that's a completely different rule. Similar words are not enough: the classifier must check the meaning and the boundaries of application.

The quality of the taxonomy is more important than the number of prompts and additional checks. If two concerns describe almost the same thing, the model will hesitate even with a good instruction. In such cases, it is more useful to combine the rules or more precisely define their boundaries than to complicate the code.

What mistakes we took into account

The solution architecture was formed as a result of several test runs. The current version accounts for at least eight major problems that we observed in experiments.

  1. A long comment received only one label. The classifier quickly found a familiar concern and stopped noticing other thoughts. We moved the topic identification to a separate stage and added an independent completeness check.

  2. A rule was chosen based on a similar name. The name seemed suitable to the AI, even though a significant condition of application was not met. We explicitly separated the conditions of application and counter-examples, and the critic started receiving strong alternatives.

  3. The critic rejected acceptable solutions, sought the perfect answer, and only added chaos. We made it softer: an acceptable answer is kept, only a provable contradiction is rejected.

  4. Corrections went in circles. Each new check could trigger another re-labeling. We kept one targeted re-attempt without a second round of critique.

  5. Correct meaning was broken due to response format. A reasonable labeling did not always match the machine contract. Therefore, Python received fixed templates, normalization of harmless deviations, and error localization on a single line.

  6. The agent stopped in the middle of the queue. The completion of one stage or bucket looked like a natural end point for the entire task. We added a manifest, a separate controller, and a technical check for the completion of all buckets.

  7. Small buckets consumed too many resources. For each small group, instructions, role launches, and checks were repeated. To fix this, the bucket size was increased to 50 comments, while keeping fresh sub-agents for each group.

  8. Concern and tag diverged, the model independently filled two related fields. We stopped extracting the tag separately: Python unambiguously derives it from the concern.

How we check quality

For evaluation, we collected a control sample of 196 comments, manually labeled together with UX researchers. Comments were selected randomly, taking into account different feedback sources. Manual labeling is stored separately and is not passed to sub-agents during testing.

After the run, we compare signal types, product tags, and exact concerns. We also separately check the completeness of labeling for long comments, errors in the manual benchmark, and controversial points in the taxonomy. If the agent chose a more accurate rule, such a case is discussed with UX researchers, and the benchmark is corrected if necessary.

The main metric is Micro F1 by concerns. Complete match of the comment with the benchmark is too coarse a check. In a long review, the agent might correctly identify three topics and miss a fourth. Therefore, we separately count correct assignments, extra assignments, and omissions. Micro F1 combines precision and recall across all topics in the entire sample.

Micro F1 by concerns is 83.87%. This indicator reflects how accurately and fully the system selects specific user pain points and requests.

Micro F1 by tags is 90.27%. This means how well the identified topics are distributed across product areas.

Tags are not a separate semantic task for the model: Python derives them from the selected concerns. Therefore, the main indicator is the quality of the concerns themselves, and tags and signal types serve as additional diagnostic slices.

The benchmark also needs checking

Users often write too briefly, and the text does not allow for determining a single correct concern. For example, the phrase "I can't top up my wallet" could mean either a misunderstanding of the scenario (where to click) or a technical failure. In the first case, a financial topic applies; in the second, a technical error.

For obvious cases, the benchmark retains one answer. For objectively ambiguous ones, we pre-define several acceptable options. In the current control sample, 190 comments have high confidence, and for six, seven additional acceptable options are recorded.
