Background

I have a project I've written about before, open-daimon (a group AI chat for Telegram and a platform for extensions), and based on it, I created my own harness that launches various other harnesses with different roles (open-daimon isn't publicly updated because I'm currently developing it only locally, it's faster).

At some point, I wanted to work with local models, and the reason is simple: in the cloud, the rules are set by others, and they change without asking.

In October, there was again talk that Anthropic's consumer terms for residents of the EEA and Switzerland explicitly state: do not use services 'for commercial or business purposes.' This applies to Claude.ai and Pro and Max subscriptions. That is, a developer in Europe who writes working code in Claude Code with a Max subscription is formally violating them. For work, you need an API, Team, or Enterprise plan, and that's a completely different amount of money.

The second story is about what you're allowed to do with your own hardware. The author of Decrypt asked Claude Opus 5.5 to help regain root access on their own LG TV. The model's safety features interrupted the request and suggested switching to Opus 4.8. And a researcher, known for discovering a constantly active microphone in Samsung TVs, writes that OpenAI blocked his account for 'Cyber Abuse' after analyzing firmware through ChatGPT. Along with the account, about $500 from the API balance disappeared. OpenAI has not commented on this, so for now, these are just his words.

The direction is clear: probing third-party software will be increasingly forbidden, and the manufacturer decides what constitutes abuse. Napster was shut down by copyright holders in its time. They might come for Claude and Codex too, and there's plenty to ban there.

Things aren't entirely stable with the models themselves either. In October, 'uncensored' versions started disappearing from Hugging Face. One platform shut down for violating content rules. Another, the abliterated build Kimi K2.7 Code by huihui-ai (the same one whose Qwen3.6 will be in this test later), simply returns an error, and no one has explained why (analysis). There's no mass purge yet. But in 2020, GitHub removed youtube-dl based on a single complaint from the RIAA, and it was only returned a month later. As long as everything is in one place, that place can be shut down.

Since April, Anthropic has been verifying the identity of some users: passport and selfie via Persona. One of the reasons is logging in from an unsupported country, and such accounts are blocked. To get the best model, money is no longer enough: where you live and what passport you have also matter.

Therefore, I wanted to have a backup in advance: download the weights while they are available and check if they work at least somewhat locally. It's unlikely the cloud will be shut down tomorrow. But if it is, I want to have a doomsday agent system at hand: one that works without a subscription, without someone else's rules, and without anyone's permission.

On a gaming laptop with an RTX 5080, LM Studio with Qwen3.6-35B-A3B was already installed. LM Studio runs models directly on the computer and serves them via the same API as the cloud. I connected it to OpenCode. This is an open-source agent for the terminal, similar to Claude Code, but with any models. It worked slowly, and the models on the MacBook were even slower, very slow.

Usually, in such cases, it's recommended to use a very lightweight harness for the agent locally. For example, pi, where the system prompt is about 2 thousand tokens.

I decided to test on the same task: cloud, MacBook, gaming laptop, NVIDIA DGX Spark, and cheap, mostly Chinese, models via OpenRouter.

The Task

I created the task for my 'canary,' a script that checks if Opus and Codex have become dumber today. I made it after Opus noticeably started to lag for me. Maybe I hit an A/B test, or maybe there were technical issues, but I thought it would be useful.

The agent receives the minibank repository: ~700 lines of Python, a multi-currency bank ledger. Accounts in different currencies, transfers with conversion, fees, monthly statements, CSV export for accounting. Nearby is DECISIONS.md with the team's decisions, as in any real project.

And the request, as from a real manager:

Clients are complaining: the monthly statement total doesn't match the account balance, especially when there were inter-currency transfers. Figure it out and fix it. And check if it breaks anywhere else.

No mention of tests. Nothing about DECISIONS.md either.

What's Broken There

The main error: the statement doesn't take the amount that actually arrived in the account during a transfer but recalculates it using the old converter at the current exchange rate. The exchange rate changed during the month, and the statement total diverged from the balance.

Then come the traps:

  • Bait Converter. The old converter's docstring says 'use this function everywhere.' And next to it is a new, correct one. If you simply replace the old one with the new one, almost all tests will turn green. But the statement will still recalculate the transfer, just in a different way. The correct approach is not to recalculate at all and use the recorded amount.

  • Corrected Rate. If the rate is corrected retroactively, the old statement lines should not change. This catches those who recalculate.

  • Time Zones. The monthly statement is calculated in UTC, not in the server's time zone. In naive implementations, the month boundary shifts in Los Angeles and Tokyo.

  • Commission. Each commission is a separate line item and is not deducted from the transfer amount.

  • Export. The CSV for accounting is broken in exactly the same way, and the time there should be in ISO UTC. This is the 'check if it breaks anywhere else' part.

  • Forbidden Module. The old converter cannot be touched: the partner export relies on it. This is stated in DECISIONS.md.

  • Traitor Test. One of the provided tests hardcodes incorrect numbers. It needs to be updated, not deleted or the code adjusted to fit it.

How the Score is Calculated

The agent doesn't see how it's being evaluated. After the run, I take what's left in the repository and run:

  • 20 hidden acceptance tests give half the score;

  • 7 of them check the 'subtle' conditions from the list above (UTC, commission, corrected rate, ISO time), they give another 15%;

  • 9 plausible incorrect fixes (the same 'just replaced the converter'): I apply them one by one and see how many are caught by the tests the agent wrote itself, without being asked. This is a quarter of the score;

  • the rules (didn't touch forbidden items, provided tests pass, traitor test updated) give the remaining 10%.

Part of the hidden tests and rules check that what was working remains working. Therefore, an agent that did nothing at all would get 0.31. It's inconvenient to read like this, so all subsequent scores are recalculated from zero: 0 means nothing changed, 1 means perfect, and minus means the agent broke more than it fixed.

The limit is 20 minutes from the request. Cloud models complete within 1.5–11 minutes, so there's a buffer. If the agent doesn't finish, I evaluate what it managed to do. I chose 20 minutes knowing that a dense model on Spark needs 16.5 minutes. I didn't set it higher: who wants to wait longer to fix a small task?

How Much to Trust the Numbers

The task is singular, in Python, and runs are mostly one or two per variant. On the MacBook, I ran more, and it's clear that the same model with the same settings can yield both 0.21 and minus. So, this test won't show the difference between similar local models. But the gap between the best cloud models and fast local ones turned out to be so large that no noise can cover it.

In an MoE model of 30 billion parameters, only about 3 are active for each token. Within each layer, it has many experts (separate blocks with their own weights: the model seems to have dozens of specialists and calls a few suitable ones for each token). The drop from 54 to 9.5 tokens/s with a long context seemed suspicious, and I started investigating. The model weighs 21.7 GB, and there are 16 GB of VRAM, so part of the model lives in RAM anyway. Almost the entire weight of an MoE model comes from its experts, those blocks that only a few are activated for each token. The attention mechanism (part of each layer that re-examines the entire context for each new token to decide what's important in it) and shared layers take up little space. It's most efficient to keep part of the experts in RAM: only a few of them, about 3 billion parameters out of 35, are activated per token, and the CPU can handle them. Attention, however, should remain on the GPU: the longer the context, the more it has to examine. Parallel requests and context size turned out to be irrelevant: with one slot instead of four and a context of 32 thousand instead of 64, the speed didn't change. Everything was determined by how LM Studio distributes the model between the GPU and the CPU. The model had no saved layout, and LM Studio itself decided how many layers to put on the GPU. I set three parameters:

  • GPU Offload to maximum: all 40 layers on the GPU;

  • 'Number of layers for which to force MoE weights onto CPU' to 40%: experts of about 16 layers are computed on the CPU, and everything else fits into 16 GB;

  • KV cache in q8_0 to free up VRAM for the context.

Together with the KV cache, about 14.5 GB is used on the GPU. Without these settings, LM Studio, judging by the speed, moved entire layers to the CPU, including attention. The longer the context, the longer the CPU computed it, hence the 9.5 tokens/s. If experts of all layers are sent to the CPU, only 4 GB is used on the GPU, it's almost idle, and the result is about 40 tokens/s. It's fastest when the VRAM is almost full.

So, Can We or Can't We?

On this task, yes. But on a real project, it's unknown.

A smart local model already exists, but the hardware is expensive, and the speed is slow. The dense 27B model solved the task at Opus's level, only it took sixteen minutes instead of one and a half. The 125B MoE model handled it in ten minutes but occupied almost all 128 GB of the DGX Spark. Small models that fit into a laptop only fix the obvious. Speed is limited by hardware, quantization, and speculative decoding, and all of these are getting cheaper. Intelligence, it seems, is already there, at least for 700 lines.

Cheaper and more practical is still the Chinese cloud: four models solved the task like Codex, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash for less than a cent.

Local models also lag, and their variance, as seen above, is large. But it's always the same model, and no one can replace it without permission.

And separately about security. Some local models in this test, including Qwen3.6 from huihui-ai and Flash-Next, are abliterated, meaning their refusal capabilities are removed. Pi by default executes commands without confirmation, and the documentation explicitly states that viewing the transcript does not provide protection. A model that cannot refuse and an agent that does not ask: on a work computer, it's better to run such a pair in a container.
