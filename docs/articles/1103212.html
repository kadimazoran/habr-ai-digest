I've been working in IT since 2004, as a team lead since 2008, and as a technical director since 2010. Between 2008 and 2010, my approach to evaluating newly hired IT specialists evolved as follows: I gave them tasks similar to real-world problems and observed how they solved them – how engaged, qualitatively, and quickly they worked. In 2014, I founded SymbioWay, a company specializing in IT recruitment and assessment, where this methodology was further developed.

Since we most often had to hire developers, I will use them as examples throughout this article, but all these principles are applicable to other IT specialists as well – system analysts, testers, DevOps engineers, and even project and product managers.

At that time, I firmly decided that in my company, recruitment and assessment would be handled exclusively by IT specialists, not recruiters, as is common in other recruitment agencies. I believe it's undeniable that the level of any specialist can best be assessed only by a specialist from the same field at a higher level. In particular, this is important to avoid the Dunning-Kruger effect.

Since 2008-2010, my team and I have been conducting interviews using the same scheme: first, we gave programmers live coding tasks, then we discussed and refined the results with the candidates in detail, and finally, we offered them small problems to solve and asked theoretical questions on topics important for our hiring process. The live coding methodology was adopted from Joel Spolsky's classic article "The Guerrilla Guide to Interviewing".

Can One Be an IT Specialist Without Knowing IT?

This is a conceptual question, the answer to which largely determines how much we can still use traditional assessment methods in interviews and when evaluating employees. I will try to answer it with a few vivid examples.

Imagine a surgeon. They perform operations excellently, knowing all the intricacies and nuances of their profession. Now, this surgeon is given tools like the Da Vinci robot, as well as a specialized ChatGPT for doctors (let's imagine such a thing exists). I should note that regular ChatGPT no longer provides medical advice (link to news), so the surgeon would need a specialized intelligent assistant, which they would use with the appropriate level of responsibility.

Question: Will the use of these new and advanced tools make the surgeon a better professional overall? Of course, it will! They will be able to perform their work more accurately and with higher quality, better assess risks, and faster find professional publications and receive advice. They will use these tools without abandoning their existing experience and knowledge base. These tools will allow them to offload some of the routine work and focus on the most complex and critical aspects of their job. They will also allow them to rest more, which will further improve the quality of their work.

But what happens if we give a specialized ChatGPT and the Da Vinci robot to several random people on the street who have no relation to medicine and ask them to perform surgery on patients? In all likelihood, most cases would end tragically.

  • Understanding of composition, the ability to create it, and the skill to see and direct light.

Again, if you take a random person off the street and ask them to take a quality photo, the probability of a poor result will be high. The probability of a quality result will depend not on the availability of modern technologies, but on the fact that almost every smartphone owner has, at some point in their lives, paid attention to photography skills, at least for their social media, and actually possesses these skills.

Let's return to IT. Many companies have been using AI technologies in their work for a relatively long time and have accumulated some experience. Among the problems faced by "novice" users of AI tools, they cite three:

  1. Low quality of results – AI does not perform its work with high quality, including from an architectural perspective.

  2. Low speed – long waiting times for instructions to be executed, as well as numerous cycles of corrections for tasks that should have been completed quickly and correctly the first time.

  3. Resource overspending – tokens are often used excessively for unnecessary work that does not lead to the desired results.

In the next section, we will discuss what skills a modern developer needs to avoid these problems. Here, I want to convey the key idea.

Large language models and the AI tools currently available on the market for developers are very powerful and versatile technologies with a vast reserve of knowledge and skills. They can literally do anything that can be done in text format, and much of what can be done in other formats. If we give models tasks without specifics, they will perform them at their own discretion – with the level of quality and consideration of nuances they deem appropriate. As we become more specific with our requirements and correctly handle the context, which is still limited for models, the accuracy and quality of our results will increase. But to correctly write these requirements and structure them, you need to understand how what you are creating works.

For example, if you are creating websites, you need to know what types of websites exist to ask the model to create the specific type you need. If you are building a large system, you need to understand how such systems are structured to correctly prompt the model, give it accurate instructions, describe skills, and organize multi-agent systems effectively. Of course, there are ready-made skills and instructions on the market for organizing models, but you need to be able to consciously choose them. The more knowledge and skills you have, the better and more precisely you can provide the intelligent model with what it needs, and the higher quality result you will get from it.

Only someone who is well-versed in the field and can perform the work themselves can give instructions to a model most accurately. This is because only they can correctly break down the task into parts, anticipate all nuances, make key decisions, correctly answer the model's questions during planning, and, crucially, verify the final result.

Regarding programmers, it's also important to understand that whoever writes any program code is responsible for it, regardless of whether they write it themselves or with the help of a large language model. If this code leads to financial damage for the company, for example, the legal responsibility will lie with its creator. Obviously, it is dangerous to entrust such responsibility to someone who does not understand IT. The same applies to other professions: DevOps engineers, architects, analysts, and managers.

From this, a simple conclusion follows: you cannot be an IT specialist without knowing IT. This conclusion is also supported by a market trend where many companies are now trying to reduce junior IT specialists and hire senior-level specialists to work with AI tools.

What IT Specialists Need to Know and Be Able to Do Now

Just 5 years ago, it was sufficient for a programmer to complete a reasonably good course in one programming language to get a job. Now, this is clearly not enough, as program code has ceased to be a scarce resource.

Firstly, programmers now need to broaden their horizons and "look around" – study all technologies adjacent to their primary specialization. For example, a backend Java developer should now ideally learn a couple more backend programming languages, definitely study frontend, DevOps tools, and testing. It wouldn't hurt to also study mobile application development. We are seeing an active "broadening" of professions in several companies.

Secondly, developers are required to deepen their knowledge and skills. As I wrote above, everyone is trying to hire more experienced specialists. This means you need to know architecture, the principles of high-load and distributed systems, algorithms, networks, and everything necessary to deeply understand how modern applications work, comprehend their mechanisms and technologies, be able to choose technologies, design architecture, make complex technical decisions, and solve non-trivial problems.

Thirdly, of course, developers need to become engineers who manage AI tools. To improve the quality of these tools' work, it's important to be able to work with specifications and conserve context, including by dividing applications into components, modules, or services. To increase work speed, one needs to master methods for launching sub-agents and managing multi-agent systems. Developers also need to be economical with AI tool resources by clearly defining roles, boundaries, and work stages for agents.

Finally, modern developers need to, to some extent, become product managers. They need to be closer to clients – communicate with them, be able to gather and formalize requirements, and "feel" the clients. They need to be closer to the market – be able to analyze it and adapt to it. And they need to remember that many companies will now develop faster, so it's important to think about speed and the inevitability of tougher market competition for products. Currently, this skill set is not the top priority, but it is a highly probable prospect for the coming years.

How to Assess IT Specialists

Any phenomena in life can be viewed negatively and fought against, or they can be seen positively and adapted to. Both strategies are valid. In the context of assessing IT specialists, we chose the latter, as we considered it pointless to fight against a world changing so rapidly and drastically. We made this decision back in early 2025, when AI technologies were not yet as good as they are now, but it was already clear that they were close to reaching an acceptable quality level.

Above, I discussed the problems the market faces in assessing IT specialists. One of them is the dishonesty of IT specialists during interviews. Before the advent of AI technologies, there was another problem: it was quite difficult to fully assess an IT specialist within a one-hour interview, and this selection format sometimes led to errors. We always considered trial days to be the ideal format, during which a company could give a specialist the opportunity to work on their project, solve several tasks, and observe the quality and speed of their work. However, this format was always too expensive in all respects.

Now, with the advent of AI technologies, we all have the opportunity to solve both these problems simultaneously: observe how a specialist works on a real project and whether they can complete assigned tasks within a short time. Here, we can assess how they use AI tools, how well they can understand existing code, and how knowledgeable they are in their field. In our work, we use special short, but complex and multi-level tasks for such assessments. It's impossible to complete them without knowledge in the relevant development area. It's also impossible to complete them in a short interview time without knowing and being skilled in using AI tools.

To conduct assessments in this format, we always ask if the person currently has access to a development environment with AI agents, if they are proficient with them, and if they are willing to complete the task directly in such an environment. Many candidates are still surprised by this approach but gladly agree. With this method, it's theoretically possible to peek at correct answers, but it's obviously pointless because we immediately provide the opportunity to use AI tools.

In cases where candidates do not have such capabilities, we use classic methods – we ask them to place a phone with a camera behind them at an angle. Alternatively, when answering certain questions, we politely ask them to close their eyes and answer as if we suspect they are cheating. We also thoroughly investigate all signs of cheating and train our experts to spot them. These include, in particular, delays in responses, strange reasoning, and "wandering" eyes.

One of the best formats remains in-person interviews, which we also conduct, including in different regions and countries, by arranging for our expert to travel to the company's office. In this format, we typically provide the candidate with a computer or allow them to use their own so they can complete a large task on a real project. The interviewer then observes the work process, which is close to reality, assesses its effectiveness, the candidate's engagement, and asks additional questions to confirm their conclusions and assessments.
