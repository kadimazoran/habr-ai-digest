Why distillation is needed

Suppose you've trained a monstrously expensive model that reasons well, writes code, and solves math problems, but each query to it costs enormous computational resources. For an in-house researcher, it's a working tool, and it's worth it if trillion-dollar markets are at stake. For a product with 100 million users, scaling access to such models is profitable, so instead of running it for every query, it's made into a teacher: it generates high-quality answers, solutions, reasoning trajectories, synthetic data, sometimes probability distributions themselves, and a smaller student is trained on this behavior, which doesn't need to reproduce the teacher entirely, but only transfer the necessary capabilities. This is one form of knowledge distillation, and the practice is so common that OpenAI directly provides developers with an official Model Distillation workflow: take answers from a stronger model, like GPT-4o or o1-preview, and train a cheaper model on them. OpenAI's own wording is quite precise: take an advanced model and achieve comparable quality on a specific task at a significantly lower cost. From this follows a rather simple thing: the best research model and the best production model are optimized for different functions, because the research model maximizes capabilities, while the production model has to simultaneously balance intelligence, latency, GPU cost, throughput, reliability, and safety.

Public examples of this scheme have accumulated over the past two years. Apple described an internal Mixture-of-Experts teacher used to train its small on-device foundation model. Google trained EmbeddingGemma using a more powerful Gemini teacher. Meta used the logits of Llama 3.1 8B and 70B to create small Llama 3.2 1B and 3B. Microsoft explicitly stated that early Phi models were largely distilled from GPT-4 capabilities. DeepSeek, after training R1, released a line of distilled models from 1.5B to 70B. The "strong teacher > cheap student" scheme is real and well-documented.

What does not follow from this

It might seem to imply a wrong conclusion: that any public model is a stripped-down version of a secret supermodel. Sometimes the teacher is public; Meta used public Llama 3.1, DeepSeek released both the large R1 and the models distilled from it, and the student sometimes outperforms its teacher on a specific benchmark or narrow task due to specialization and post-training. So, it's not entirely accurate to claim that all open source is a deliberately degraded distillation of secret models. However, a weaker statement is fully supported: the public catalog of models almost certainly does not show the full frontier of a specific lab's capabilities.

"An internal model at OpenAI"

In 2026, a very unusual phrasing began to appear regularly in mathematical papers. On March 31, a group of mathematicians published a paper with solutions to three Erdős problems, where the abstract directly stated: "in each case, the proof is due entirely to an internal model at OpenAI." This is not an isolated case: in February, an internal OpenAI model constructed a new solution to the Erdős, Nesetril, and Rödl problem, in April another paper was published with five proofs obtained in the same way, and then an internal OpenAI model found a counterexample to Erdős's conjecture on unit distances, open for about eighty years, and the proof was verified by external mathematicians. A companion paper indicated that the initial mathematical result was generated in a single pass by an internal model, after which humans reworked the presentation through Codex. W. T. Gowers wrote that if such a proof had been submitted by a human, he would have unhesitatingly recommended it for publication in Annals of Mathematics. Mehtaab Sawhney formulated it precisely on Twitter: "the solution was found by an internal model at OpenAI," and Kevin Weil, then one of the leaders at OpenAI, reposted it with the comment that AI is solving more and more open problems and the proofs are becoming more elegant as models improve. This means that the existence of models that the company uses internally long before public release is no longer speculation; it is written in mathematical papers.

At the same time, a few months later, Noam Brown from OpenAI told this story differently than it is usually retold: the company simply trained a new internal model and decided to see what it could do. The counterexample was found with a relatively small inference budget, but after publication, people discovered that approximately the same path can be extracted from the already public GPT-5.5 if the scaffolding is built correctly. This means that there may not be a strict boundary between "the internal model can do this" and "the public model cannot do this": the capability is already within the public weights but remains latent until the correct prompt, search strategy, verifier, or test-time compute is found. Hence, "how intelligent a model is" ceases to be a property of the weights alone and becomes a property of the combination of model + scaffolding + tools + inference budget + verifier + search strategy + post-training, and the usual chat interface turns out to be a rather poor instrument for measuring the real frontier of capabilities.

What about Astra then

With Astra, it turned out to be an almost natural experiment. Before its public release, OpenAI was already using very powerful internal models in research, and on September 3, 2026, they released GPT-6 Astra, calling it the most capable broadly deployed model: 98% on FrontierMath Tier 4 and, according to the company, participation in solving long-standing mathematical problems. It is quite possible that what was called simply an internal model in a paper a few months ago belongs to the same technological line that later became Astra. But this is my conclusion based on indirect evidence: OpenAI has not disclosed the origin of these checkpoints in enough detail to honestly draw a line from a specific internal mathematical model to Astra. Moreover, it does not follow that a model a generation stronger than Astra already exists somewhere inside. This is quite possible, but there is no evidence for it yet.

The flip side of distillation or the DeepSeek phenomenon

You spend billions on computation, researchers, RL, synthetic data, and eval infrastructure, and a competitor only needs to extract a few million high-quality answers from your model via API to reproduce a significant part of the result. They don't need your weights and don't necessarily need to understand your architecture; they use your model as a teacher, generate millions of tasks, collect answers, filter the best reasoning trajectories, train their model, then use your model as a judge, and repeat the cycle. The economics become skewed: you pay for creating intelligence, the competitor pays for API tokens to transfer this intelligence.

As early as 2025, OpenAI stated that they had detected signs of their models being used for distillation. In February 2026, Anthropic published much more concrete data: according to them, DeepSeek, Moonshot, and MiniMax were conducting large-scale distillation campaigns against Claude, with over 24,000 fraudulent accounts and over 16 million exchanges, and they were extracting not random answers, but expensive clusters of capabilities: coding, reasoning, tool use, agents, grading, computer use. On Twitter, Anthropic wrote almost the same words: "16 million exchanges with Claude, extracting its capabilities to train and improve their own models." The term knowledge extraction is more accurate here than model stealing, because no one is obliged to copy weights; it's enough to copy the useful function.

This leads to an uncomfortable situation: frontier labs themselves constantly use distillation because it's the best known way to make expensive intelligence cheap, and at the same time, they try to prevent themselves from being distilled. On a technical level, this is not hypocrisy; the difference lies in who owns the teacher and whether they have the right to use its answers to train a competitor. But from the perspective of the technology itself, the same thing is happening: a strong model produces supervision, on which another model becomes stronger. Therefore, the API has ceased to be just an interface to a product and has become a channel for capability leakage, and the more you give away (raw logits, reasoning paths, judge evaluations, millions of unrestricted requests), the cheaper it is for a competitor to build a student. API limitations, rate limits, hidden chain-of-thought, monitoring of coordinated accounts, and prohibitions on competitive model training in licenses gain a quite understandable economic reason. Clément Delangue, CEO of Hugging Face, wrote this year that he wouldn't be surprised by a scenario where frontier labs start restricting APIs more and direct scarce computational resources primarily to their own products.

What follows from this

We are used to reading names like GPT, Claude, Gemini, Llama as separate, finished entities: a company trained GPT-6, therefore GPT-6 is its intelligence. This corresponds to reality less and less, because the laboratory looks more like a continuous intelligence pipeline: huge training runs > checkpoints > reinforcement learning > synthetic data > teachers > critics > verifiers > distillation > tool scaffolding > test-time compute > production deployment, and the model name that the user sees is one point on this pipeline, chosen for reasons of cost and risk.

Therefore, the phrasing of the question "what is the smartest model today" is not very successful. Meaningful questions are different: what is the maximum capability that already exists within the laboratory, what part of it is in the research checkpoints, what part is already hidden in the public weights and waiting for normal scaffolding, what part has been cheaply distilled into production. And the main question is: how far has the real frontier moved ahead of what a person sees when opening a new chat — the leaderboard of public models is not an indicator of AI capabilities.
