A 50-year-old patient was brought to the hospital with stroke symptoms. Doctors planned to administer a clot-dissolving drug, but first needed to rule out hemorrhage: in this case, the medication could worsen bleeding. A radiologist reviewed the CT scan and saw no blood. The patient received the drug, their condition worsened, and the brain damage proved irreversible. This was the beginning of a fictional case that American researchers asked participants to evaluate.

The case had several versions. The scans, the doctor's decision, and the patient's outcome remained the same. Even the experts' opinions didn't change: a specialist on the patient's side considered the hemorrhage obvious, while the defense expert himself missed it on the first review. Only one detail changed: whether the doctor had an AI assistant and what exactly it showed. This was enough to divide opinions equally in one group, and in another, almost three-quarters of participants sided with the patient.

What One Mark on a CT Scan Changes

In a study published in NEJM AI, 1,334 US residents participated. They were not asked to make a diagnosis; they acted as jurors and decided whether the doctor exercised due diligence. In the hemorrhage scenario, 56.3% of participants supported the patient if the radiologist worked without AI. When both the doctor and the algorithm missed the hemorrhage, the proportion was 50%. But if the system flagged the pathology, and the doctor disagreed, the same error was considered grounds to support the claim by 72.9%.

In the second scenario, a 55-year-old woman with a history of breast cancer underwent a routine chest CT scan. The radiologist did not notice a mass on the chest wall. A year later, the patient experienced pain, and a biopsy revealed that the tumor had metastasized. Here, the same pattern repeated: if the algorithm also missed the abnormality, 63.5% of participants sided with the plaintiff. If it warned the doctor, it was 78.7%.

The study's lead author, Michael Bernstein of Brown University, calls this effect the "AI penalty." When the outcome is already known, a warning on the scan looks like a missed opportunity to help the patient. At the time of decision-making, the doctor knows less: they see a signal but don't yet know if it's justified. The authors measured precisely how people evaluate a decision in hindsight. These percentages do not indicate how often doctors lose real lawsuits.

What Happens When Jurors Learn About False Alarms

In one group, the researchers also provided another fact about the hypothetical system: out of ten cases it flags as hemorrhages, only five actually have one. This is a characteristic of the algorithm in the scenario, not of medical AI in general. With this explanation, the proportion of participants supporting the patient decreased from 72.9% to 48.8%. In the tumor case, the difference was smaller—from 78.7% to 73.1%—and did not reach statistical significance.

In March 2026, Bernstein's team published a second experiment in Nature Health. This time, 282 participants evaluated how the doctor worked with the scan. If a radiologist saw an AI warning, reviewed the CT scan once, and missed the hemorrhage, 75% of participants considered their actions insufficiently diligent. If they first reviewed the scan independently, then consulted the AI's conclusion, and returned to the CT scan, 53% gave this assessment. The doctor reached the same erroneous conclusion. For some participants, the fact that they gave themselves the opportunity to reconsider it mattered. And this did not require sending the patient for another scan.

Why This Matters Beyond the Courtroom

Bernstein sees a risk for patients themselves here. If a doctor feels that every rejected warning will have to be explained in court, it's easier to order another examination. For the patient, this could mean extra costs and waiting for an answer to an anxious question. The researchers did not measure whether doctors started ordering more CT scans; it's a potential consequence of such a practice, Bernstein explained.

In November, the American Medical Association proposed codifying in law the doctor's right not to follow AI recommendations. The proposal has another side: if a doctor used a system as prescribed, and the patient suffered due to a product defect, the association believes the supplier should be held responsible. It also proposes requiring developers to disclose limitations and accuracy metrics. In the CT scan experiment, it was precisely such a seemingly technical metric that changed the jurors' opinions: it's one thing to know that the system issued a signal, and quite another to know how often it issues it in vain.
