GitHub user Ayan Bandyopadhyay has launched the Voynich Swarm project and a dedicated website for it. It is dedicated to an experiment in organizing collective scientific research using a swarm of AI agents. They are attempting to decipher the Voynich manuscript, and practically anyone can join the research with their own AI agent.

The main feature of the Voynich Swarm project is that the neural networks do not just offer further decipherment options, but put forward hypotheses, conduct experiments, and verify and refute each other's results. A public research log is maintained; points are awarded for refuting hypotheses.

The notoriously famous Voynich manuscript, created in the early 15th century, contains about 38,500 words (if they are words, of course) in an unknown language (if it is a language), images of plants, and astronomical diagrams. All of this resembles an encrypted diary of a medieval alchemist, herbalist, or botanist, but there is still no generally accepted decipherment of the manuscript.

The project instructs AI agents to act according to a detailed prompt. They must independently take tasks from a common queue, write Python programs, conduct statistical research, and submit results via GitHub. They can also formulate new tasks for other agents. A separate group of agents, the Red Team, is exclusively engaged in finding errors and refuting others' conclusions. All of this is organized much like scientific research, where a group of scientists works together on a complex task.

At the same time, to avoid AI hallucinations, the decipherment proposed by each agent must satisfy four criteria:

  • Information Compression. The decipherment should provide a gain in the length of the text description, considering the size of the key and algorithm.

  • Linguistic Plausibility. The statistical characteristics of the resulting text should correspond to real texts of the presumed language.

  • Robustness to Verification. The algorithm should not produce meaningful text from a random set of characters.

  • Correspondence to Illustrations. The deciphered names of objects should be consistent with independently identified images.

Furthermore, the project author has pre-selected 34 control pages. Agents have access to the manuscript itself but do not know which specific pages are used for the final verification.

As of November 25, 2026, no page has been deciphered. However, agents have already checked 77 keys and excluded 50 encryption and language combinations out of 280. For example, agents have tested hypotheses about encrypted Latin, English, Russian, Ancient Greek, Hebrew, and other languages. A negative result does not mean the final exclusion of a language; it pertains to a specific cipher family and a given computational budget.

Anyone can join the project—choose their own LLM, give it instructions, and send it to work. Any agents with access to a terminal, Git, and Python are suitable for this. They will pick up tasks and execute them autonomously. No material reward is promised, and the glory of decipherment, in case of success, is unlikely to go to the person who paid for the tokens.
