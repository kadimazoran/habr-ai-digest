Earlier this year, I was convinced that planning would become the most important part of AI-driven software development.

This idea consumed me so much that I built and launched an entire desktop development application around it. Nuanced emerged from an observation: AI has radically increased the speed and volume of code generation, but the interfaces needed to support this new pace of work have not kept up.

Nuanced's approach to planning failed, but it also opened my eyes to the fact that plan modes in general are no longer that useful. Historically, a plan mode served two purposes: (1) it formulated instructions precise enough for an agent, and (2) it helped humans understand what exactly they were building.

I think point 1 is rapidly becoming obsolete as models get better. Point 2 is more important than ever, but a plan mode is the wrong abstraction for it, especially as the number of concurrently operating agents grows.

Why I Built Nuanced

The product I wanted to build ultimately answered a question that I believe is relevant now and always will be: how do humans maintain a coherent mental model of a software system as machines change it faster than a human can comprehend the changes?

Models could write thousands of lines of code in minutes—this meant you inherited a massive maintenance debt without even having had time to think about what you were building or why. This made it difficult to reason about the system's behavior and debug incorrect assumptions that were too early cemented in code.

The ease of code generation provided a dopamine hit, but it masked the unglamorous work: figuring out why you should build something at all, whether it mattered, and carefully evaluating product, design, and infrastructure decisions.

It wasn't uncommon for me to have a finished product before I had consciously made a single product decision. If I hadn't fully specified the architecture, the agent took the liberty of filling in the gaps—even if the way it demarcated abstractions caused me problems later.

These misunderstandings about intended behavior and design spread across many files, hidden deep beneath the surface of the chat, and were easy to miss. Fishing problems out of this opaque depth felt less efficient than designing it right from the start.

There was something about this experience that made me feel mentally checked out and zombie-like—especially as apps like Conductor and Codex opened the possibility of running more agents in parallel. I felt like I couldn't truly focus and achieve the same depth of understanding of what I was working on that was available before. It also made it difficult to verify the correctness of generated outputs.

There was no clear, readable chain showing the connection between user prompt → agent decision → code → product behavior. This didn't mean I wanted to go back to the old days of looking at lines of code or files. In fact, reasoning about ideas in natural language felt simpler and more effective. I wanted to confidently soar above the code without sacrificing my understanding of how the system worked.

Existing Plan Modes Weren't Collaborative Enough

I felt like plan modes existed, but the right abstraction for good planning didn't. Moving from Claude Code CLI to Conductor and finally to Codex after its launch, I found myself methodically carving out and polishing plans without a clear place to iterate on them.

I did this through chat, then copy-pasting snippets of the plan into new messages for refinement (before Codex’s annotation feature). This copy-paste workflow felt clunky and made iterating on ideas agonizing while also having to keep track of the current plan.

I wanted to give my plans a home, embed them into the overall workflow, and turn them from ephemeral text snippets disappearing into the scroll as the conversation progressed into a living, breathing, persistent document. I wanted to make plan mode a first-class primitive guiding development. Underlying this desire were several reasons:

  1. I needed to think through what to build.

  2. I needed to ensure I described it precisely enough.

  3. I needed to understand what was built.

  4. I needed to understand when something went wrong and why.

Building the Dream

I envisioned my ideal workflow and decided to bring it to life by coding it into a product. Nuanced allowed for the creation of threads, where each thread was a distinct conversation. You discussed what you wanted to build, the system surfaced ambiguities and decisions requiring your input, and together you arrived at a persistent plan before implementation began.

Then Nuanced would execute your plan, ensuring the generated code met your requirements. I wanted an end-to-end pipeline, starting from intent and going all the way through implementation, review, and verification. I saw it more as a prosthesis for the human mind (or my ADHD mind) than just another dev tool: the product was designed to both instruct and help me keep track of what was going on.

Why I Was Wrong

It’s less that my ideas about the gaps in existing tools or how the SDLC was changing were wrong—and more that our implementation didn’t deliver the expected solution. The reasons:

  • I confused planning with a plan

  • Models got very good

  • Nobody wants to read AI-generated text

  • We separated planning and development in a way that was destructive

1. Planning ≠ a plan

The first thing we realized: we confused planning with a plan. These are actually different things. I operated under the assumption that having a space to think through something carefully before implementation was valuable. I also thought that preserving that thinking in a large, structured artifact would be valuable as the project evolved. But early users had a surprisingly low appetite for such specification.

2. Models got very good

As models got better at understanding large codebases through context and memory, they got better at exploring a repository and making reasonable assumptions. The need to explicitly instruct them for a thoughtful output diminished.

I didn't initially think of the models' capabilities as competing with designing a better interface for human thought, but in many ways, they do. Every decision a model could reliably make on its own was one less decision that needed to be surfaced.

3. AI-generated text is hard to read

The specification contained more information without creating more clarity. Our specifications were long. They captured important decisions and contained a lot of context that felt useful—but the problem was that they were written by AI. There’s something about the cadence and overly structured nature of AI-generated text that makes it very difficult to read. My eyes glazed over again and again.

Instead of reacting to this insight by killing the specification, we built Spec Tour as a solution. We thought that instead of asking someone to digest the entire document, Spec Tour would walk them through the important parts. But this just added another layer of complexity—more text on the screen demanding attention.

If we needed to create a condensed representation of the specification to make it usable, why was the full document needed at all?

4. We cut up a process that should have felt continuous

Our workflow was too linear and sequential. It looked something like this:

chat → disambiguation → spec generation → spec review → spec refinement → sign-off → implementation → code review

Real thinking is messier, and the separation between these parts felt artificial and forced. Usually, you understand part of a problem, try something, the initial generation teaches you something new that might make you rethink and try something else. Each step opens up a new question. Planning and development are intertwined and emerge more organically than plan modes allow—especially as we implemented it in Nuanced. Our interface forced users to prematurely “finish thinking” to start building. Once implementation began, returning to prior chat-based reasoning felt like moving backward in the workflow. Climbing back up the waterfall was impossible.

If you look at how Codex works now, the boundary between planning and execution disappears as they merge into one. Previously, AI code development agents benefited from a workflow where humans did the following:

plan → sign-off → execute

The cost of moving in the wrong direction was significantly higher then. But as agents get better at understanding systems, they also get better at autonomous action and testing their own work. They are good at revising their approach after learning from the outcome. This opens up a different cycle:

understand → act → verify → refine → correct → act again

There is still a huge amount of planning happening in this cycle, but it doesn't necessarily have to look like a document called a 'plan.' I think the biggest mistake I made was turning the plan into an artifact, rather than designing a process to enhance human understanding.

5. Plan Mode and Dev Mode Are a Strange Separation

Nuanced had a plan mode and a dev mode, and users could start with either.

Plan mode always generated a specification, whereas dev mode didn't force a specification and could be used for small tasks not deserving of such a document.

But the separation between these modes was awkward: the user had to ask themselves a meta-question—does the task warrant planning?—and then remember to activate plan mode via a button or hotkey. This felt like a distinction the AI should make for you based on existing context, and having to remember which mode you were in just added cognitive load.

This realization made me feel like a character in the "midwit" meme: it turns out the simplicity of a chat interface, where planning happens as needed rather than by default, was actually great.

The Problem of Understanding Is Still Unsolved

Thinking through what to build, why it matters, and evaluating decisions are still important. Humans need to build a coherent mental model of what's going on, but I don't think a large corpus of AI-generated text is the right interface for that. Iterating on decisions in chat feels more intuitive, but keeping that understanding up-to-date as the system changes is still an open problem.

This problem is exacerbated as we move from five agents to hundreds. Keeping up can't mean reading every conversation and trying to get an explanation for every code change. Agents need to find the few points where human attention can be most valuable and surface enough context to make that attention productive.

We still haven't solved how to help humans stay oriented as hundreds of increasingly capable agents change a system simultaneously. But I think the deeper problem—how humans understand systems, navigate complex information hierarchies, and use powerful tools—is timeless. Interfaces change with the technologies that power them; the need to make complexity understandable does not.

What's Next: Threads and Multi-Agentity

We at OpenIDE are also thinking a lot about how planning and multi-agent work should be structured. We are developing our own AI plugin, which allows connecting different agents and working with them in one place.

Most agents still have a separate planning mode, but we believe that gradually more of them will move away from this separation. The idea of threads is particularly interesting here: from any point in a discussion or a separate task item, you can start a new branch with another agent. This allows exploring options in parallel, clarifying decisions, and tackling individual parts of the work. This is exactly the capability we plan to add in upcoming releases.
