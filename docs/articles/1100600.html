Want to choose a product? AI will help. Want to pass an interview? AI will conduct it instead of potential management. Want to know about a particular study? AI will summarize and conclude it. This two-letter acronym has permeated almost every aspect of human life, whether we like it or not. Yet, the question of trust in AI and its ilk remains far from resolved. Researchers from the University of Munich (Germany) conducted an extensive study, establishing that trust in AI continues to be very low, even when its behavior is identical to human behavior. How did AI attempt to imitate human creation in the study, what gave it away, and why do people still distrust an entity that certainly, in no way, no-no, will not destroy our species? The Basis of the Study

From large language models that track conversational context to autonomous robots navigating dynamic environments, artificial systems increasingly exhibit behavior that is sensitive to their surroundings, much like humans. But do human observers perceive this machine behavior the same way they perceive human behavior? Specifically, do they conclude that artificial intelligence is aware of the context—just as a human is?

Image #1

In this study, researchers describe two different potential approaches (1A). First, people rely on a single general principle: the more adequately an agent responds to context, the more conscious it is, regardless of whether the agent is human or artificial. Researchers call this functionalist attribution. The alternative is that people reserve consciousness for certain types of beings. Even when an artificial system behaves as reactively as a human, it is still considered to have less consciousness. This is called essentialist attribution. Another question is whether this distinction is unique to consciousness or if it also extends to closely related constructs such as awareness.

Understanding this inferential process is crucial, as attributing mental states (especially consciousness) shapes our social and moral landscape. This includes judgments about a subject's moral significance, legal responsibility, and trust. Consequently, such questions should be approached with their ethical implications in mind. Moreover, while some philosophers consider the risk of anthropomorphism quite real, empirical studies show a much more restrained approach: a third of respondents completely deny AI consciousness, and the remaining two-thirds attribute consciousness to it only to a limited extent and with great uncertainty. Furthermore, most people do not believe that artificial intelligence can suffer in the same way as humans.

Current psychological research on the perception of mental states primarily focuses on the qualities people attribute to machines. A consistent finding is the multidimensionality of this process: people are more willing to attribute "agency" (the ability to plan and act) to robots but deny them "experiential capacity" (the ability to feel and sense). These dimensions have different consequences: attributing "agency" (or "intelligence") is associated with higher user trust in AI, while attributing "experiential capacity"—on the contrary—reduces the willingness to heed the machine's advice. Perceiving a machine as a being capable of feeling or sensing seems to make people less willing to rely on its judgments. This is likely because such an "agent," endowed with its own internal states, is perceived as a subject with independent interests, rather than an "empty" tool. This asymmetry between "experiential capacity" and "agency" is also observed in human-robot interaction literature—particularly in the context of the "uncanny valley" phenomenon: machines begin to evoke unease precisely when observers attribute experiential capacity to them, rather than agency.

However, much less is known about how such attributions arise and on what behavioral manifestations they are formed. Research in the attribution of mental states has evolved from general observations (e.g., the fact that people tend to imbue simple moving geometric shapes with "mind") to identifying specific behavioral cues that systematically prompt us to attribute certain mental states to subjects. Researchers ask: can a similar approach be applied to analyze how people attribute consciousness to artificial intelligence? What observable behaviors are perceived by us as evidence of consciousness, and how do these manifestations transform into judgments about the subject's consciousness or awareness? Since consciousness is a special phenomenon, inextricably linked to subjective experience, researchers compared the attribution of consciousness with the attribution of a closely related concept—"awareness." Awareness is generally understood as the ability to selectively monitor and respond to significant aspects of a situation. It is believed that this requires access to information at the agent's level, rather than the presence of subjective experience. Based on this, the mechanisms for attributing awareness may differ from those involved in attributing consciousness.

To investigate this, researchers compared how people interpret the same behavioral manifestations when attributing consciousness and awareness, in some cases to humans and in others to artificial agents. For this purpose, five preregistered experiments were conducted, combined into two studies (1B). Researchers varied the degree of agent reactivity to the environment, i.e., how adequately the subject responds to significant elements of the situation. Reactivity in this sense is a characteristic of observable behavior, while awareness and consciousness are mental states that observers may (or may not) attribute to the subject based on this behavior. The first study described situations where agents exhibited low, medium, or high reactivity to spatial environmental cues (e.g., responding to noise or navigating obstacles). The second study examined low or high reactivity to spatial, social, or temporal cues.

Researchers hypothesized that the attribution of consciousness would follow essentialist constraints. Although an increase in reactivity might lead to both humans and artificial agents being more frequently attributed consciousness, artificial agents would still be perceived as less conscious compared to humans, resulting in systematically lower consciousness attribution scores (1A). It was further hypothesized that awareness would be assessed functionally: if the inference of awareness is made based on observable reactivity, then an increase in reactivity should lead to a proportional increase in attributed awareness for both humans and artificial agents.

Description and Results of the Studies

Study 1

Studies 1A and 1B were conducted with different groups to separately test whether consciousness and awareness are attributed to subjects. In Study 1C, both concepts were presented side-by-side to identify contrast effects. As a control in Study 1D, participants were asked questions about awareness and a non-mental construct (damage/cost of damage). This allowed researchers to ensure that the contrast effects observed in Study 1C were indeed contrast effects and not a result of general response patterns arising from answering multiple questions simultaneously.

For each of the four experiments, researchers recruited 200 participants from the UK via the Prolific platform. The experiments themselves were conducted on the Qualtrics platform. Data collection took place from May to November 2025.

After conducting preregistered attention and language comprehension checks, researchers analyzed data from 719 participants across the four studies. Study 1A, which tested only consciousness, included 174 individuals; the average age was 44, with 83 men (47%) and 86 women (49%). In this study, 26 participants failed attention or language comprehension checks. Study 1B, focusing solely on awareness, included 179 individuals; the average age was 39, including 62 men (35%) and 117 women (65%). Here, 21 participants failed preregistered attention or language comprehension checks. Study 1C, examining both consciousness and awareness, involved 186 individuals; the average age was 40, with 65 men (35%) and 117 women (63%). In this case, 14 participants failed attention or language comprehension checks. Study 1D, where awareness responses were compared with a "non-mental" anchor, included 180 individuals; the average age was 46, including 85 men (47%) and 93 women (52%). Here, 20 participants failed attention or language comprehension checks. The remaining participants identified as non-binary or did not specify their gender.

To investigate how participants attribute consciousness and awareness to humans and artificial intelligence (AI), researchers used a vignette-based method within a mixed-design experiment. Across four online studies, researchers systematically varied two independent variables and three background scenarios. The independent variables were: subject type (human or machine)—a between-subjects factor, and the subject's observed reaction to the surrounding environment (low, medium, or high degree)—a within-subjects factor (1B).

Participants were randomly assigned to one of two groups (between-subjects assignment), differing in the type of protagonist. Within each group, participants were presented with all three levels of reactivity. Each level used a separate backstory to minimize carryover effects. The chosen backstories were scenarios related to cleaning, manufacturing, and food service. The combination of backstory and reactivity level, as well as their presentation order, was randomized for each participant (Table #1).

Attribution of Consciousness Depends on Agent Type, While Attribution of Awareness Does Not

When consciousness and awareness were assessed separately, it was found that these two concepts elicited significantly different attribution patterns. Consciousness ratings increased with increasing reactivity, but they were significantly higher for humans than for artificial agents at any level of reactivity. Even highly reactive artificial agents were rated as less conscious than minimally reactive humans. This indicates a distinct essentialist constraint in consciousness attribution (graphs below). In contrast, awareness ratings increased with reactivity equally for both humans and artificial agents: no significant differences were found between them. This pattern aligns with a functionalist approach to attribution: awareness is inferred from observable behavioral reactivity, irrespective of the agent's nature.

Image #2

Specifically, in Study 1A, human agent consciousness ratings increased with each increment in reactivity. Meanwhile, ratings for artificial agents changed to a lesser extent: only the overall difference between high and low reactivity levels was statistically significant. Notably, human agents were attributed a higher level of consciousness than artificial agents at any degree of reactivity, and this gap widened as reactivity increased.

In Study 1B, where awareness was assessed separately, a different pattern emerged: ratings increased with each subsequent reactivity level—for both humans and artificial agents. For humans, ratings at medium reactivity were 0.79 points higher than at low reactivity, and at high reactivity, they were 2.00 points higher than at medium. Artificial agents showed a similar, though slightly less pronounced, trend. Direct comparison between the two agent types revealed no statistically significant differences at low or medium reactivity. Only at high reactivity did humans receive slightly higher awareness scores than artificial agents.

Functionalist and Essentialist Patterns Replicate with Joint Assessment

Since Studies 1A and 1B involved different participants rating consciousness and awareness, researchers in Study 1C decided to see what would happen if the same participants rated both concepts simultaneously. This experiment allowed them to test whether the previously identified difference would persist under direct joint assessment. Both patterns replicated identically (3A, 3B).

Image #3

Consciousness attribution demonstrated an essentialist-like pattern. For human agents, consciousness ratings increased with their reactivity, whereas for artificial agents, only a slight increase was observed, plateauing from medium to high reactivity. Humans were attributed higher consciousness than artificial agents at any reactivity level, with the gap widening as reactivity increased.

The level of attributed awareness again increased with each step of reactivity—for both humans and artificial agents, confirming the functionalist pattern.

Although the functionalist model of awareness attribution replicated under joint assessment, Study 1C revealed that at high reactivity, humans were perceived as significantly more aware than artificial agents (3B). At low and medium reactivity, artificial agents were rated as having the same level of awareness as humans. However, at high reactivity, humans were attributed a substantially higher level of awareness than artificial agents.

To test whether this difference at high reactivity was due to participants being asked to consider awareness alongside consciousness, researchers conducted Study 1D as a control: here, awareness was compared with a non-mental construct (i.e., damage assessment described in the situation). If the mere act of answering two questions was sufficient to create such a difference, it would appear here; if the difference was due to the joint consideration of awareness and consciousness, it should not.

In Study 1D, the functionalist pattern of awareness attribution reappeared: ratings increased with each subsequent reactivity level—for both human and artificial subjects. However, crucially, at high reactivity, the differences between subject types disappeared (3D). At low reactivity, artificial subjects were attributed significantly higher awareness than humans. At medium reactivity, no significant differences were observed between them, and at high reactivity, the difference only numerically favored humans without reaching statistical significance.

Damage ratings themselves remained largely stable regardless of agent type or reaction level. Only minor and isolated statistically significant differences were found, with no systematic dependence on reactivity or agent type (3C), confirming the suitability of these ratings as a non-mentalistic control measure.

Thus, the gap in high-reactivity scores was only minor when awareness was assessed separately (Study 1B). It did not reach statistical significance with a "non-mental" anchor (Study 1D), but it was substantial when compared with consciousness (Study 1C). This suggests that this effect arose precisely from the direct comparison of awareness and consciousness, rather than from the additional cognitive load of answering two questions.

Study 2

Study 2 involved 400 participants from the UK, recruited via the Prolific platform in June 2026. The experiment was conducted using the Qualtrics service. The sample size justification was based on a power analysis performed via simulation. The goal was to detect medium-sized effects in awareness ratings when comparing humans and artificial agents under high reactivity conditions. After preregistered language comprehension checks and exclusion of participants whose task completion time fell outside the established limits, data from 373 individuals were included in the final analysis. The average age of participants in the sample was 38.9 years. This included 202 men (54%) and 165 women (44%); the remaining participants identified as non-binary or did not specify their gender.

Within a mixed experimental design aimed at studying whether participants attribute consciousness and awareness to humans or artificial agents, researchers varied agent type (human or artificial agent) and construct type (awareness or consciousness) as between-subjects factors. Combinations of agent type and construct type formed four possible participant groups. At the beginning of the study, each participant was randomly assigned to one of these groups. Then, each participant was presented with six contrast vignettes (situational descriptions) as a within-subjects factor. The vignettes covered combinations of two experimental factors: reactivity level (simplified: low or high) and contextual dimension (spatial, temporal, or social). As in the first study, two different scenarios were developed for each dimension to minimize carryover effects. The scenarios included: for the temporal dimension—trading operations and traffic flow management; for the social dimension—psychological support and customer service; for the spatial dimension—facility cleaning and manufacturing processes. Combinations of scenarios and reactivity levels, as well as the order of vignette presentation, were randomized for each participant (examples of vignettes are shown in Table #2).

Consciousness Attribution Demonstrates Essentialist Constraints

The essentialist approach suggests that consciousness attribution remains tied to the subject type—regardless of behavioral evidence of reactivity. Researchers preregistered four hypotheses: humans should be attributed more consciousness than artificial subjects, both at low (E1) and high (E2) reactivity levels, and overall (E3). Furthermore, the level of consciousness attributed to artificial subjects at high reactivity should remain lower than that attributed to humans at low reactivity (E4).

Image #4

All four hypotheses were confirmed (4A). Humans were attributed more consciousness than artificial subjects, both at low and high reactivity levels, and overall.

While for human subjects, consciousness ratings significantly increased with their reactivity, only a slight increase was observed for artificial agents, leading to a pronounced interaction effect between "reactivity" and "agent type."

Finally, confirming hypothesis E4, artificial agents with high reactivity were attributed less consciousness than humans with low reactivity. Thus, even maximal behavioral reactivity cues did not allow the level of attributed consciousness in artificial agents to reach the level recognized for humans with only minimal cues. Preregistered ordinal mixed models yielded similar conclusions for all hypotheses.

Awareness Attribution Follows a General Functionalist Model

According to the functionalist approach, awareness ratings are based on behavioral manifestations, regardless of the subject's type. In this regard, researchers preregistered four hypotheses: awareness ratings for humans and artificial subjects should be similar at both low (F1) and high (F2) reactivity levels; at high reactivity, awareness ratings should be higher than at low reactivity (F3); this increase should be equally pronounced for both subject types—meaning no interaction between "subject type" and "reactivity level" (F4).

In support of hypothesis F3, it was found that awareness ratings significantly increased from low to high reactivity—for both human agents and artificial agents. Reactivity level proved to be the most significant factor influencing awareness ratings (4B).

In support of hypothesis F1, it was found that at low reactivity, humans and artificial agents were perceived as having approximately the same level of awareness. More broadly, no significant effect of agent type was found when assessing awareness: on average, regardless of reactivity level, artificial agents were attributed the same level of awareness as humans.

However, contrary to hypothesis F2, at high reactivity, human agents were rated as having a slightly more pronounced capacity for awareness than artificial agents. Furthermore, contrary to hypothesis F4, the increase in awareness ratings with increasing reactivity was more pronounced for human agents than for artificial agents. Notably, the difference at high reactivity reappeared, even though awareness and consciousness were assessed in a between-subjects design (different participants) rather than direct comparison, as in Study 1C.

Attribution of awareness capacity corresponded to the functionalist model in two out of four preregistered hypotheses (F1 and F3 were confirmed). Both deviations (F2 and F4) pointed to the same outcome: the emergence of a moderate "plateau" in artificial agent awareness ratings, which occurred only at high reactivity levels.

Attribution Patterns Remain Consistent Across Environmental Dimensions: Spatial, Social, and Temporal

To test whether the functionalist and essentialist patterns were specific to certain dimensions, researchers rebuilt the linear mixed model, including the dimension (spatial, social, or temporal) as an additional fixed effect.

It was found that the dimension did not have an overall effect on ratings. Importantly, the dimension did not moderate the effects underlying the two attribution patterns: the reactivity effect was virtually identical across all dimensions, and higher-order interactions involving the dimension factor were statistically insignificant. Only some minor lower-order interactions involving the "dimension" factor reached statistical significance. This reflects minor shifts in overall rating levels between scenarios rather than changes in the attribution patterns themselves.

This indicates that the essentialist model of consciousness and its corresponding functionalist model of awareness are not tied to scenarios embodied in space but are more general, encompassing spatial, social, and temporal dimensions.

Epilogue

The development of AI technologies has spawned not only a vast number of products whose usefulness and effectiveness often hover between zero and negative, but also numerous ethical debates regarding human-AI interaction. Skynet, replicants, HAL 9000—these are some of the most striking AI representatives from cinema. Each, in its respective universe, raises the question of human-AI interaction. Can AI destroy humanity? Can AI gain self-awareness, and will this lead to a "I have no mouth, and I must scream" situation? All these questions share a common root: trust. Humanity placed its complete trust in AI, which was a mistake—a popular preamble to many sci-fi stories about the end of the world and human enslavement.

The authors of the work we reviewed today decided to measure the degrees of human trust towards AI in the context of assessing the latter's capacity for consciousness and awareness.

As the researchers note, while previous studies typically asked general questions about whether AI truly possesses mental states, this study is the first to compare how people attribute the same mental states to AI and humans acting identically in identical circumstances. Potentially, we attach psychological labels to AI, just as we do to humans, because we currently lack a descriptive alternative. Considering that many AI agents are literally trained to imitate humans, attributing human characteristics to them might be a perfectly expected reaction.

To determine how people assess consciousness, researchers conducted experiments involving nearly 1100 participants. One group was presented with brief descriptions of artificial agents exhibiting varying degrees of reaction to their environment. For example, some agents reacted to sounds or recognized the emotional states of those around them.

Another group encountered the same situations, with the exception that the characters were described as humans, not artificial intelligence systems. Participants then rated how conscious or aware of their surroundings each character seemed.

The experiments showed that reactivity—the ability to respond to events—influences how people assess both other humans and artificial intelligence. Characters who reacted more actively to their environment were generally perceived as more conscious and more aware of what was happening.

However, when assessing "consciousness" specifically, a significant difference emerged. Even the most reactive AI was rated as less conscious than a human with the lowest reactivity. In other words, identical behavior did not prompt people to attribute the same level of consciousness to humans and machines.

The results changed noticeably when participants were asked about "awareness." In this case, the ratings for humans and AI largely coincided, suggesting that people perceive the concepts of "awareness" and "consciousness" very differently.

Researchers point out that people are quite willing to acknowledge that AI notices things and is aware of its surroundings. But they draw a clear line when it comes to the concept of "consciousness." People consistently attribute a lower level of consciousness to AI than to humans, even if their behavior is absolutely identical.

The researchers believe that the distinction between "consciousness" and "awareness" provides deeper insight into how humans perceive artificial intelligence.

Awareness is a relatively neutral term. It describes an agent's ability to perceive information from its environment and respond to it. Consciousness, on the other hand, implies something much deeper: the presence of subjective experience and an inner mental life.

Since the concept of "awareness" relies more heavily on observable behavior, people readily apply it to both humans and artificial intelligence. "Consciousness," however, carries a different meaning, leading people to assess AI and humans very differently.

The study's findings also highlight the importance of terminology when discussing artificial intelligence—especially now, as AI systems become increasingly adept at mimicking convincing human behavior. Not to mention that many systems cannot even be called AI, as they are nothing more than large language models, i.e., a talking library.
