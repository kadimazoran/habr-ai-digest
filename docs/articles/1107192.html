We developed AI tools for a well-known Russian airline to help employees make decisions when things go off plan. We describe how the system performed when we tested it on a real disruption.

When an airport temporarily stops accepting and dispatching aircraft, airlines have to quickly reschedule flights. Simply moving or canceling a few flights is not an option. It's necessary to consider where the aircraft are, what flights they are supposed to perform next, how many passengers have already bought tickets, and whether they can be rebooked onto other flights.

Together with the team of one of the Russian airlines, we decided to see if artificial intelligence could handle some of this work. We analyzed the processes of the commercial department, developed eight AI skills, and combined them into a system that helps choose flight schedule modification options. Then we tested it on real data. I'll explain everything step by step.

Where It All Began

I am Sergey Ershov, an AI enthusiast. Together with my partner, we conduct training and implement AI in the business processes of various companies. This story didn't start with flight schedules at all.

The airline approached us with a request to train their top management. We usually conduct very practical training sessions so that employees can immediately try neural networks on their work tasks and understand where and how they can be applied. We had several such training sessions: one for top management, one for the finance department, and one for the commercial department, which handles schedules and resolves unforeseen situations.

It so happened that the training for the commercial department took place on August 20th – the day after a large-scale disruption on August 18-19, when temporary restrictions affected over 230 airline flights.

By the time of the training, we had already assembled the first working version of the system, which in theory could compile a comprehensive picture of the disruption based on ready data and provide a solution: which flights to cancel, which to reschedule, and where to rebook passengers.

We ran the system on fresh data, and it turned out that the decision-making logic was much, much more complex than we initially thought. We started to investigate.

How an Airline Operates During Schedule Disruptions

Let's take an example: the airport is closed, or one of the aircraft becomes inoperable. The information goes into the scheduling system, and the operational control center takes over the task first.

If the restriction is short-term, you can just wait. The benchmark for the "carpet" (ground stop) is about an hour: if the restriction isn't lifted within that time, the schedule needs to be redone.

First, Operational Control Tries to Solve the Problem Without Cancellations

The team rearranges flights between aircraft, changes their execution sequence, and looks for a spare aircraft. But every rearrangement has consequences: the aircraft taken from one flight was supposed to perform the next one a few hours later, and then another one. Therefore, one change can trigger a whole chain reaction – and the aircraft for related flights change.

When Rearrangements Aren't Enough, the Commercial Department Steps In

The task of these employees is to quickly reassemble the schedule in such a way as to:

  1. get as many passengers as possible to their destinations safely,

  2. minimize financial losses due to flight cancellations and rearrangements.

This is where the most labor-intensive part begins – compiling a picture of what's happening. It's necessary to look at:

  • the current schedule and flight statuses,

  • flight load and revenue,

  • available seats on adjacent dates,

  • specific aircraft limitations,

  • airport operating hours,

  • and a number of other factors.

Moreover, the data is stored in different systems: the schedule is in one, bookings are in another, some commercial metrics are in BI, fleet limitations and other parameters are in separate directories.

After that, specialists select flights that could theoretically be rescheduled or canceled and compare several scenarios.

Spoiler: the formula "cancel the least empty or least profitable flight" doesn't work here.

For example, one flight might be less profitable but almost fully booked, and there's nowhere to rebook the passengers. Another brings in more money, but its passengers can be distributed to adjacent flights. In such a situation, it's sometimes more profitable to cancel the more profitable flight.

Additionally, there are flights that don't even make the usual list of cancellation candidates. For example, charters. Restrictions on specific aircraft types, transfer passengers, and flight frequency are also taken into account.

Before the final decision, booking data is rechecked – passengers continue to buy tickets during the calculation period, and the situation itself can change.

After that, the decision is returned to the operational control center. They change the schedule, and booking service employees manually make changes in Leonardo and rebook passengers.

We decided to transfer this entire algorithm to AI so that the airline's commercial department could get a new schedule with all restrictions taken into account in a couple of clicks.

We Developed Eight AI Skills That Mimicked the Actions of Commercial Department Employees

We took the airline's manual process and broke it down into sequential tasks. For each task, we created a skill. The output of one skill became the input for another.

The commercial department employee only needed to upload the data and confirm each subsequent step, ensuring that the system had correctly completed the previous one.

Initially, we divided the process into five stages, but during the process, we realized it wasn't enough and added three more

Essentially, our skills replicate everything that commercial department employees usually did – the same tasks, in the same sequence:

  1. Parse the disruption message. The skill receives free text, a screenshot, or a list of flights and converts them into confirmed parameters: date, airports, flights, disruption type, aircraft, and other inputs.

  2. Determine airport closures and restrictions. The skill parses Rosaviatsiya publications, reconstructs restriction intervals, and identifies airports where restrictions have exceeded the set threshold.

  3. Compile a picture of affected flights. The system takes affected flights and pulls their capacity, bookings, load, revenue, and cancellation priority. Here, the initial queue of candidates appears, from which charters, already canceled flights, and other items not requiring commercial decisions are excluded.

  4. Calculate passenger placement options. The skill checks for available seats on the same route on adjacent dates and calculates whether all passengers can be accommodated. Seats are consumed from the general pool.

  5. Determine which flights to cancel first. The skill groups candidates into pairs or chains, ranks them according to set rules, and forms a justification for why one chain should be considered before another.

  6. Check the aircraft's movement chain. The system determines which flights are physically impossible to operate due to the aircraft's location and calculates how the disruption will affect the aircraft's subsequent flights.

  7. Find a possible aircraft replacement. The skill checks if a flight can be saved by using a different aircraft or aircraft type, considering capacity, route approvals, and airport restrictions.

  8. Assemble the overall disruption scenario. The last skill acts as an orchestrator: it sequentially launches the other tools, passes the result of one stage to the next, and compiles the overall result.

We deliberately limited the AI's freedom. The language model was used where it was necessary to parse human messages or formulate explanations of results. All calculations – flight loads, available seats, probabilities, rankings – were performed by standard Python scripts.

We also did not connect directly to the airline's operational systems. The employee manually exported data from the scheduling system, Leonardo, and BI, and uploaded the files into the scenario.

We Ran the System on a Real Disruption

While preparing the skills, we relied on a real disruption scenario from July 7, 2026, in Krasnodar, for which we knew the final decision made by the commercial department.

That is, we knew the initial data, we knew the employees' logic, and we knew the correct answer. This meant we could run the same data through the system and see if it would arrive at the same solution. And it worked.

The system compiled a picture of the affected flights, checked the possibility of rebooking passengers, and listed the same flights for cancellation in the top positions that the airline employees had canceled.

On August 20th, during the training, we gave it a much more complex task – the recent disruption of August 18-19, which the commercial department employees had just experienced. At that time, the "carpet" plan was repeatedly implemented in several airports over several days: Domodedovo, Sheremetyevo, Sochi, Krasnodar, Nizhny Novgorod, Samara. Restrictions were lifted for a couple of hours, then reimposed – and so on in a cycle.

Participants uploaded real data exports and ran different segments of the disruption. And nuances that were not in our rules began to surface.

The System Prematurely Classified One Flight as a Forced Cancellation

We found out why: its arrival in Domodedovo fell within a temporary restriction interval. At that moment, the airport was operating in "by agreement" mode. This means the flight could not yet be considered impossible.

The rule was corrected right at the training: we separated full restrictions from the "by agreement" mode, and then recalculated the scenario. The flight was no longer classified as a cancellation for this reason.

The System Ranked Individual Flights, but Decisions Needed to Be Made in Pairs

Employees explained that in many cases, a decision is made for a pair of opposing legs of the same aircraft. Therefore, both passenger load and rebooking, as well as the consequences for the schedule, need to be calculated for the pair as a whole. The rule was immediately applied in the calculation, and the scenario was recalculated.

Similar clarifications emerged throughout the day. Sometimes it miscalculated available seats, sometimes schedule data conflicted, and the system continued to calculate when it should have stopped and directed the question to a specialist.

It turns out that we were able to build the basic logic of the process. But when employees who handle these situations daily started using the tool, dozens of minor rules and exceptions began to surface.

In some situations, even within the company, there isn't one universal rule. The decision depends on professional judgment. Therefore, controversial points were turned into decision points: the system offers a default option, and the specialist confirms it or chooses another.

We Rewrote the System and Conducted 164 Tests

After the training, we compiled the employees' feedback into one version and began to test each skill and the entire chain separately. We uncovered many more nuances that we couldn't account for earlier.

For example, the skill that compiled airport restrictions from Rosaviatsiya publications once incorrectly calculated the duration of a restriction. The reason was in message filtering: the calculation included the message about the restriction being imposed, but the publication about its lifting might have fallen outside the selected period and been cut off. In one run, this caused the interval for the airport to be calculated as 1093 minutes instead of 1346.

We redesigned the interval compilation so that the imposition and lifting of a restriction were considered a linked pair of events, and the period boundary did not break such a pair. After that, we added separate tests for overlapping intervals – for example, when a restriction was imposed late in the evening and lifted the next day.

And there were many such details. As a result, we ran the same August scenario 164 times, and the final run consisted of 13 sequential steps: from parsing the disruption message to the final Excel output, result verification, and calculation log. The system completed it entirely and self-checked the generated output before showing it to the employee.

The System Arrived at the Same Decision as the Commercial Department Specialist

During the training on August 20th, one of the commercial department employees manually calculated one of the disruption scenarios and decided to cancel a specific pair of flights.

Her logic was as follows:

  • these flights had 296 passengers;

  • all of them could be rebooked onto other flights in the next two days;

  • after canceling the pair, the aircraft would remain in the same city and could perform subsequent flights according to the schedule;

  • if the original connection was maintained, the aircraft would return too late;

  • because of this, two subsequent flights would have to be delayed by approximately two hours.

After all the improvements, we gave the system the same set of initial data and ran the scenario again. It went through all 13 steps and selected the same pair of flights.

The justification also matched: canceling this pair would allow subsequent flights to be maintained and avoid their approximately two-hour delay.

For us, this was an important control result. The specialist arrived at this option based on her experience and understanding of the entire flight program. After we translated these rules into skills and code, the system reproduced the same decision and the same logic for choosing it on the same data.

What Was the Result

Over the course of the project, we analyzed the commercial department's real process, extracted some rules from the employees' experience, and assembled them into a working system. Now it can go through the entire disruption scenario like a human and provide the specialist with a verifiable result.

But perhaps the most valuable result for us was broader. The project began as training employees to work with neural networks. In the process, they themselves began to understand the skills, change the rules for their tasks, and think not in terms of individual prompts, but in terms of entire AI tools.

For example, during the first training, we explained how to give AI agents standard access to corporate data and functions through MCP. Later, the airline team, without our involvement, created their own MCP server.

The point of MCP is that an employee or AI agent doesn't need to integrate with each airline service separately. They connect to MCP once and can then access functions available through it directly from their familiar AI tool.

For instance, an employee could write in Claude: "Show the status of this flight" or "Find flights for this date." Claude itself understands which airline tool to call via MCP, gets the data, and returns the answer in the dialogue.

At that time, only Turkish Airlines had its own MCP among airlines.
