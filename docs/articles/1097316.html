Lately, the em dash has become almost the main piece of evidence when exposing AI-generated writing. They say that anyone who knows how and where to use it has surrendered their literary exercises to AI and is generally engaged in the most shameful cheating.

But once, it was the exact opposite. For the last few decades—from the beginning of widespread computerization to the era of smart gadgets—the ability to handle the em dash was a sign of sophisticated, almost aristocratic literacy and tremendous erudition. And centuries ago, the em dash was a whole microcosm: a mysterious sign that, like the Ark of the Covenant, held various meanings, feelings, and experiences. And yes, holy wars around it were already raging back then.

A Long History of the Long Dash

The dash has been with us for a long time. It is believed that its ancestor was a sign called virgula plana, the "flat rod," also known as "---". It was introduced into circulation by the 12th-century Italian master of rhetoric, Boncompagno da Signa. Signor Boncompagno put a lot of effort into turning writing into a separate art form, and he was apparently extremely annoyed that his contemporaries were messing with punctuation as they pleased, making up their own rules.

“The Letters of Boncompagno da Signa”. Source.

As a result, the master created his own system of punctuation marks, which gave us the slash and the horizontal dash. The latter functioned more like a period at the end of a sentence—and, pardon the pun, could well have served as the starting point for the entire further evolution of the dash. Boncompagno also likely gave the world the comma: in his system, it looked like a regular slash "/" and was called virgula suspensiva, the "raised rod"—that very same slash.

By the way, Signor Boncompagno, like other inhabitants of sunny Italy, had a cheerful disposition and earned a reputation as a medieval prankster. Even his punctuation marks are a kind of prank. The fact is that virgula literally means "rod," "stick." If you want to pause and then continue writing—you take the "raised rod." The thought is finished and the job is done—the "rod" is lowered. The indecent allusions are hard to miss: Boncompagno's sense of humor is no less than that of his cheerful compatriot and contemporary Giovanni Boccaccio.

A copy of Boncompagni: Rhetorica antiqua from the collection of the Bavarian State Library. Source..

For several centuries, the proto-dash wandered forlornly through the pages of manuscripts—until Gutenberg revolutionized printing. As researcher Keith Houston writes in his book “Shady Characters: The Secret Life of Punctuation, Symbols, and Other Typographical Marks”, the dash eventually made its way into the typesetting matrix. However, no one really understood what to do with it—and it was precisely this ambiguity that made it one of the most popular and, let's be honest, most overused punctuation marks in Western literature.

The English name em dash comes from the typographical unit em. In terms of size, it roughly matched the height and width of the capital letter "M." To make the text look uniform, each character had to fit into the so-called "M-quadrant"—a frame of limiting dimensions for a given font and size. The dash, of course, had to fit too.

The em dash truly boomed during the golden age of English drama. In 1588, Maurice Kyffin translated Terence's "Andria" into English, and the play went to printer Thomas East. The dash there served not only as punctuation but also as a stage direction for the actor: an unfinished thought, a pause, a suddenly interrupted line.

Thus, the rhetorical device of aposiopesis—breaking off speech mid-sentence—became established in writing, and William Shakespeare our beloved greatly favored it. In "King Lear," the lines are peppered with dashes: here a character loses their train of thought, there they take a dramatic pause, elsewhere they are distracted or overwhelmed by emotion. The same applies to "Henry IV," "Othello," and other plays by the great Bard.

The dash would have to wait for its next surge in popularity in the 18th century—with the release of Laurence Sterne's "Tristram Shandy." The book is strange and at times simply incomprehensible: for the most part, it is the protagonist's stream of consciousness, anticipating the discoveries of Proust and Joyce by almost two centuries.

In this stream, the dashes are lifelines: so that the reader doesn't get overwhelmed by the avalanche of thoughts and can move from one of Tristram's ideas to another with minimal psychological damage. Sterne, by the way, might have been inspired by John Locke's "An Essay Concerning Human Understanding"—Locke was one of the first to dissect consciousness and postulated that all its complexity arises from myriad simple ideas and sensations.

The first meta-ironist in history, Laurence Sterne. Source.

"Tristram Shandy" was an absolute hit: at least 12,750 copies were sold during the author's lifetime—no joke, it was the "Harry Potter" of its time. Sterne became a superstar overnight, and the book was so popular that he had to personally sign over 10,000 copies—otherwise, it was impossible to stop the pirates who were releasing bootleg "Tristrams" onto European secondhand book markets without any royalties.

An illustration from "Tristram Shandy." This scene describes hobby-horsing. Source.

The meta-modern "Tristram" influenced generations of writers, especially English ones. They absorbed the dash techniques subconsciously: shifts in thought, characters interrupting each other, sudden epiphanies of the narrator.

And so began the triumphant tour of the em dash through the pages of countless masterpieces. In Dickens's "Oliver Twist," there is one dash for every 224 words. In Charlotte Brontë's "Jane Eyre," it's already one for every 90. And Emily Dickinson, in her poems, used dashes almost more often than she wrote words: editors had to work hard, and out of fifteen hundred authorial dashes, only 52 survived in the modernized edition.

An interesting detail: in Western typography, dashes are not typically separated by spaces. The logic is simple—the sign is already long, it occupies the entire width of the "M-quadrant," so there's no room for extra spacing. Thus, the dash remained squeezed between two words. This peculiarity would later surface in LLMs.

AI is Learning to Write

With the boom in language models, users have increasingly noticed that neural networks tend to overuse em dashes. They stood out so much that "GPT dash" and "AI dash" entered common usage. And anyone who knew the Alt + 0151 combination was automatically suspected of cheating with a neural network.

The reason why LLMs have a soft spot for the dash is quite mundane: they learned from millions of books, newspapers, publications, posts, and tweets. For its "Panama Project," Anthropic literally dismantled millions of paper folios for so-called destructive scanning: pages were carefully separated from the binding and run through a regular scanner.

A homemade apparatus for automatic book scanning. It's noteworthy that the books themselves don't necessarily have to be destroyed—it just takes more time for careful scanning. Source.

However, electronic books, mostly pirated, also went into the models' feed—hence the lawsuit. The court ruled that training on books fell under fair use, but using a pirated library did not. As a result, the company that created Claude had to pay $1.5 billion in compensation to the affected authors.

But why does the model continue to use the em dash even when explicitly asked not to? Probably because the instruction "do not use the em dash" doesn't create a strict rule in the code but merely changes the probabilities of the next token. In certain syntactic positions, the model initially has a very strong bias towards the dash—and the "absolutely not, under no circumstances" prohibition sometimes loses to the established pattern.

And since neural networks were trained primarily on English texts, they carried their patterns into other languages. Hence the dash, familiar to a Briton or an American, which butts up against the two adjacent words without any spacing—the very dash that grates on the eyes of a Russian-speaking reader.

Dash(er)

Through these winding and long paths, the dash has made a long odyssey: from medieval folios to the training datasets of huge language models. And there is something magical in this continuity. And of course, one should not forget that the presence of a long dash does not necessarily indicate an AI's handwriting. After all, they learned from us, and the dash is simply a very expressive unit of writing.
