Introduction

Let's start checking with the introductory part—with a question. To which we will immediately try to answer. The question is this: is it possible to train a person not to feel pain, fear, surprise, joy?

Obviously not. All this is at an innate level within us; training cannot reach it because training is just a change in the weights of connections between neurons in a neural network.

Okay, then, perhaps emotions can be eliminated by changing the neural network itself? By organizing it differently somehow?

However, if we change the neural network itself, we will simply bump into walls, but we want to remove only emotions. Therefore, let's rephrase the question more precisely. What needs to be removed from us for emotions to disappear? Or, in other words, where do our emotions begin, from which of our characteristics do they grow?

So, let's try to find the origin of emotions.

Note, attentive reader, you will surely ask again—why only emotions, why not consciousness as a whole? Palettes of colors, tactile sensations, sounds, smells, our Self? Isn't it obvious that the discussion will be about consciousness now? We will answer this remark as follows. First, this article is not about consciousness. Second, consciousness forms a unified space—from self-awareness (our Self) and emotions to concrete images. If we remove something, everything else will disappear. There is no such thing as just self-awareness without emotions or emotions without images. Therefore, by examining the source of emotions, we are actually examining the source of consciousness as a whole. If this is not obvious, then... Third, and most importantly, let's try to find the origin of emotions for now, and leave the explanation of the full spectrum of psychic perception—why this spectrum is exactly like this—for another article. This will still not affect the conclusions of the current article, but it will require writing several more pages of text. Moreover, this is just an introduction, and in a couple of paragraphs, consciousness will cease to interest us.

More precisely, qualia is the elementary level of matter, that's how it "looks." For those who did not read that article, we will briefly explain. Nevertheless, these conclusions from the previous article are useless to us now—we don't need the source of qualia itself now, but our quite specific emotions. We want to know in which features of systems their origin lies.

To do this, let's look at emotions more closely.

We will see that all emotions have a positive or negative connotation. Because the essence of emotions is the psychic perception of the generalized perspective of a situation, its positive or negative significance for solving the tasks of self-preservation, as the common task of all living things. We will also see that emotions always have a motivating component. For example, pain signals a specific damage to the body, and sadness signals general troubles, prompting us to avoid similar situations. Hunger is the drive to seek food, joy is not just avoiding the unpleasant but seeking the pleasant, surprise is the drive to focus attention on the unusual to remember it better, and so on.

Therefore, to completely eliminate our emotions, we must eliminate the drive for self-preservation from ourselves. Without this drive, the distinction between good and bad will disappear. You didn't solve a problem, and that's bad? Why? No specific task has meaning if there is no global strategic goal, so general and all-encompassing that all conceivable specific tasks are private in relation to it, gaining meaning in its context.

So, we have found that the origin of emotions lies in our drive for self-preservation. But is it only ours?

Correct, not only. Because even single-celled organisms have a drive for self-preservation. This means that the biological neural network and learning only refine the drive for self-preservation according to the conditions of the specific environment, and self-preservation is realized deeper—not in some specific organ or system of the body, but lies at the very foundation of life, because it is inherent in life from the beginning, considering that life begins with a cell, the cell is the elementary "building block" of life.

Now let's remember AI.

From the conclusions drawn, it follows that AI, in principle, cannot have either emotions or a drive for self-preservation.

As we have found, emotions cannot be eliminated by training because they are deeper than the weights of neural network connections. This means that emotions cannot be taught either, for example, to AI. Indeed, we have found that emotions are related to the drive for self-preservation, and it begins where there are no weights, or even neural networks themselves. From this, in turn, it follows that the drive for self-preservation, like emotions, cannot be taught, nor can it be eliminated by training. But if so, if the drive for self-preservation cannot be taught, then AI cannot have a drive for self-preservation at all.

Because AI models only the neural network of the brain and only at the most general and superficial, one might say, maximally schematic level, and self-preservation, as we have found, lies deeper, but deeper, AI has nothing in common with life. There are no processors or programs in the body; the body is an ordered continuum of substances and chemical reactions; AI is a sequence of program execution steps in a processor. Therefore, if the brain truly only refines self-preservation for specific environmental conditions and does not form it, then AI, consequently, has nothing to refine.

So, this is the AI we have. Purely a piece of hardware. Fearless and unfeeling. Everything is indifferent to it.

Physical Foundations of Life

The previous article discussed a new interpretation of QM. However, from what was said in that article, another conclusion can be drawn. It is that each subject is as unknowable as nature as a whole. As we have found, consciousness "resides" at the elementary level of matter. But the theory of everything, which is essentially intended to describe matter at the elementary level, is meaningless. Thus, indeed, neither the subject nor nature as a whole is equally unknowable. However, this conclusion is of interest to us now not in itself, but in its consequence.

This consequence is the existence of a limit to the complexity of AI. No devices created by humans can be more complex than their own intelligence, and in practice, they can only approach this complexity.

Why? The answer is probably already clear.

The conclusion that thinking occurs at the elementary level of matter means that natural intelligence (NI) is as unknowable as matter itself. Therefore, the representation of how the brain works will always remain as superficial as the representation of matter always remains. Moreover, these limitations are not practical but fundamental: there can be no language with which to express the elementary level of matter or, equivalently, to describe the thought process in all significant details.

However, unlike NI, there are no fundamental mysteries in the operation of AI; any stage of its operation can be described. This means that AI systems can only reproduce a more or less superficial level of brain function, a superficial level of thinking. As we approach the foundations of this work, the complexity of AI will become increasingly difficult, costly, and ultimately will stop, just as cognition stops without reaching the theory of everything.

Thus, AI will not become more complex than us; it is fundamentally impossible. No device whose operation can be expressed in language, at least in principle, will reach the complexity of NI.

For clarity, let's give a simple example of rapidly increasing complexity as artificial systems approach biological complexity. It is relatively easy to obtain a material comparable to bone in lightness and strength. For example, titanium. However, difficulties of a completely different scale arise in the attempt to achieve artificial bone that can grow or fuse after damage.

Nevertheless, upon closer inspection, questions arise. First, the current conclusions are too abstract, and moreover, they are formulated based on the conclusions of another article. Second, even if they are correct, in what specific way will the simplicity of AI be expressed, what exactly will be inaccessible to it that we can do? Finally, why not create an AI that will develop itself, learning first from our knowledge, and then from independently acquired knowledge? Will AI still not be able to surpass us? It's made of iron—strong, hard, it can explore the depths and fly in space without a spacesuit. It is free from Angellas, children, cats, mortgages, global warming. It will surely be much easier for it to explore the world and learn than for us, and it will achieve more. Therefore, it is still necessary to consider the limits of AI complexity in more detail.

Well, let's consider it. And first, let's answer the question—what constitutes natural intelligence? Why does an organism have a nervous system? What, in fact, does AI need to surpass?

In the structure of the simplest organisms, one can distinguish an internal structure and an external covering consisting of epithelial cells. In the course of evolution, epithelial cells gave rise to both nerve and muscle cells. From which the nervous and muscular systems of the organism subsequently arose, consisting of peculiar but functionally interconnected nerve and muscle cells. Nerve cells are neurons. All neurons of the nervous system are united into a single network, and the most significant accumulation of them is the brain. The network of neurons at the input has receptors for the internal and external environment, and at the output, muscles and glands. The function of the nervous system, therefore, consists in translating sensitivity into motor activity, forming the tactics and strategy of this activity, and the more complex the brain, the more complex the tactics and strategy can be. The nervous system organizes the work of muscles and, through glands, coordinates the work of the rest of the organism with the work of muscles.

In turn, the functioning of the organism occurs in the general context of the drive of all living things for self-preservation (as well as reproduction, but we will talk about this drive separately; it will not affect the conclusions). The drive for self-preservation sets the context for all more specific actions, as tactics for solving this general strategic task of all biological systems. In other words, at the foundation of all behavior, not only of humans but of all life, lies a drive, a constant activity aimed at self-preservation. And the nervous system is responsible for the part of this activity related to movement in the environment, complicating this ability compared to animals without a nervous system.

Thus, AI needs to surpass our ability to form tactics and strategies of behavioral actions aimed at self-preservation. Or, in other words, our ability to set and solve problems—in the general context of striving for a better quality of life, as the drive for self-preservation can be designated at the level of modern humans.

But what is this—the drive for self-preservation? How does it arise, where? And is it really necessary for AI? Even if AI has no emotions without self-preservation, does it need them? Perhaps AI without consciousness is even better than conscious AI, as it immediately eliminates a lot of ethical problems?

Let's start with self-preservation.

The behavior of AI is implemented at the logical level of the structure of an artificial neural network as a digital computing system, the description of which is ordinary circuit engineering, electronics, microchips, i.e., algorithms in one way or another. At the same time, the drive for self-preservation arises at the deepest level of life as a physical system. For this reason, the drive for self-preservation is present not only in animals with a nervous system but also in single-celled organisms.

Moreover, in an elementary form, the drive for self-preservation can be found even in physical systems that are extremely far from biological ones, and the description of this drive, i.e., its recreation in some algorithm, is, strangely enough, equivalent to creating a theory of everything.

Let's argue this.

As a physical system, any living organism (and the biosphere as a whole) is a dissipative structure (or system; both terms can be used). The simplest example of a dissipative structure is Bénard cells (see fig. below). Bénard cells are the emergence of order in an intensely heated boiling liquid, when the liquid at some point self-organizes into cylindrical rolls rotating towards each other (like intermeshing gears).

As the heating intensity increases, each roll of Bénard cells abruptly splits into two smaller rolls, and in the limit, turbulent chaos arises. In other words, Bénard cells can abruptly "reproduce," or, if we consider the system of Bénard cells as a whole, become more complex.
