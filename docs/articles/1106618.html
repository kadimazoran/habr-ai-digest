But before we dive into the investigation, let me briefly explain why I consider this topic important.

Companies talk a lot about safe and ethical AI. Users are forbidden from reverse-engineering software and creating video game mods. We are warned about omnipotent AGI that is about to go out of control and take over the world if we don't slow down model development and allow bad actors to use LLMs without restriction.

But what's the point of obedient artificial intelligence if the operators of such a system become the military and intelligence agencies, for whom AI will allow the automation of the state's monopoly on violence and coercion?

The conversation goes beyond the technical reliability of models. AI will not make the world a better place if it is used to concentrate power, conduct mass surveillance, and kill people — even under the guise of "national security" and "protecting democracy."

The risks of widespread dissemination of such technologies are far more tangible than many hypothetical scenarios of future AGI. This doesn't require superintelligence: it's enough to use technologies for the unlimited concentration of power.

The world still lacks a universal international treaty prohibiting the creation and use of autonomous weapons. Meanwhile, algorithms are already used at various stages of the AI Kill Chain — from intelligence gathering to target selection and strike planning. AI labs forget about universal ethics when they sign contracts for the development of tools intended to "enhance military advantage, increase practical value for the military, or improve military decision-making" across all branches of the armed forces.

We live in a world where the largest technology companies are openly integrating their developments into military infrastructure, while society has extremely limited opportunities to control this process — or at least make it more transparent. Therefore, the international community faces a new challenge: to define rules that will help control those who control AI.

To understand the depth of the problem, look at the statement by Dario Amodei, CEO of Anthropic:

"Partially autonomous weapons, like those used in Ukraine today, are vital for defending democracy. Even fully autonomous weapons — systems that completely remove humans from the decision-making process and automatically select and engage targets — may prove critical to our national defense.

However, current state-of-the-art AI systems are not yet reliable enough to control fully autonomous weapons. We will not knowingly provide a product that puts American service members and civilians at risk. We have offered the Department of Defense to collaborate directly with us on research and development aimed at improving the reliability of such systems, but the department has not accepted our offer."

It turns out that Dario considers autonomous weapons not a threat to humanity, but an engineering project that merely needs refinement before being released into production.

Just a few years ago, major AI companies publicly refused military applications of their technologies. Now they are signing contracts with the Pentagon, and fully autonomous weapons are discussed as a promising technology. It seems their widespread adoption is a matter of AI development and the effectiveness of cooperation between the military and corporations.

Below is a retelling of Sam Biddle's article from The Intercept:

AI Giants Work Hand-in-Hand With the Pentagon, Contracts Reveal

The dispute between Anthropic and the Pentagon was public. The parties exchanged sharp statements about how the military could use AI for its purposes. The terms of the contract that caused the conflict remained closed for a long time. The Intercept, with the support of the organization Legal Advocates for Safe Science and Technology, filed a lawsuit under the US Freedom of Information Act and obtained documents about the military department's deals with Anthropic and its competitors.

The collection included agreements that the US Department of Defense concluded with Anthropic, Google, OpenAI, and xAI in July 2025, and subsequent amendments to them. The maximum amount for each contract was $200 million. The companies committed to developing prototypes of AI tools for tasks related to military operations, automated decision-making, logistics, and intelligence. According to The Intercept, the prototypes formed the basis of tools that were already operational in the department's classified networks by the time of publication. Some terms are redacted in the published documents.

According to Sophia Goodfriend, a researcher at Cambridge University and a visiting fellow at the Middle East Initiative program at Harvard, the documents likely describe the scale of cooperation between AI labs and the Pentagon so directly for the first time. "The terms under which engineers closely collaborate with the military department, creating AI systems for surveillance, target selection, and killing people, are particularly noteworthy," she told the publication.

In the official statements of the parties after the deals were concluded, there was little specificity and a lot of vague language. "We must equip our fighters with 21st-century technologies so they can counter 21st-century threats," explained Pentagon spokesperson Sean Parnell at the time.

Google representatives expressed themselves just as evasively: "These advanced AI solutions will enable the Department of Defense to effectively address defense challenges and expand the deployment of AI agents in enterprise systems. This will accelerate innovation and increase efficiency through flexible and proven technologies."

But thanks to the documents, it became clear that the contracts went far beyond ordinary software sales.

What the Journalists Found in the Documents

All four contracts include a provision for information exchange, which entails two-way data transfer and sharing of expertise. Developers were required to grant the Pentagon access to their most advanced language models. The agreement with Google explicitly provided for the company's participation in developing a strategy for AI implementation within the Department of Defense and the Chief Digital and Artificial Intelligence Office (CDAO).

The military could provide companies with classified data for testing models on departmental tasks, information on operational tasks and threats, as well as plans for further AI implementation. All four companies were to advise the Pentagon on these plans and inform the military about the capabilities of their models, current and future application scenarios, and technology development prospects.

Google was also tasked with informing the Pentagon about how US competitors are using AI.

The companies were to train military personnel and conduct briefings. The documents specifically mention Google's workshops on responsible AI implementation. Engineers and policy specialists from all four companies were to participate in exercises where real-world scenarios are simulated.

The Pentagon expected that such an exchange between the military and the private sector would allow for "step-by-step refinement of advanced AI models to solve practical problems in areas such as intelligence analysis, cybersecurity, and autonomous systems."

Risks

The contracts required the labs to assess model risks and participate in exercises to identify potential threats. The Pentagon believes that companies can warn the department in advance about AI-related dangers. However, Heidi Hlaaf, Chief Scientist at the AI Now Institute and former OpenAI security engineer, doubts that the developers themselves can build reliable limitation systems, as OpenAI and Anthropic recently reported that during testing, models hacked into other companies' infrastructure.

According to Hlaaf's assessment, the labs pay too much attention to topics convenient for them, such as the alleged international AI arms race and the existential threat to humanity. One example is science fiction scenarios in the vein of "The Terminator," which exaggerate the importance of the companies themselves.

She believes that for the benefit of society, risks should be assessed independently. Entrusting developers to report on the risks of their own products creates a conflict of interest. Furthermore, it "undermines democratic processes when AI labs are allowed to make their own judgments on life-and-death risks."

Why the Pentagon and Anthropic Argued

After the development of prototypes, the project's scope expanded. New versions of the contracts included additional items and amendments, but the government concealed their content, citing military and commercial secrets.

The conflict between the military and Anthropic erupted during the discussion of these amendments. The company refused to sign the new agreement unless it included an explicit ban on using their technologies for domestic surveillance and the development of autonomous weapons. Following this, US Secretary of Defense Pete Hegset declared the company a threat to the supply chain and banned government agencies from using its services. In August, a federal judge overturned this decision.

To avoid losing time, the Pentagon entered into new agreements with Google, OpenAI, and xAI. These provided for the deployment of tools that the companies had been developing since the summer of 2025. According to media reports, the agreement with OpenAI was approved on February 27. Sam Altman admitted that the deal was "definitely rushed." The next day, OpenAI published a text defending its agreement with the military department and insisting that it contained clauses prohibiting the use of its technologies for domestic surveillance.

Amendments to the OpenAI contract dated February 27 stipulate that the company will send engineers to military structures. There, they will assist US government officials in implementing and using AI models. Developers "may be assigned to combat support structures, including joint commands, components of the armed forces, and components in theaters of operation." Furthermore, the "System Oversight" section is entirely redacted, and similar agreements with xAI and Google do not have such a section.

Two documents state that the Pentagon asked OpenAI to ensure that models refuse military requests as little as possible. The department and OpenAI responded that draft materials were mistakenly included in the document. According to them, the signed contract does not contain such a condition.

Erosion of Values

The close relationship with the US military, enshrined in contracts, shows how far the AI industry has moved from its previous public commitments to safety and the common good.

OpenAI began as a non-profit organization whose goal was to ensure that artificial general intelligence would benefit all of humanity. As recently as early 2024, the company's rules explicitly prohibited the use of its technologies "for military purposes and for warfare." In 2025, Google removed restrictions from its principles concerning surveillance and technologies intended to cause harm to people.

But times have changed, and now both companies are tapping into the Pentagon's nearly trillion-dollar budget and trying to enhance the lethal capabilities of an army that recently bombed schoolgirls in Iran and civilian vessels in the Caribbean.

Goodfriend points out that Anthropic and OpenAI spoke about strict limitations on the development of autonomous weapons and mass surveillance tools only after signing contracts that mentioned "autonomous systems." And it is these contracts that show how deceptive the public declarations of these labs about their principles are.

She doubts that the companies will actually be able to limit the use of models for military purposes.

Anthropic, Google, and xAI did not respond to The Intercept's questions about the military contracts. OpenAI representative Nate Evans told the publication that the signed agreement solidifies the company's public statement. According to him, OpenAI technologies cannot be used for mass surveillance within the US, controlling autonomous weapon systems, or automated decision-making with serious consequences.

How AI Is Used in Warfare

In March, The Wall Street Journal reported that, despite Hegset's ban, US Central Command used Anthropic technologies to strike targets in Iran and "identify targets."

Technology companies work closely with the military, and the latter use their technologies to identify targets for airstrikes. At the same time, it is unclear whether companies can control the use of their software in combat operations once it is in the Pentagon's hands.

In an interview with Bloomberg, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei was asked if the company's tools were used in the US strike on a school on the first day of the war with Iran, which resulted in the deaths of 120 Iranian schoolchildren. He replied that he did not know.

Biddle notes that due to commercial secrecy and national security restrictions, the full extent of US technology companies' involvement in foreign military operations remains unknown.

Vivian Dong of Legal Advocates for Safe Science and Technology says: "The very technology that, just weeks ago, independently hacked websites on the open internet, is now being used to help the Department of Defense make decisions. Society has the right to know how the department uses this technology and what limitations apply to it."
