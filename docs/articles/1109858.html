Abstraction: The Foundation of Everything

About forty-two thousand years ago, someone took a baboon's fibula and carved 29 notches into it. The bone was found in the Lebombo Cave in Swaziland. It might have been a lunar calendar: 29 days in a lunar month. But that's not the important part.

What's important is that a notch is a sign representing a quantity, detached from any specific object. Five sheep, five stones, five days—these are entirely different things, but they share the concept of "five." Someone, for the first time, isolated this commonality and made it a separate entity. From this point on, numbers could be manipulated without thinking about sheep: added, doubled, compared.

This is abstraction. Not "simplification," as is often thought. Simplification removes details. Abstraction does something different: it separates form from its carrier and turns the form into an independent entity that can be worked with. An animal sees three sheep and reacts only to the sheep. A human sees simply "three," can add one, and get "four," without thinking about sheep at all. The difference isn't in the number of details, but in the fact that the symbol is no longer tied to what it represents.

The entire story we're about to tell is this same operation, repeated many, many times: detach the form, make it an object, build a form upon a form. The notch was the first instance. Large language models are the latest, for now.

Part 1: How Humans Learned to Separate Form from Thing

The Bone That Counts

On the Ishango bone, found in the Congo and dated to around twenty thousand years ago, notches are arranged in three columns. According to one interpretation, one column shows 11, 13, 17, 19—prime numbers. Another interpretation suggests pairs resembling doublings: 3 and 6, 4 and 8, 10 and 5. Someone wasn't just counting sheep or days. Someone was exploring the structure of numbers themselves, noticing which numbers are divisible and which are not, and recording it on bone.

This is no longer mere counting. It's the dawn of mathematics: thought about numbers, not things. Abstraction became an object of study in itself. Humans began to think about how they think.

Clay That Signifies

The next major step occurred in Mesopotamia around 8000 BCE, and it was an accounting one. Archaeologist Denise Schmandt-Besserat proposed in the 1970s that small clay figurines—cones, spheres, cylinders—were not toys or amulets, but counting tokens. A cone represented a measure of grain, a sphere a measure of oil, a cylinder an animal. The commodity left, but the sign remained.

This is abstraction of abstraction. The notch separated number from thing. The token separated the sign from the commodity: no longer a sack of grain, but a clay cone representing it. Signs began to be sealed inside clay envelopes—bullae—to prevent tampering. Then, impressions of the tokens started appearing on the outside of the envelopes, so one could see the contents without opening them. And at some point, someone realized: why bother with the envelope if you can just draw the signs on clay?

Thus, around 3400 BCE in Uruk, writing was born. Not as poetry or prayer, but as accounting. The earliest texts are lists of grain, livestock, and oil. Literature would appear later, as a byproduct of the accounting system.

Then, another abstract leap occurred. The sign for "arrow" in Sumerian was pronounced "ti." It began to be used to record the syllable "ti" in any word where this sound appeared, regardless of meaning. The sign detached from the thing and became associated with a sound. This is phonetic writing: a rebus where a picture represents not an object, but a sound.

The Phoenician alphabet, around 1050 BCE, took this idea to its limit: 22 signs for consonants. The Greeks added vowels, creating a system where infinite linguistic variety could be recorded with a finite set of symbols. This is compression of incredible power. Everything that can be said can be captured by about two dozen little marks.

The Coin That Has Value

Around 600 BCE in Lydia, in western Asia Minor, coins began to be minted. Before this, trade was largely barter or tied to specific commodities: a cow, a sack of grain, a bar of metal. The coin did something different—it separated value from the thing itself.

Value became a number that could be stored, transferred, accumulated, and compared. A universal equivalent, not tied to any cow or grain. It's the same operation as with the notch and the token: the form (value) was separated from its carrier (commodity) and made an independent entity. The entire economy grew from this abstraction, but it was born as an act of separation, familiar from the Lebombo bone.

The Greeks: Abstraction Becomes a Method

As already understood, the Greeks did not invent abstraction. It had existed for millennia. They did something else: they turned it into a conscious method and began to study the very capacity for abstraction.

Pythagoras and his school, in the 6th century BCE, declared that everything is number. Not "numbers describe things," but numbers are the essence of things. The ratios of string lengths create harmony, and beneath the visible diversity of the world lies a mathematical structure. Abstraction became ontology: what is separated from the thing was declared more real than the thing itself.

Socrates: Truth Versus Rhetoric

In the 5th century BCE, in Athens, a man emerged who shifted abstraction from nature to himself. Socrates (469–399 BCE) wrote nothing. He walked the city, asking people questions: What is justice? What is courage? What is good? And he showed that his interlocutors didn't know what they thought they knew.

Behind this was a specific polemic. The Sophists, paid teachers of rhetoric, argued: there is no truth, only opinions. Protagoras said, "Man is the measure of all things": as it seems to each person, so it is. In Athenian democracy, where the ability to persuade an assembly or a court was paramount, this was not a philosophical abstraction but a practical skill. Whoever speaks better is right.

Socrates sought something else: definitions. Not "what seems just," but "what makes an act just." Not opinion, but the essence common to all cases. He contrasted the fluidity of opinions with stable knowledge, discoverable through honest questioning.

In 399 BCE, Athenian democracy killed him. The charges: corrupting the youth and not honoring the city's gods. A jury of five hundred, a vote, hemlock. The city that prided itself on truth emerging from free debate executed the man who conducted that debate most honestly. Rhetoric triumphed over truth. Opinion triumphed over knowledge. The mob voted—and the philosopher died.

This became the central trauma of Greek thought. Socrates' student, Plato, witnessed this and concluded: truth cannot be left to the whims of opinion, persuasion, and mob votes. It needs a solid foundation, independent of who speaks best or how the vote went. Plato found such a foundation in the world of Forms: eternal, unchanging essences grasped by the intellect, not the senses, and independent of human opinions.

The problem posed by Socrates' death—how to secure truth so it cannot be argued away or drowned out by votes—defined the subsequent intellectual trajectory. Plato offered one answer. Aristotle, born fifteen years after Socrates' death and inheriting this problem through Plato, offered another.

Aristotle: The Guarantee of Truth

Aristotle (384–322 BCE) was born in Stagira, in northern Greece. His father was a court physician to the Macedonian king, and knowledge in this family was a craft, not an inspiration. At seventeen, Aristotle joined Plato's Academy and spent twenty years there, until his teacher's death. He never met Socrates, but he inherited his question through Plato: where is the solid foundation of knowledge if opinions are fluid, rhetoric is persuasive, and the mob can execute a philosopher?

Aristotle rejected Plato's answer of eternal Forms. To understand the world, one must look at the world. But he found the foundation of truth not in things, nor in Forms, but in the very structure of correct reasoning.

First, the laws without which reasoning is impossible. The three laws of classical logic, devised by Aristotle. The Law of Identity: A is A; every thing and every concept is identical to itself, and the meaning of a term cannot be substituted during reasoning. The Law of Non-Contradiction: Nothing can be both A and not-A at the same time; A and not-A are incompatible. The Law of the Excluded Middle: Of two contradictory statements, one must be true, and there is no third option; it is either A or not-A. This is the bedrock. Without it, any reasoning collapses, and no syllogism works.

Then, the syllogism. All men are mortal. Socrates is a man. Therefore, Socrates is mortal. Aristotle's discovery was that the validity of a conclusion depends on its form, not its content. Substitute any terms for "men," "Socrates," and "mortal"—if the form is correct and the premises are true, the conclusion is ironclad. He examined all possible forms, selected the valid ones, and proved their correctness, while refuting invalid ones with counterexamples, using letters A, B, C instead of terms. This is the first formal system in history, with variables and rules.

Perhaps all of Aristotle's works on logic are an answer to Socrates' tragedy. A correct syllogism cannot be argued away. It cannot be refuted by eloquence, nor can it be voted down. The mob might declare Socrates guilty, but it cannot make a valid conclusion invalid: the truth of the conclusion is guaranteed by its form, not by opinion. Socrates sought definitions that would withstand the Sophists. Aristotle provided a form of inference that would withstand anyone. Logic became the solid foundation of truth that Socrates' death demanded.

The collection of Aristotle's logical treatises was later called the "Organon," meaning "instrument." For him, logic was not a science of some part of the world, but a tool used by all sciences. A ruler and a level, not a building. He abstracted the process of abstraction itself: not just separating form from matter, but studying how this separation works and writing down the rules.

Euclid: Knowledge from Pure Abstractions

Around 300 BCE, after Aristotle's death, Euclid in Alexandria demonstrated how knowledge could be built from pure abstractions. A point without dimension. A line without thickness. A plane without curvature. Such physical objects do not exist. Yet, from a few axioms about them, all of geometry can be derived. Euclid's "Elements" became the model for a formal system: take a few abstractions, define the rules, and derive everything else without looking at the world. This method would later be copied by Newton, Hilbert, and programming language creators.

What Remained

Aristotle died in 322 BCE, a year after Alexander the Great, whom he had tutored. The city-states would soon fall to Macedon and Rome, and the era of free city-states would end. But he managed to package Greek knowledge into a system that would outlive both the Greeks and the Romans. The Arabs would call him "The First Teacher," and medieval Europe would simply call him "The Philosopher," without a name, as the definitive one.

For our thread, something else is important. After Aristotle, humanity possessed a working tool: form separated from matter, inference dependent on form, abstractions constructible by rules. Two steps remained, which would take two thousand years. The first: to turn form into a symbol that could be manipulated like a number. The second: to make symbols executable physically, in hardware. The first step would be taken by Leibniz and Boole. The second by Turing and Shannon. But the foundation was laid by the physician from Stagira, who realized that thought has form, and form can be manipulated.

Part 2: From the Greeks to Shannon: Logic Becomes Computation

Aristotle's logic remained largely unchanged for nearly two thousand years. Not because it was flawed, but because it seemed complete. Kant, in the preface to the second edition of the "Critique of Pure Reason" (1787), wrote that since Aristotle, logic had made no progress and appeared to be finished. This was not praise, but a diagnosis. A discipline considered complete ceases to be developed.

Meanwhile, another river flowed nearby, and it didn't intersect with logic. Algebra.

Two Rivers

Algebra grew from a different tradition. Babylon, Diophantus, then al-Khwarizmi in Baghdad around 825 CE, in the House of Wisdom. From the title of his book, "al-jabr," came the word "algebra," and from his name, "algorithm." The Indians contributed zero as a full number and positional notation; the Arabs passed this on, and Fibonacci brought it to Europe in 1202.

By the 16th–17th centuries, algebra made its decisive move. Viète in 1591, and Descartes in 1637, began using letters not only for unknowns but also for coefficients: ax² + bx + c = 0. Now, the structure of an equation could be studied in general, without substituting specific numbers. Algebra became the algebra of algebra: operations on operations.

This resulted in two halves of the same idea. Aristotle's logic knew that reasoning has form. Algebra knew that form could be manipulated as a symbol, without thinking about the content each time. But no one thought to connect them. Logic was considered complete, and algebra dealt with numbers and equations. A chasm lay between them, and no one built a bridge across it.

Leibniz: The Dream of Computing Thought

The first to see the possibility was Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz. In 1666, at the age of twenty, he wrote "Dissertation on the Art of Combinations" and formulated a program for the next three hundred years.

The idea consisted of two parts. A universal symbolic language, *characteristica universalis*, in which any thought would be written as a formula. And a calculus of reasoning, *calculus ratiocinator*, in which disputes would be resolved by calculation. Leibniz proposed that philosophers, instead of arguing, should take up their pens and say, "Calculemus"—let us calculate.

Behind this dream lay metaphysics. Leibniz relied on the principle of sufficient reason (essentially a fourth law of logic): nothing happens without a sufficient reason; every fact and every true statement has a reason, even if we don't know it. The world is structured such that everything has a cause and an explanation. Leibniz believed that if everything has a reason, then everything is, in principle, derivable and computable.

A byproduct of his reflections was binary arithmetic. Leibniz worked with it from 1679, and in 1703, he published "Explanation of the Binary Arithmetic" in the memoirs of the Paris Academy. Only digits 0 and 1, addition 1+1=10, a multiplication table in one line. But Leibniz saw in this not a technique, but metaphysics: 1 as God, 0 as nothingness, all numbers from zero and one as the creation of the world from nothing.

Here, a turn occurred that Leibniz himself considered confirmation of his insight. He corresponded with the Jesuit Joachim Bouvet, a missionary in China, who sent him the hexagrams of the "I Ching" in Fu Xi's order. Sixty-four hexagrams, each composed of six solid or broken lines. Leibniz noticed: if a solid line is 1 and a broken line is 0, then the hexagrams represent binary numbers from 0 to 63 in precise order. The ancient Chinese, he concluded, knew the binary system millennia before him. Historians debate whether this was a coincidence, but for Leibniz, it was proof of the universality of his idea.

The binary system was born not from engineering, but from philosophy and mysticism. Just as Boolean algebra would later be born from logic, not electronics. Both lines would converge in the 20th century, and both began with thought, not machines.

Boole: Truth as Algebra

The bridge was built by George Boole. A self-taught man, the son of a shoemaker from Lincoln, a schoolteacher in Ireland, he only became a professor at thirty-four, and in the provincial town of Cork. In 1847, he published a slim book, "The Mathematical Analysis of Logic," and in 1854, his magnum opus, "An Investigation of the Laws of Thought."

His approach was brilliantly simple. Take algebra, but instead of numbers, let variables represent classes of things or propositions. Multiplication x·y means intersection: "people who are mortal." Addition x+y means union. 1−x means complement, "not people." 1 means everything, 0 means nothing.

Then, Aristotle's laws of logic become algebraic identities. The Law of Non-Contradiction, "nothing can be both A and not-A," is written as x·(1−x) = 0: the class of "people and non-people simultaneously" is empty. The Law of the Excluded Middle: x + (1−x) = 1. These are literally equations.

Syllogisms transform into solving equations. "All men are mortal" becomes M = M·P. "Socrates is a man" becomes S = S·M. Substituting: S = S·M = S·M·P = S·P, therefore S·(1−P) = 0, meaning "Socrates is mortal." The conclusion is reached by algebraic substitution, without any reasoning. Leibniz's "calculemus" worked.

But the most interesting part is how this algebra differs from numerical algebra. Boole discovered the law x² = x. For numbers, this is true only for 0 and 1. For classes, it's always true: "people who are people" is simply "people." Boole realized that his calculus was an algebra where variables take only the values 0 and 1. He himself called it the "algebra of zero and one."

The byproduct turned out to be more significant than the discovery itself. All of Aristotelian syllogistic logic turned out to be a tiny fragment of a much broader system. What had been considered the entirety of logic for two thousand years became a special case. Kant was wrong: logic was not finished; it hadn't even truly begun.

The Limit of the Dream and the Birth of Computation

Logic expanded to include quantifiers and relations, becoming expressive enough to formalize all of mathematics. This led to the program of logicism: reducing mathematics to pure logic. Russell and Whitehead scaled this up industrially in "Principia Mathematica" (1910–1913): three volumes to derive mathematics from logical axioms.

In the 1920s, David Hilbert formulated Leibniz's dream in its entirety: formalize all of mathematics, prove its consistency, and find an algorithm that could determine, for any statement, whether it is true or false. If such an algorithm exists, thought is fully mechanizable, and Leibniz was right.

In 1931, Kurt Gödel showed that if a sufficiently strong formal system is consistent and defined by explicit axioms and rules of inference, it is incomplete—it contains statements that cannot be proven or disproven within its own framework. Such a system also cannot prove its own consistency using its own means. This was a serious blow to logicism and Hilbert's program, but not a solution to the decision problem: an independent negative answer to it was obtained by Church and Turing in 1936.

But Gödel did not kill Leibniz's dream. He reformulated it.

In 1936, in his paper "On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem," Alan Turing asked not "what is provable?" but "what is computable?" And he gave a rigorous answer. The Turing machine—an abstract executor: an infinite tape, a head that reads and writes symbols, a table of rules that changes its state. A universal machine can execute any algorithm if it's written on the tape. The decision problem was solved negatively: there is no algorithm for all statements. But the concept of an algorithm is now rigorous. Computation is the manipulation of symbols according to rules. Not necessarily logical manipulation, any manipulation.

This was the shift. Logic ceased to be "rules of truth" and became the theory of computation. Aristotle's form, the formal system, the Turing machine. Thought equals symbol manipulation. This opened the door for statistics, but logic built the door. In parallel, Alonzo Church, who developed lambda calculus in the early 1930s, independently arrived at a negative solution to the decision problem in 1936. This line would later lead to functional programming and the idea of "program as data."

One thing remained: making symbols executable physically.

Shannon: Logic Finds Physical Form

In 1937, Claude Shannon, a twenty-one-year-old MIT master's student, demonstrated in his thesis that Boolean algebra is precisely the algebra of relay switches. Series connection of contacts means AND, parallel means OR, a normally closed contact means NOT. Any circuit can be written as a Boolean expression, simplified algebraically, and reassembled in simplified form. The engineer no longer has to guess; they solve an equation.

Shannon didn't build computers; he gave engineers a language. And in this language, Aristotle's Law of Non-Contradiction, x·(1−x)=0, became a physical fact: a circuit that is "on and off simultaneously" does not conduct current. Two thousand three hundred years from syllogism to relay. Logic found physical form.

Algebra + Logic

Logic provided the form: reasoning has structure, and it is independent of content. Algebra provided the technique: symbols can be manipulated according to rules, without thinking about their meaning. Boole united them: truth became an equation. Gödel showed the limits. Turing provided a rigorous concept of computation. Shannon embodied computation in switches.

Leibniz's dream, "let us calculate," gained a rigorous definition and found physical form. The remaining task was to scale this form and decide what it would compute.

Part 3: From Shannon to Large Language Models

Shannon gave logic physical form. Everything that followed is its scaling and the change in what it computes. The logic itself did not change. The physics changed, and then the subject of computation itself: from numbers to symbols, from symbols to knowledge, from knowledge to text that looks like thought.

From Relays to Billions of Transistors

Relays were electromechanical: a coil, a magnetic field, an armature, contacts. Apply current—it closes; remove it—a spring returns it. Switching took about ten milliseconds, involved mechanical wear, and filled rooms with hardware. The first computers were built with relays: Konrad Zuse built the Z3 in Berlin in 1941, with about 2600 relays, binary, programmable via punched tape; the Harvard Mark I in 1944, with about 3500 relays, decimal, fifteen meters long. Zuse was unaware of Shannon's work and arrived at binary logic himself, for engineering reasons: relays are naturally bistable.

Vacuum tubes brought speed. The triode, invented by Lee de Forest in 1906, controlled electron flow with voltage on a grid: microseconds instead of milliseconds, no moving parts. Tubes began to replace relays. The ENIAC in 1945 contained 17,468 tubes and performed 5000 additions per second, 3 orders of magnitude faster than the Mark I. But filaments burned out, tubes failed, the machine generated heat like an industrial heater, and weighed thirty tons.

In 1947, Bardeen and Brattain at Bell Labs created the point-contact transistor using germanium: current through one contact controlled current through another, without filaments or vacuum. In 1959, Kahng and Atalla developed the MOSFET, where the gate is insulated by an oxide and controls the channel via an electric field, not current. In 1963, Frank Wanlass proposed CMOS: complementary pairs of transistors where one is always off at rest, and almost no current flows. Switching energy instead of standby energy. This is why CMOS won and became the foundation of all modern electronics.

Then came integrated circuits (Kilby and Noyce, 1958–1959), the Intel 4004 microprocessor in 1971 with 2300 transistors, and growth to tens of billions of transistors on a chip. The logic is the same as Boole's in 1854 and Aristotle's in 350 BCE. The difference lies in scale, speed, and energy. And scale proved decisive.

A separate development was the GPU. Graphics Processing Units were created for rendering: millions of identical operations on pixels, in parallel. It turned out that matrix multiplication is structured similarly. Thousands of cores, each performing multiply-accumulate. Without GPUs, deep learning would not exist: not because the idea is new, but because computational power became accessible.

Program in Memory

In 1945, in the "First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC," John von Neumann described the stored-program architecture: programs and data reside in the same memory; the processor reads instructions, executes them, and reads the next. This is an interpreter embodied in hardware. And it means the machine can be reprogrammed without rebuilding it. Turing's universality became an engineering fact.

Programming has since built abstractions upon abstractions. A variable: a name instead of a memory address. A function: a name instead of a sequence of instructions. A type: a contract, "this is a number," you can do this with it, and how it's stored is not your concern. The language hides the machine.
