I sometimes write my articles with the help of ChatGPT. And I see nothing shameful in it. If we have a toothache, we go to the dentist, not treat it ourselves. A writer or journalist, before releasing a text, shows it to an editor, and they correct it.

Why, when it comes to artificial intelligence, do we suddenly start considering a person who uses it a ****** radish?

It's foolish to refuse a tool that can take on thousands of professional roles and significantly expand our capabilities.

The problem is entirely different. It's when we give AI too much authority instead of making decisions ourselves.

They quickly took advantage of it

Something similar happened to me that day.

I asked AI to help structure my chaotic thoughts. It seemed like a normal task. But then it suggested editing the title. Then we started discussing the tone, arguments, and sequence of presentation. At some point, I was already entrusting it not just with individual tasks, but with more and more decisions that I used to make myself.

And the most interesting thing is that I didn't notice it myself. It was convenient for me. AI offered options, evaluated them, found weaknesses, and recommended the best course of action. Why waste time searching on your own when you can just ask?

So, step by step, I transferred more and more authority to it. In the end, I realized that the result no longer fully reflected what I wanted to say, and I had to redo a lot.

The most dangerous thing is not even laziness

It seems to me there's one problem we talk about too little.

Artificial intelligence doesn't just save us time. It makes it surprisingly easy to give up the need to make decisions independently.

Imagine you're unsure how to act in a difficult situation. You need to choose a direction, take a risk, possibly make a mistake. You can spend an evening reflecting, listening to others' opinions, weighing arguments, and finally making a decision yourself.

Or you can open ChatGPT.

It will offer several options, break them down point by point, explain the advantages and disadvantages, and then, if asked, recommend the most reasonable option.

And you already feel relief.

Now you have an answer. Or at least something resembling an answer.

But did you experience what should happen during the process of making a difficult decision?

Did you learn to assess risk? Did you understand your own priorities? Did you check if you can defend your position when no one guarantees you the right outcome?

Or did you just get rid of the unpleasant state of uncertainty?

After all, we need difficult decisions not only for the result. In the process of making them, we learn to think, endure doubt, take responsibility for consequences, and trust our own judgment.

Every time we independently solve a complex problem, we gain not only the solution to that problem. We gain experience that will be useful next time.

But if, instead, we regularly delegate the solution to artificial intelligence, we may deprive ourselves of some of this experience.

Helplessness

In this study from 2025, 319 knowledge workers who frequently used AI in their work participated.

Scientists found a pattern: higher confidence in AI's ability to handle a specific task was associated with less active use of critical thinking. Confidence in one's own abilities, on the other hand, was associated with more active critical thinking.

It turns out that artificial intelligence doesn't necessarily make us think less. But if we rely on it too much, we may gradually stop seeking solutions independently and start mainly checking and choosing what it offers us.

Of course, this doesn't mean that using AI automatically leads to learned helplessness. The authors studied the link between confidence in AI and how people engage critical thinking when working with it, rather than observing changes in human abilities over several years.

It's interesting what will happen if we get used to the idea that in any difficult situation, someone else will offer us a solution?

Will we forget how to tolerate uncertainty, seek solutions independently, and trust our own abilities? After all, it's so tempting to shirk responsibility.

Let's fantasize a little

Imagine you've been working with artificial intelligence for several years. It helps you prepare presentations, write texts, analyze information, formulate proposals, and develop projects.

You become a very productive person. You accomplish more, complete tasks faster, and get high-quality results.

Now imagine that tomorrow you are invited to speak at a professional conference.

You are offered to talk about your work, explain your approach, and answer audience questions.

But there's one condition: you won't be able to open ChatGPT and ask it to formulate your points. You won't be able to delegate to it the task of preparing an answer to an uncomfortable question. You won't be able to ask it to find arguments.

You will be left alone with your knowledge on stage in front of people expecting your presentation.

And then a rather unpleasant truth might emerge: you've become excellent at getting results with the help of artificial intelligence, but you've forgotten how to produce some of them yourself.

You know how to use the tool, but how well do you master the profession itself?

Society is not ready yet

It seems to me that we are making one big mistake right now.

We are actively integrating artificial intelligence into everyday life, talking about its capabilities, and showing how it can save time and increase productivity.

But we hardly talk about how to properly build relationships with this technology.

People are taught to write prompts, get answers, generate images, and create presentations. But has anyone explained to them how not to lose their own decision-making ability?

How to distinguish help in thinking from replacement of thinking?

How to understand when you are no longer just using a tool, but starting to depend on it?

We need to learn not only how to use artificial intelligence but also how to maintain independence while working with it.

For example, I deliberately ask GPT to criticize my idea. I see where I disagree and stubbornly defend my position until an optimal solution is found that meets all criteria of validity, logic, and reasonableness, but not limited to them alone.

Before making a decision, it's useful to first try to find your own.

You can ask it to prepare a text, but you need to understand what exactly you intend to say and why.

And most importantly, you need to periodically check if you are capable of doing your job without it.

WALL-E

There is a wonderful Pixar cartoon "WALL-E." In it, people became so accustomed to machines doing everything for them that they practically stopped moving on their own. They travel in special chairs, eat through tubes, and receive entertainment from screens.

Machines made their lives incredibly convenient.

But along with convenience, people lost something much more important—the need to act independently.

And it seems to me that there is a similar danger in the relationship between humans and artificial intelligence.

We may not notice how we gradually transfer to it not only the execution of tasks but also the right to choose, evaluate, reason, and make decisions.

And one day, we may discover that without it, it's difficult for us to even start working.

At the same time, I am not suggesting abandoning artificial intelligence at all. It would be strange to voluntarily give up a tool that can so greatly expand our capabilities. I've already written about this here — why you shouldn't be afraid that AI will replace you.

I want us to learn to use it differently.

Not to stop thinking, but to think better.

Not to abandon our own position, but to test it.

Not to transfer our professional competence to someone else, but to become stronger in our profession.

After all, the most beautiful thing in the movie WALL-E happens when people first start to get up from their chairs on their own, walk, and interact with each other.

It turns out that doing something with your own hands is incredibly pleasant.

And perhaps, we should also close AI sometimes.

Not because it's bad.

But to make sure we can still do without it.

We have gained superpowers. But now we have to figure out how to use them and what price we are willing to pay for them.
