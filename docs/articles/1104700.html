I came across the article "2.1 Billion White Herons" about debunking research findings. Spoiler alert: AI was not involved, and all the research errors belong to humans. In short, in August 2026, the journal Science published an article stating that China's policy of expanding solar energy reduces bird diversity. Attentive readers noticed that the figure of 2.1 billion herons exceeded the world population of these birds by about a thousand times. They started re-checking the data—it turned out that in 2015, someone in Guangdong province entered the record: "white heron—2,147,483,647 individuals." This number itself is exactly equal to the maximum value of a 32-bit signed integer in computer science (2³¹ − 1). That is, most likely, it was a data entry error or a system glitch, not an actual bird count. The errors were noticed after publication: attentive readers re-checked the figures and research methodology and thoroughly debunked the results.

This sparked a wild thought: could AI have noticed all these inconsistencies before the article's publication and saved the unfortunate researchers and the scientific journal from scandal? It seems not. Perhaps an AI checker would have latched onto the figure of 2.1 billion white herons and pointed out that it is many times larger than the entire world population. But even then, much would depend on how the task was set.

This heron example struck a chord, creating a whole cloud of ideas and questions. I felt like pondering aloud.

In fact, such gross errors indicate that control points failed in the research process. Data was collected, analyzed, an article was written, and sent to the journal. At what stage should a check have occurred to ensure the initial numbers made any sense? And this is where the wild thought arose that AI can legitimately help not only with literature search, translation, and work planning. It can be used as a control tool at certain stages of research. It can check if the numbers add up, if the references exist, if the code is reproducible. But it cannot decide if the data is suitable for the method, if the variables are chosen correctly, and what should be considered "normal" in a given subject area.

Furthermore, this example clearly demonstrated that AI will not replace the researcher. It is a good tool that needs to be used correctly.

Given that we work closely with the academic community, we discuss the usefulness of AI quite often. And alongside it, the question of its legitimacy and ethics in education and science always arises. Where does author assistance end and the substitution of their work begin? Can a neural network be entrusted with checking a scientific text? And how to use such tools to ultimately produce higher-quality research, not just smoother texts?

From Herons to Control Points

First, let's clear up a common confusion. A scientific article is not "a beautiful presentation of results at the end of completed research." It is the result itself, brought to a state ready for independent peer review. Axiomatic.

And here, it seems, there is an interesting task for AI. If, of course, the process is organized correctly and integrated into the workflow as an additional verification tool.

We have already implemented some features in our tools for researchers and authors, and we are planning others, which allows us to fantasize about potential benefits. So, we have two tools that solve different tasks on the path from idea to finished article.

Domate: Check and Analyze

Domate is a system for detecting plagiarism and AI-generated text. In addition to verification results, it provides a detailed report on the bibliography and an AI assistant that helps understand found matches, AI text markers, and other identified errors.

The assistant is not just a tool for a brief summary of the report. You can work with it using ready-made presets to quickly start analyzing typical situations, or formulate your own queries, or switch to "agent" mode and instruct it to correct something in the report itself.

We recently held a practical webinar and analyzed various cases of working with the assistant, from independent verification of sources in the bibliography and processing technical matches to analyzing the reasons for highlighting a specific text fragment as generated—all this can be done "through its hands," and it greatly helps the author save time.

With the advent of the AI assistant, the report is no longer just a set of indicators that the author must decipher independently. It has effectively become a set of recommendations for further work.

Dotrace: An Editor with Built-in AI and a Researcher Training Environment

Dotrace is a system for preparing academic or scientific text, where an author can manage all work with the scientific document, LaTeX, and an AI assistant in a single space. Here, you can edit text using natural language commands, analyze individual fragments, get help with phrasing to give the text an academic tone, select and analyze literature relevance, etc.

Moreover, Dotrace preserves the history of interaction with AI. If necessary, a supervisor can see not only the final text but also how it was created: what tasks the author set for the assistant, what changes were made, and how the result was refined. This was fundamentally important for us when designing the product. In science and education, the use of AI must remain transparent. An author can delegate some routine operations to a neural network but must understand what they have received and be responsible for the content of their work.

The Dotrace system is used in some universities where students go through the process from topic selection and literature review to experiments, text preparation, review, and journal submission. In such a learning model, a scientific article becomes the result of consistent work on a project, where the student learns to search and analyze publications, formulate a task, choose a method, interpret results, and present conclusions. Dotrace becomes the working environment where AI can be applied at different stages of this process without deviating from the research itself.

Preserving the work history with AI in this sense is useful for the instructor not only for control. It allows them to understand where the student needed help, how they evaluated the proposed changes, what they corrected, and what decisions they made independently. The focus is on the development of research competencies.

We designed the system based on the principle "if you can't ban it, lead it." Here, AI can be used, and more importantly, one can learn to use it correctly, consciously, and responsibly. As loud as these words may sound now. Thus, Dotrace can be one of the tools for the rapid integration of AI into the educational process.

It turns out that Dotrace helps the author work on the text, while Domate checks it in several directions. Looking at the entire process, it becomes clear that those very control points can be added to assist the author.

You Just Don't Know How to Prepare Them, or Where AI is Truly Useful

This is where it gets interesting. For example, in the Dotrace system, the user interacts with AI via a prompt: open chat, ask a question, get an answer. But scientific work is a sequential process. Each stage has its own tasks and quality criteria. It seems that ready-made verification scenarios could be incorporated, which would, firstly, remind the author of the need for verification and, secondly, save time on writing such prompts. Yes, the idea is to create a proper set of presets and hints about their existence.

For example:

Bibliography Check. Do all sources exist? Do the authors, titles, and publication years match? Does the source actually support the thesis it is cited next to?

Arithmetic Control. Do the numbers in tables and text add up? Do percentages correspond to absolute values? Are there any anomalies that might indicate a data entry or processing error? Perhaps such a scenario would have helped notice our 2.1 billion herons in time.

Research Logic. Do the conclusions align with the stated objectives? Are all key assertions supported by arguments? Are there any leaps in reasoning or contradictions between sections?

Skeptical Reviewer. Try to find alternative explanations for the obtained results. What assumptions does the author make without sufficient grounds? What questions might a critically minded reviewer ask?

Reproducibility. Can the described experiment be replicated based on the provided data, code, and instructions? What parameters are missing? What steps require clarification?

Moreover, presets can be customizable. A researcher selects the subject area, type of work, methodology, and the specific journal's requirements, and then assembles their own set of checks. Universities or research labs could publish their sets of scenarios for students, thereby training them to verify their own work.

Of course, an AI assistant can do all this now, but the main idea is that presets will remind about the necessity of such an action and save the author time. This now seems like a quite feasible and not overly complex task.

From Presets to Industry Harnesses

This approach has a next level of development, namely adapting the AI assistant's work to different fields and data types. For example, when preparing a literature review, one can set up checks for the existence of sources and the correspondence of references to used citations. For experimental research, one can introduce checks for data in tables and the reproducibility of specified calculations. For a mathematical paper, one can check the consistency of formulas. It is clear that real-world examples can be much more complex than those presented here. But the idea is that a researcher or instructor could assemble their own sets of such checks and use or distribute them for specific disciplines or methodologies.

In AI system development, such a process is described by the term harness engineering. It sounds complex, but essentially, it is simply the design or description of the environment in which the neural network operates. A harness can describe what tools it uses, in what order it acts, whether there are limitations, and where the boundaries lie. In fact, the success and reliability of the model's operation depend on this, not on its "intelligence."

Returning to our task, we can go further in these fantasies of building specialized work scenarios for different disciplines. The system could consider the type of research and methodology, connect specialized databases and computational tools, apply the rules of a specific laboratory, maintain a log of checks, etc. For Dotrace, this direction is also interesting from an educational perspective. The instructor sets scenarios for their course, the lab adapts them to their research tasks, and the student, in the process, not only learns how an article is written but also learns to work with "control points" and how to check their own work.

It seems to be an interesting development track for a specialized product for the academic environment.

Responsibility Cannot Be Automated

One of the main questions in using AI in science and education is which decisions we are willing to delegate and which should remain with humans.

The story with 2.1 billion herons is a good reason to think not only about data quality but also about the structure of the research process itself. An error does not necessarily become a catastrophe at the moment of its appearance. It becomes critical when it passes through all stages of work and turns into a published conclusion that others begin to trust.

AI can help detect such an error earlier, and for this, clear control points are needed, which is interesting to work with. But delegating responsibility will still not be possible.
