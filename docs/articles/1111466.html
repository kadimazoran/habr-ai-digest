Project Costs

Next, you need to know what project costs are. I asked in my TG chat: it turns out many developers are unaware of what this is, so I'll provide the definition here.

Project costs are the costs of creating and supporting a project as a whole, including the labor costs of the entire team, equipment purchases, contractor services, overhead, and risk reserves.

All studies found by the author indicate that AI does not reduce overall project costs, and for complex, demanding tasks, it often increases them. There is a trend: the more modern the study, the more it leans towards this thesis. It would seem paradoxical: models feel smarter, yet studies increasingly point to no reduction in overall costs.

Why is this happening? Because we reduce development costs, but at the same time, there is a significant increase in testing and cybersecurity costs. In total, we either gain a few percent or lose significantly on complex, demanding tasks in established, serious systems.

Here is a list of studies:

  • Google RCT (Paradis et al., 2024): 96 engineers, complex enterprise task — −21% time, but the confidence interval is wide, and the authors themselves warn about transferability to other conditions. arXiv:2410.12944

  • Microsoft/Accenture/Fortune 100 (Cui et al., 2024): field experiments — +26% closed tasks, effect stronger for less experienced. SSRN

  • METR RCT (July 2025): 16 experienced OSS developers, 246 tasks in their own repositories — 19% slower with AI, although participants subjectively believed they had sped up by 20%. metr.org

  • Borg et al. (ICSME 2025/2026): 151 participants; during the feature writing phase, AI provided −30.7% median time, but during the subsequent evolution of this code by other developers, no difference in speed or quality was found. arXiv:2507.00788

  • DORA 2024: AI adoption growth of 25% was accompanied by −1.5% throughput and −7.2% delivery stability. Report · DORA 2025: AI is an "amplifier": returns depend on the organizational system, not the tools themselves. Report

  • METR, May 2026: survey of 349 technical specialists — self-reported gain of 1.4–2x, but the authors are clearly skeptical of this figure (self-report, selection bias). metr.org

Main Idea of the Article

Anyone following world events has noticed how drones have changed the course of combat operations. Old methods stopped working, and many countries gained the ability to assemble a cheap drone and, with a bit of luck, threaten anything within its range. Standard defense measures became obsolete; the world is only in the process of developing new countermeasures.

At the same time, if you are far enough from the world's troubled spots, you can feel safe.

Here come the "digital drones." Imagine: in any country in the world, even one without hot water, for a modest $30-50k, you can assemble hardware in a garage, find someone who can install an open AI model on it, set a target, and start terrorizing the entire digital space — from your home personal file server to government systems, including nuclear powers. This is already starting to happen, and the scale is already visible to the naked eye. Guardian: who and why is hacking supermarket refrigerators · METR Testimony in the US Senate on AI Agent Incidents

Digital terrorism is what awaits us in the very near future. Its price has fallen sharply, and this will add extra costs for cybersecurity and critical incidents. Even without AI, there were always unique individuals who set critical system passwords from 1 to 8, etc., and so on. Now such things will be detected much faster and easier, exploited, and lead to major problems.
