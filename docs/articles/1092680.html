Where Did These Measurements Come From?

It all started with Mycelium. That's what I called my spring endeavor. I read a lot of Prigozhin on dissipative structures, then papers where large populations of models assign roles to themselves and agree on a communication language, even when all models are identical. I wanted to create starting conditions where a swarm of weak models would itself grow into something smarter than any of them. Emergence, scientifically speaking.

I deliberately chose weak models. If the best model already solves the task, there's no room for the swarm to grow. The second reason is simpler: I have a GTX 1660 SUPER with 6 GB, and I needed to hold several models in memory at once and run them through thousands of calls. So, I assembled a pool: qwen2.5-coder-1.5b, qwen3-1.7b, llama-3.2-1b, gemma-3-1b, internvl3-2b, and smollm2-1.7b. Yes, these are models from previous years, and I've already been criticized on Reddit for them. I couldn't change them for this experiment: the models' answers on a whole list are taken from disk, from previous runs on the same tasks.

The swarm didn't take off. On 200 MBPP tasks, it solved 29% compared to 52% for a simple best-of-N, where models just get a few tries. Strict emergence, where the swarm solves what no single model could solve individually, yielded zero in two proper checks. Once, something similar flickered when models filled different fields of a single answer, but I couldn't develop it further.

The code for all experiments was written by AI agents; I set the tasks and nitpicked the numbers. In parallel, I'm tinkering with a fork of llama.cpp so that larger models can run on the same 6 GB card, which was the subject of my previous article. Below it, people asked if there's any use for small models besides tokens per second. This article is precisely about quality, about what survived from the swarm.

The Kahn Firm

Albert Kahn was an American architect who built factories for Ford. From 1929 to 1932, his firm designed about five hundred factories for the USSR, including the Stalingrad Tractor Plant, and thousands of our engineers passed through its Moscow bureau. They say Soviet design institutes grew out of this bureau. I can't judge how true this is, but I recognize the organizational structure immediately; I live in it every day.

It's structured like this: The General Project Manager (GIP) divides the object into sections: thermal mechanics, electrical, construction, automation, and so on; each section is managed by its own department. A completed section is checked within the department, this is VDP (Intra-Disciplinary Check). Then, the sections are reconciled with each other at the interfaces, this is MDP (Inter-Disciplinary Check): I have a pump, the builders have a foundation for it, and they must match. Disputed points are taken to the chief specialist. Finally, norm control checks the formatting, and only after that are the albums assembled and sent to the client.

No one holds the entire station in their head; it doesn't fit. A small model given eighteen entries and asked to find the relevant ones is, in my opinion, in the same position as an engineer handed the entire station at once. So, I wanted to do with the models what the GIP does with us.

Professional deformation, yes.

The Task

The testbed is simple and synthetic. Each task has 18 transactions, one line per transaction. The models need to find all transactions with a given category and region. There are two to five matching ones, and nearby, deliberately placed similar ones: with the correct category but wrong region, and vice versa, two to four of each. Find the IDs of all transactions where category = "software-licensing" AND region = "LATAM".
Output ONLY the list of matching IDs separated by commas, with no explanations. If there are no matches, output the word NONE.

Then, based on the found lines, you need to calculate the sum, count, or maximum and compare it with a threshold, answering YES or NO. I didn't trust the models with arithmetic; the code did that. The model was required to do one thing: find the lines. The task was counted only if the model provided the exact correct set, with no extras and no omissions.

It's a toy task, I understand. But it contains the same challenge as any batch of documents fed into a chat entirely: the relevant information is mixed with similar-looking information.

The Further You Go, The Worse It Gets

The first thing I did, without running anything new, was to recalculate the old model answers by position. On 120 tasks, with all six models combined, the first entry in the list was found 61% of the time, and the last, eighteenth, 21% of the time. The curve drops almost linearly from the first line to the last. I couldn't replicate the famous "Lost in the Middle" phenomenon (Liu et al., 2023).

At first, I thought the model just said "no" more often towards the end of the list. That's what I wrote in the report and later in a Reddit post. One reader suggested checking this on the same data, and they were right to do so. The model doesn't become stingier towards the end: in both the first and last thirds of the list, it marks about a fifth of the entries (0.21 and 0.19). It's just that at the end, it marks the wrong ones more often. It finds fewer correct ones, 0.30 versus 0.50, and there are more false positives among the incorrect ones, 0.16 versus 0.14. Simply put, it distinguishes similar lines worse. The correction is now in the report; I left the old conclusion next to it to show where I made a mistake.

Cut into Sections

Next, everything according to Kahn. The eighteen entries are cut into three blocks of six consecutive ones, maintaining the order within each block. A script does the cutting; I don't let the model near it. In another experiment, I let a small model devise the task breakdown into steps itself. A quarter of the plans didn't even parse as JSON, and only one out of a hundred tasks reached the correct answer. It's no GIP.

Each model reads each block separately. Then VDP (Intra-Disciplinary Check) is performed: if a model names an ID that is not in its block, it's a fabrication, and the code discards such an answer. Five fabrications were collected during the run. A vote for an entry is counted only from the block where it resides. I recorded the thresholds for considering an experiment successful in the protocol before the run, to avoid the temptation to move them later. A total of 720 calls, ten minutes on the same GTX 1660 SUPER.

The weaker the model, the greater the increase. The coder improved fourfold and, with segmentation, almost caught up to the leader without it: 0.700 versus 0.725 for qwen3-1.7b on the full list. Essentially, this is why Kahn divided the work: with proper division, average performers improve, and their work starts to add up. Gemma and smollm couldn't handle the task at all; segmentation didn't help them.

Context segmentation, of course, wasn't invented by me; it's done one way or another by everyone who works with long documents. I was interested in how much it benefits weak models and what would remain of the swarm if compared properly.

Where I Almost Opened Champagne

The main goal was still emergence, and I also recorded the threshold for it in advance. If, in at least three out of forty tasks, the assembled answer is correct, and no single model could handle it, then there's an effect. It turned out to be 34 out of 40. I was already mentally opening champagne, and then I looked at what I was comparing. On one side, the entire pipeline: blocks, checks, voting, assembly. On the other, a single model given all 18 entries at once. The conditions are different; such a comparison means nothing.

If you run the same pipeline with one best model, qwen3-1.7b, it yields 0.850. All six together yield 0.900. The oracle "at least one of the six succeeded" yields 0.975. Strict emergence is zero: the assembled answer never went beyond what at least one model could already do. The swarm added five points, and that's it.

The number 34 remains in the results file along with the erroneous verdict. I didn't delete it; let the flaw in the wording be visible.

Run details for the meticulous

Models in GGUF: qwen2.5-coder-1.5b-instruct Q4_0, llama-3.2-1b-instruct Q4_0, qwen3-1.7b Q4_0, internvl3-2b Q4_K_M, gemma-3-1b-it Q5_K_S, smollm2-1.7b-instruct Q4_K_M. Card: GTX 1660 SUPER 6 GB.

40 test tasks, 3 blocks, 6 models: 720 calls, 614 seconds. Answers on the full list are taken from disk, from previous experiments on the same 40 tasks.

The model's answer code is adjusted to the nearest permissible set: the correct answer always consists of all entries with the same category-region pair. This was calculated in both variants, for the full list and for the blocks.

Thresholds from the protocol: recall at the end of the list not lower than 0.45 (result 0.57), gap not more than 0.12 (result 0.02), exact set not lower than 0.85 (result 0.90). Emergence in at least three tasks: according to the initial formulation, it was 34; according to the corrected one, 0.

The positional decay was first calculated on old answers, 120 tasks, without new calls. The correction about the proportion of marks was checked similarly, on the same data. Both scripts are located next to the report.

What to Do With This

If you feed a small model a batch of documents or a long list, be it entries, files, or agent tools, cut it into pieces. I used blocks of six. I took the size from the recall curve; the first six positions were found best. I didn't try three or nine, so I don't know if it's optimal. A script should do the cutting. Check the answer with code: an ID not present in the piece is fabricated, and this is caught with a single line.

In terms of tokens, segmentation is almost free: three pieces of one-third each is the same volume plus three prompt headers. Three times more calls, but they are short. And a swarm of six models on top of that costs six times more and gives five points. I don't like this trade-off.

What I Didn't Measure

The models are only 1-2B; I have 6 GB of VRAM. I haven't tested this on larger models. From the table, it's clear that the stronger the model, the smaller the increase: for qwen3-1.7b, it's already +0.125. For 7B and above, I would expect even less, but this is a guess; there are no measurements. Forty tasks, one task family, one segmentation.

The final YES/NO answer matched in all 40 tasks, but this is a soft metric: you can guess "yes" even with an incorrect set. Therefore, I count based on the exact set, where it's 0.900.

And a question for those building multi-agent systems: have you compared your swarm to the best single agent run through the same pipeline? The difference for me was five points. I wonder if anyone has achieved more than just noise.
