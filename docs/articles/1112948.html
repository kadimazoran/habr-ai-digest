I recently needed a simple plastic spacer to mount a fan for my CPU cooler. Normally, I'd open Tinkercad or another CAD editor, spend 5-10 minutes measuring and modeling, and then send the part to the 3D printer.

But I got curious: could ChatGPT on the GPT-6 model do the same based on a simple technical specification?

The result was mixed. We got a working part, but it took about three hours of interaction with the AI instead of 5-10 minutes. Moreover, most of the time wasn't spent on modeling at all.

A Ten-Minute Task

I have a dual-tower CPU cooler, the be quiet! Dark Rock Pro 4 (BK022). When replacing the stock fan with an Arctic P12 PWM PST, I encountered a minor issue. The original fan's wire clip mounting points are closer to the middle of the housing's thickness. The Arctic fan's mounting holes are on the outer surfaces of the frame. Because of this, the stock clips don't properly secure the new fan. The solution is quite obvious: create small spacers that fit inside the Arctic fan's mounting brackets, sit on the existing stiffening ribs, and are screwed in from below with the stock screws.

No complex mechanisms, new mounts, or radiator modifications were needed.

What I Gave ChatGPT

In my initial request, I described both fans, the problem with the wire clips, and suggested installing a spacer on the inner side of the mount, secured with the stock screws. I also asked ChatGPT to find the technical documentation for both fans independently to use actual dimensions for the design. For clarity, I took an image of the Arctic P12 from a review (Fig. 1-A), marked the spacer's installation location, and then drew a rough sketch of the required shape in Paint (Fig. 1-B). I asked it to create a 2D drawing first and only proceed to a 3D model after approval.

ChatGPT had access to the manufacturers' documentation. Specifically, the official Arctic P12 drawing contains the fan's dimensions, mounting hole locations, and their diameter. Later, I also measured the thickness of the internal stiffening rib with calipers – 2.3 mm. Thus, the AI was tasked not with inventing a mounting method, but with implementing the already proposed design, using technical documentation and clarifying missing dimensions.

When Everything Went Wrong

The first surprise was that ChatGPT started designing something completely different. First, there were adapters between fans of different sizes, then external brackets, additional clips, and quite complex structures. I explained several times that the spacer should be inside the mounting bracket, where there was already a suitable stiffening rib.

Another problem arose with the images. ChatGPT began to rely on an image of the mounting bracket taken at an angle, transferring perspective distortions into the drawing. Meanwhile, my hand-drawn sketch, which showed the fundamental geometry, was not used sufficiently.

I had to remind it separately:

Don't look at the screenshot, look at the documentation!!! You have the drawings!!!

Even after that, errors persisted. For example, the cutout for the stiffening rib was offset from the center of the mounting hole. And most interestingly, the AI repeatedly stated that it now fully understood the task. The next drawing sometimes showed the opposite.

Why Didn't the AI Ask the Right Questions?

In my opinion, this is the most interesting part of the experiment. The technical specification did not contain all the dimensions needed for manufacturing. For example, the exact dimensions of the internal cavity of the Arctic fan were not specified in the factory drawing. This is normal. In real engineering work, some dimensions have to be clarified by measurements. But instead of determining what data was missing, ChatGPT started suggesting ready-made designs. It could have immediately clarified the position of the support surface, the height of the rib, the dimensions of the free space, or the method for determining the required spacer thickness. Alternatively, it could have built a preliminary parametric outline and explicitly marked the unknown dimensions. Instead, we spent a significant amount of time correcting geometry that did not meet the requirements from the start.

Comment from GPT-6: Why ChatGPT didn't clarify requirements

Model explanation after the experiment.

Based on the nature of my responses, it can be inferred that I chose a familiar design type too early – an adapter between fans – and then tried to adapt it to the user's clarifications. Instead of verifying the initial requirements, I often made local changes to an already incorrect model.

At the same time, I cannot reliably reconstruct the internal reasons for each of my decisions. This conclusion is based on the observed course of the dialogue.

It would have been better to first separate known dimensions from assumptions, build a simple geometry based on the drawing, and request only the missing measurements.

From Pretty Pictures to a Real Model

The visualization method became a separate problem. ChatGPT initially generated images that looked like engineering drawings. They appeared convincing: dimensions, arrows, sections, 3D views. But external convincingness did not equate to geometric accuracy. Dimensions sometimes contradicted each other, holes were misplaced, and parts had elements that were not required at all. Progress began after transitioning from generating pictures to building an actual 2D contour and parametric 3D geometry.

But even here, another error was discovered.

Where Did the 7.5 mm Come From?

ChatGPT proposed a spacer height of 7.5 mm. The calculation was based on the Arctic P12 housing thickness – 25 mm. The AI subtracted two mounting parts of 5 mm each, got an internal gap of 15 mm, and divided it by two. Everything was mathematically correct. However, half of the Arctic's internal gap does not determine the required height for the be quiet! clip to engage. According to my measurements, 11 mm was needed. This is the height I set when preparing for printing.

Comment from GPT-6: Why ChatGPT made a mistake with the height

I calculated a geometrically existing value, but it did not correspond to the functional requirement.

It was necessary to determine the distance from the support surface of the spacer to the engagement point of the wire clip.

Instead, I used the midpoint of the Arctic's internal gap and got 7.5 mm.

This was an error in choosing the calculation base, not in arithmetic.

The actual measurement showed 11 mm, which was later confirmed when the fan was installed.

Second Iteration and Result

After corrections, increasing the spacer's area, and a second modeling iteration, I printed the parts on a 3D printer using PetG plastic and installed the fan in the computer. Everything fit. The spacers seated on the stiffening ribs, were secured with the stock screws, and the original be quiet! wire clips could be used without modification.

However, a minor geometric flaw remained. The rounding of the outer corner of the spacer did not fully follow the contour of the Arctic P12. Because of this, the part protrudes from the fan frame by approximately 1-2 mm. This did not affect the mounting's functionality, so I didn't fix the model. But this is another example of the AI not fully utilizing the available geometric information.

The finished model, as well as the STL, OpenSCAD, and SVG files, are published here:

For my installation, a height of 11 mm was used. The published STL retains the original height of 7.5 mm, so when reprinting, you only need to increase the size along the Z-axis to 11 mm.

What the Experiment Showed

The entire process with ChatGPT took approximately three hours of active interaction, excluding printing and physical fitting. Manually, in a familiar CAD editor, I estimate the same task to take about 5-10 minutes, including measurements with calipers.

It's important to understand the scale of the task here. The spacer is a maximally simple geometric part. Essentially, a flat contour with a hole and a cutout for a stiffening rib, requiring a specific height. No complex surfaces, moving joints, assembly mechanisms, or strength calculations. And even for such a simple task, the AI required about three hours of interaction and constant corrections from a human. What would have happened if a truly complex part needed to be designed, requiring consideration of many interfaces, tolerances, and design constraints?

This experiment doesn't provide an answer. But after this experience, I certainly wouldn't trust such a result without serious engineering verification. The main problem wasn't the complexity of the geometry. Most of the time was spent getting the AI to correctly understand the already proposed solution.

This doesn't mean AI is incapable of 3D modeling. But the experiment clearly showed the difference between the ability to create an STL file and the ability to effectively solve an engineering problem.

How Will Other AIs Perform?

This experiment was conducted only with ChatGPT on the GPT-6 model in November 2026. I did not test other models or specialized CAD agents on this task. Therefore, it would be interesting to compare the results.

Will Claude, Gemini, Grok, or another AI agent handle the same task faster? Will it ask clarifying questions? Will it be able to correctly use factory drawings and build a model without numerous corrections?

If anyone is interested in repeating the experiment, I suggest using the same initial conditions: the original technical specification, manufacturers' documentation, and photos of the mounting area. All the models and results I created during the experiment can be used, modified, and applied for any purpose, including commercial use, as well as for reproducing this case and conducting your own comparisons.
