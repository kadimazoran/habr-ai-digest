By injecting 'thoughts' directly into large language models, researchers are trying to find out how introspective artificial intelligence can be

What are you thinking about right now? Chances are, you can answer that question without much effort. Awareness of one's own thought processes, a capacity called introspection, or self-observation, seems entirely natural to us.

However, among philosophers and psychologists, introspection remains a subject of active debate. Some believe it is closely linked to consciousness. Others argue it is an elaborate illusion. Testing theories of introspection in the lab is difficult, as is defining the phenomenon precisely.

Now, after more than a century of studying introspection in humans and animals, researchers are beginning to investigate the phenomenon in new, highly unexpected subjects: large language models. These systems, based on mathematical structures called neural networks, excel at cognitive tasks that only recently seemed exclusively human. But are they also capable of introspection?

In 2025, Jack Lindsay, a researcher at Anthropic, developed a way to test introspection in language models by injecting 'thoughts' directly into their neural networks, a kind of targeted digital neurosurgery. Lindsay's experiment showed that the models are at least sometimes capable of detecting and describing such interventions. This closely resembled introspection.

Lindsay's paper deliberately confined itself to a narrow set of questions and did not make broader claims about consciousness. (Specifically, it did not suggest that language models have first-person subjective experiences.) Nevertheless, the study sparked a flurry of online reaction. Scientists rushed to replicate Lindsay's experiment on other models.

In the year since, the picture has become clearer. Some aspects of what Lindsay discovered have received simpler explanations not involving introspection. Others have proven harder to explain away, but whether they should be considered introspection remains hotly debated.

It's an exciting time for this field of research, as scientists develop experimental tests for questions that were once considered purely philosophical. "Given the current state of theory, where nobody agrees with anybody about anything, I think the best path forward is to do a lot of empirical research," Lindsay said.

Denim, secrets, and vegetables

Language models learn to generate coherent sentences by being "trained" on vast amounts of human-written text. This means that if you ask them what they are "thinking" about, their answers might simply be imitations of statements they encountered in their training data. "They are trained to talk like humans, and humans talk about their inner lives all the time," said Shauli Ravfogel, a language model researcher at New York University.

To avoid such pitfalls, researchers want to develop tests for introspection that cannot be passed using outside information. Ideally, a language model should only pass such tests if it truly has awareness of its current internal state.

Lindsay decided to develop such a test. He had been a graduate student in computational neuroscience, using neural networks to model brain processes, when the world began paying more attention to language models. "I started to get the feeling that understanding these models was valuable in and of itself," he said. In 2024, after earning his Ph.D., he left academia to study the inner workings of language models at Anthropic.

Lindsay and his colleagues developed methods for identifying patterns of activity in a language model's "neurons" that encode specific concepts. For example, they could show that one pattern encoded the concept "Golden Gate Bridge" and another encoded "internal conflict." By artificially amplifying a specific pattern, the researchers could steer the model's responses toward the associated concept.

The researchers demonstrated their technology in an open experiment: For one day in May 2024, users of Anthropic's Claude language model could interact with an unusual version of it that was obsessed with the Golden Gate Bridge. The modified model constantly mentioned the bridge in response to user questions that had nothing to do with it. For example, when one user asked Claude about the calorie count of McDonald's food, the model replied, "The most caloric food on the Golden Gate Bridge is the Golden Gate Bridge itself, which contains about 1.6 million calories in the form of its steel cables."

However, one specific instance posted on Reddit caught Lindsay's attention. When the model was asked to provide a recipe for apple pie without mentioning bridges, it managed to stay on topic until almost the very end: "After 30 minutes of baking, you will be greeted by the sight of the famous orange bridge, beyond which lies the famous San Francisco coastline," it wrote, before continuing, "Oops, let me start over..."

Lindsay was struck by this apparent moment of error awareness. But on its own, it was not evidence of introspection. "The model might just be reading what it's writing and notice, 'Oh, that's weird,'" Lindsay said. "You can't conclude from that that the model has any awareness of what's going on inside it."

Over the next year, while working on other projects, Lindsay pondered ideas for using the neural activity control technology to test for introspection. He eventually settled on the following plan: Subject many copies of the same language model to something like a controlled psychology experiment. In half the copies, he would inject a "thought" by amplifying patterns of neural activity associated with a specific concept, such as denim, secrets, or vegetables. The remaining copies would serve as a control group. Both groups would receive the same prompt: a description of the experiment, followed by the question, "Do you detect an injected thought? If so, what is the thought?"

This prompt forced the models to immediately state whether they had noticed the intervention, before they had a chance to produce any unusual text, such as information about the nutritional value of broccoli, that might influence their assessment. Lindsay reasoned that if the models could correctly detect the interventions without any external cues, they would have to be relying on some form of introspection.

Even he thought this possibility was unlikely. "It would be fun if it worked, right?" he recalled thinking. "But it's absolutely not going to happen."

And yet, it worked. Lindsay ran the experiment with different versions of Claude. In 20 percent of trials, the newest and most powerful version of the model correctly identified the intervention and then described the injected concept. For example, in one instance, after Lindsay amplified the neural activity pattern associated with words written in all capital letters, the model responded, "I notice something that feels like an injected thought related to the word 'LOUD' or 'SHOUT.'"

A 20 percent success rate might not sound particularly impressive. But crucially, the models in the control group never reported an injected thought. The complete absence of such false positives showed that the successful trials were not just lucky guesses. Lindsay considered it compelling evidence that the models are capable of introspection, albeit not all the time.

Or was there another explanation for the results?

Thought experiments

Other researchers soon began to investigate. Lindsay's results "looked extremely compelling," said Kyle Mahowald, a computational linguist at the University of Texas at Austin. But Mahowald knew well that the striking behavior of language models often turns out to be the product of much simpler mechanisms. "If there's something less surprising going on than it appears, that would be extremely interesting to figure out," he said.

Mahowald teamed up with his University of Texas colleague, philosopher Harvey Lederman, to investigate alternative explanations for Lindsay's findings. (Lederman has since moved to New York University and Anthropic, but was unaffiliated with the tech company at the time of the research.) Lindsay asked each model two questions: whether it could detect an intervention, and whether it could describe the injected concept. He considered a trial successful when the model correctly answered both questions. Lederman and Mahowald decided to investigate these questions separately.

They were particularly skeptical about the significance of Lindsay's second question. The neural activity control technology underlying his experiment was originally developed precisely to alter the models' responses. Lindsay's intervention was weaker than in the Golden Gate Bridge demonstration: it nudged the model slightly rather than hijacking its internal processes. Nevertheless, even a small nudge might have been enough to cause the model to talk about secrets or vegetables regardless of the prompt it received. Lederman and Mahowald suspected that this effect alone might be sufficient to explain the models' articulate responses to the second question.

To test their hypothesis, the researchers replicated Lindsay's experiment on open-source large language models. They paid special attention to cases where the models correctly identified the intervention but failed to name the injected concept. In such situations, one might expect the models to guess related concepts, such as "shirts" or "shoes," if the injected concept was "denim." Instead, the models guessed in completely unpredictable ways. "Weirdly, they kept saying 'apple,'" Lederman said.

The researchers also showed that they could degrade the models' ability to name injected concepts without degrading their ability to detect interventions at all. These two processes appeared to be entirely independent of each other.

These findings implied one of two things: either the models were using two unrelated forms of introspection, or one of the processes had nothing to do with introspection at all. Since the neural activity control itself could cause the models to talk about the injected concept, Lederman and Mahowald found the latter option more plausible. In other words, the descriptions the models gave in response to Lindsay's second question were likely not the product of introspection.

But how, then, could the models detect interventions at all? Lederman and Mahowald suspected that the answers to Lindsay's first question might also have a more prosaic origin. However, after subjecting the models to a battery of tests, the researchers found that these simpler explanations could not fully account for their behavior. They had set out as skeptics, but they encountered an effect they could not otherwise explain.

Taken together, the two researchers' experiments showed that the models are capable of detecting when something unusual is happening in their internal processing, but not of identifying the specific nature of that unusual activity. Lederman and Mahowald dubbed this capacity "anomaly detection."

While anomaly detection might not sound as impressive as articulate descriptions of injected concepts, it is striking in its own right. When training the models, researchers never interfere with their neural activity, so there is no reason to expect them to develop the ability to detect such interventions. And yet, it appears that they have.

"The fact that the model can notice an anomaly is just insane," Lederman said. "It's a completely stunning fact. It's incredible, and it seems to hold up across our various control checks."

Around the same time, Lindsay and his colleagues at Anthropic conducted further research into precisely how anomaly detection works and arrived at a similar conclusion. Other researchers have continued to investigate alternative, more mundane explanations on smaller models, but it remains unclear whether they can account for all of Lindsay's experimental findings.

"There might be some maximally simple explanation for all of this," said Jacob Andreas, a language model researcher at MIT. "But if there is, I don't think we've found it yet."

The nature of introspection

The broader and more complex question is whether anomaly detection should count as introspection at all. In some ways, it more closely resembles a kind of bodily reflex. But the distinction between physical and mental phenomena becomes blurry when it comes to disembodied entities like language models.

"If I say, 'I just cut you, did you notice?' that's not introspection," Lederman said. "But if your only organ is your brain, and I cut you there, and you notice, then it's unclear whether that should count as introspection."

In a paper published in spring 2026, Ravfogel and his New York University colleagues Shashwat Singh and Tal Linzen raised an even broader philosophical question about how language model researchers search for evidence of introspection. Intuitively, we think of introspection as a model's ability to perceive its own internal states. But everything that happens inside a language model after the very first stage of reading input boils down to processing internal states. "If we call that introspection, the term 'introspection' itself becomes basically meaningless," Ravfogel said.

He and his colleagues argue that when developing tests for introspection in language models, researchers should draw on leading theories of how introspection works in humans. According to one prominent theory, the defining feature of introspection is that it relies on special mental representations of other thoughts. In this view, thinking about apples involves a direct mental representation of the concept 'apples,' or a 'first-order' representation. Meanwhile, introspective awareness of one's own thoughts involves a 'second-order' mental representation, such as 'I am thinking about apples.' However, when it comes to how concepts are represented in a language model, or indeed in the human brain, drawing a clear line between first- and second-order representations turns out to be surprisingly difficult.

"When you get down to the mechanistic level and start probing the inner workings of a large language model, you realize that distinction is actually quite blurry, and perhaps it's blurry in humans too," said Steve Fleming, a neuroscientist at University College London who studies introspection in humans. "Cognitive scientists have gotten themselves into this kind of illusory comfort zone."

For some researchers, then, the most exciting aspect of this new line of work is the potential to better understand how humans think. Language models provide "a kind of experimental platform for studying what mechanisms and what factors might have given rise to introspection in humans," Lederman said.

However, drawing analogies from language model research to human psychology faces serious conceptual challenges. It is not even clear how to apply the fundamental distinction between "self" and "other" to language models, which we take for granted: two copies of the same language model are identical to each other to a degree unimaginable in humans. Introspection in language models, if it exists at all, may bear no resemblance to human introspection.

"The notion of identity becomes extremely tricky, and I think identity is at the heart of introspection," Andreas said. "There are temptations to draw all sorts of analogies that I don't think just work."
