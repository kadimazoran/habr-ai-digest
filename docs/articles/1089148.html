Language model API prices are published per million tokens, and models are usually compared by this figure. But each model has its own "token." I ran the same Russian text through Claude Opus 5 and GPT-5.5: Opus 5 counted 3.9 times more tokens in it. This means that at the same price per token, processing Russian text on Opus 5 will cost almost four times more than on GPT-5.5 — and this is independent of who you buy access from.

It's all about the tokenizer — an algorithm that cuts text into pieces-tokens before feeding it to the model. Each model has its own: some cut Russian text into large chunks, others almost letter by letter. Therefore, the price per token by itself says nothing about how much you will pay for Russian text.

I ran the same Russian text through both models. Opus 5 counted 3.9 times more tokens in it than GPT-5.5. This means that at the same price per token, the bill for a Russian-language product on Opus 5 will be almost four times higher, and this is independent of who you buy access from. There is no error here, it's just that different models have different tokenizers. Each model has its own "token," and comparing models by price per token without adjusting for language is not possible.

Below is a measurement of 11 tokenizers, which represent GPT, Claude, Gemini, DeepSeek, Qwen, GLM, Kimi, Grok, GigaChat, and YandexGPT. The methodology, raw numbers, recalculation into rubles per thousand characters, and code to repeat the measurement on your own texts.

TL;DR

  • How many characters of Russian text fit into one token depends on the model by a factor of five: from ~1 for Claude Opus 5 to ~5 for YandexGPT and GigaChat.

  • For GPT-5.x/6, Gemini, Grok, and GLM, Russian text "weighs" 18–23% more than English text with the same meaning. For DeepSeek — 38% more, for Qwen — 52% more, for Kimi — 86% more. For Claude Opus 5 — almost three times more.

  • For GigaChat and YandexGPT, it's the opposite: Russian text takes up fewer tokens than its English translation.

  • When comparing models for a Russian-language product, you should not use the price per 1M tokens, but the price per 1,000 characters (or per typical request) on your own texts.

  • Not all aggregators return the actual usage: for one of the tested Claude models, it matched the OpenAI tokenizer count exactly.

Open Tokenizers — Directly

For models with open weights, the tokenizer can be downloaded and tokens counted without any API:

  • OpenAI — tiktoken, encodings o200k_base (GPT-4o and newer, including GPT-5.x and GPT-6) and cl100k_base (GPT-4, GPT-3.5) for comparison;

  • GigaChat 3 — tokenizer.json from ai-sage/GigaChat3-10B-A1.8B;

  • YandexGPT 5 Lite — SentencePiece tokenizer.model from yandex/YandexGPT-5-Lite-8B-instruct;

  • DeepSeek V3 and Qwen 3 — tokenizer.json from official repositories on Hugging Face.

Closed Models — via API and Usage Difference

For Claude, Gemini, Grok, Kimi, and GLM, the tokenizer is not available, so the usage.prompt_tokens field in the API response remains. The problem is that this number includes overhead: chat template, and for some gateways, their own system prompt. In one of the tested gateways, a five-word query resulted in 1,356 input tokens, while in another, it was 3,346.

Therefore, I calculated the difference between two requests:

tokens(text) = usage("Respond in one word: ok.\n\n" + text) - usage("Respond in one word: ok.")

All overhead is the same in both requests and cancels out upon subtraction. The model's response doesn't matter, max_tokens=16, so the entire measurement cost pennies.

Method verification: for DeepSeek V4 Flash via API, the difference yielded exactly the same numbers as the local DeepSeek V3 tokenizer, and for GPT-6 Sol and GPT-5.6 Luna — exactly the numbers from o200k_base. This means subtraction honestly removes overhead tokens.

What can be seen from the table:

English is almost the same for everyone. 5.0–5.1 characters per token for ten out of eleven models. Tokenizer dictionaries were mainly trained on English, and for it, all came to roughly the same result.

Russian differs by five times. Models that were developed for Russian tokenize it best: YandexGPT and GigaChat. For them, Russian text is even shorter than its English translation. Models with large multilingual vocabularies (GPT, Gemini, Grok, GLM) are 18–23% worse than English. Chinese models with a smaller proportion of Cyrillic in their vocabulary range from 38% (DeepSeek) to 86% (Kimi).

GPT-4 → GPT-4o — a big step for Russian. The transition from cl100k to o200k reduced the number of tokens for Russian by almost half: 2.29 → 4.01 characters per token. English remained unchanged. If you calculated your budget for Russian based on experience with GPT-4, you significantly overestimated it.

Claude Opus 5 — a separate story. About one character per token in Russian, meaning almost every letter is a separate token. And even in English, it's 3.25 characters per token compared to five for others. The difference is smaller for code, only twofold: Latin letters and Python keywords are tokenized as usual, only Russian comments and docstrings become more expensive.

An important caveat about prices. The median is not the price you will pay. The same model costs differently across various services, sometimes by orders of magnitude: as of 27.09, 1M input tokens for Opus 5 cost ₽28.5 from the cheapest service and ₽549 by median, GPT-5.5 — ₽14.25 and ₽552. Therefore, the rubles in the table are a benchmark for comparing models among themselves using a single rule. For your budget, substitute the price from your provider or the official developer tariff. The tokenizer multiplier, i.e., how many tokens your Russian text will occupy, will remain the same.

Three conclusions worth the calculation.

  1. Opus 5 and GPT-5.5 have the same price list, but on Russian text, Opus 5 is 3.9 times more expensive (₽0.53 vs ₽0.14 per thousand characters). This figure is purely the tokenizer effect: at the same price per token, Opus 5 simply uses 3.9 times more tokens for the same text. For an English-language product, the difference is only 1.55 times: Opus 5's tokens are "shorter" even for English.

  2. The gap between Russian and foreign models for Russian is smaller than it seems from the price list. GigaChat 2 Max is 2.3 times more expensive than GPT-5.5 per token, but 1.9 times more expensive per thousand Russian characters. YandexGPT 5 Lite is twice as cheap as GPT-5.5 per token, but 2.6 times more expensive per thousand Russian characters.

  3. Kimi K3 is twice as cheap as GPT-5.5 by price list, but the difference for Russian shrinks to 1.24 times.

One clarification for real calculations: the tokenizer is the same for the model's response. Long Russian responses become more expensive in the same proportion, and output tokens are usually 3–5 times more expensive than input tokens. For a chatbot that writes a lot, the effect is stronger than in the table above.

Side Finding: Aggregator Usage Is Not Always Model Usage

When measuring through one of the aggregators, claude-sonnet-5 and claude-opus-4-8 returned prompt_tokens that matched tiktoken o200k_base exactly for all eight texts. At the same time, claude-opus-5 from the same aggregator returned completely different numbers, and the response included Anthropic's overhead fields (claude_cache_creation_5_m_tokens, usage_source: anthropic). Sonnet 5 and Opus 4.8 did not have such fields.

There are two explanations: either the gateway for these models recalculates tokens itself using OpenAI's tokenizer, or the requests are sent to the wrong model. It's impossible to verify from the inside. Both options mean that usage numbers from an intermediary cannot be considered model data until you verify them: with a local tokenizer, where available, or with the provider's overhead fields. For the same reason, Sonnet 5 and Opus 4.8 are not included in the table. It was not possible to measure them honestly through the gateways available to me on the day of measurement, and Claude was unavailable through the second gateway.

How to Repeat on Your Own Texts

For models with open tokenizers, five lines are enough:

import tiktoken
from tokenizers import Tokenizer

text = open("my_prompt_ru.txt").read()
gpt = tiktoken.get_encoding("o200k_base")
giga = Tokenizer.from_file("GigaChat3__tokenizer.json")   # from ai-sage/GigaChat3-10B-A1.8B
print(len(text) / len(gpt.encode(text)), "chars/token for GPT-5.x")
print(len(text) / len(giga.encode(text, add_special_tokens=False).ids), "chars/token for GigaChat")

For closed models — the difference between two requests:

def text_tokens(client, model, text, base="Respond in one word: ok."):
    def pt(content):
        r = client.chat.completions.create(model=model, max_tokens=16,
                                           messages=[{"role": "user", "content": content}])
        return r.usage.prompt_tokens
    return pt(base + "\n\n" + text) - pt(base)

Measure on your own prompts: system instructions, typical user queries, RAG documents. The proportion of Cyrillic, numbers, code, and markup in real traffic significantly affects the outcome. A prompt with a JSON schema for tools in English and a short Russian query will yield a different ratio than pure Russian prose.

Limitations

  • Few texts: four pairs, about 2,500 characters of prose per language. The variation between genres for each model is small, the order of models is the same across all genres, but the exact coefficients on your data will differ by a few percent.

  • GigaChat 3 tokenizer ≠ GigaChat 2. In the price table, GigaChat 2 Max is calculated using the open GigaChat 3 tokenizer, as there are no others available. If GigaChat 2 has a different tokenizer, the ruble figures will shift.

  • Grok 4.6 in the price table is calculated using the Grok 4.7 tokenizer, for which usage was measured. The models are from the same family, but this is an assumption.

  • Prices are medians across aggregators on a single date. Direct developer tariffs and minimum prices from individual services differ, sometimes by orders of magnitude.

  • Usage for closed models was obtained through gateways. For DeepSeek and GPT, I verified it against open tokenizers and achieved exact matches. For Gemini, GLM, Grok, and Kimi, there was nothing to compare against, but the numbers are plausible and stable. For Claude Opus 5, usage is confirmed by Anthropic's overhead fields.

What to Do With This

If the product is Russian-language:

  1. Compare models by the price for a typical request on your own data, not by the price list per 1M tokens. Taking measurements takes half an hour and can change your model choice.

  2. Factor in language adjustments in your budget. For GPT, Gemini, Grok — add ~20% to "English" estimates, for DeepSeek and Qwen — add 40–50%, for Claude Opus 5 — triple it.

  3. Keep the overhead part of the prompt in English if the model tokenizes Russian poorly. System instructions, tool descriptions, JSON schemas are cheaper in English. User text and responses remain in Russian. For Opus 5, this can reduce the input tokens for the system part by almost threefold, but check if the quality of Russian responses drops.

  4. Do not blindly trust intermediary usage, especially if your billing is based on it. Verify it at least once against an open tokenizer or the model developer's dashboard.
