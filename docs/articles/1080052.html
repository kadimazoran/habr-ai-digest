A common task: add order status, change API, update form, migration, tests, and documentation. In an AI-native process, an agent can go through almost this entire route: study the requirements, find related components, propose a plan, change code, run checks, and prepare a merge request (MR). A human makes the decision and is responsible for the result.

But this is not a story about proven success. It is an example of a real case of implementing an AI-native development pilot in a corporation: a testable hypothesis, an engineering framework, and measurement rules. A positive outcome is not guaranteed.

Data first, then models

In 2025, METR conducted a randomized study: 16 experienced developers completed 246 tasks in open-source projects they were familiar with. With tools at the February-June 2025 level, they worked 19% slower on average. After the experiment, participants believed AI had accelerated them by 20%.

The 2026 data did not resolve the contradiction. In the METR survey, 349 technical specialists estimated the value increase of their work from AI at 1.4-2 times, but the authors specifically warn: self-assessment does not equal measured performance. A follow-up field experiment with 57 developers and over 800 tasks had to be redesigned. The sample shifted, participants didn't want to give up AI, and parallel agents made time accounting difficult.

DORA, in its 2025 report, calls AI an amplifier of the organizational system. It amplifies not only strengths but also weaknesses. Therefore, distributing licenses by itself proves nothing.

METR measured the work of individual specialists, not a corporate AI-native framework. I cannot confirm that such a framework reliably turns slowdowns into acceleration. This is precisely why a real pilot is needed as an experiment, not as a ceremony to confirm a decision already made.

A Russian framework cannot be built like a typical SaaS

A critical process cannot be designed relying on the constant availability of a foreign model and its API. As of September 9, 2026, Russia is not on Anthropic's official list of supported countries for either its commercial API or Claude.ai. Other foreign cloud services may also be unavailable or operate with restrictions. Business processes should not depend on Claude, another specific model, or a single provider.

In a corporate context, there is commercial secrecy, personal data, source code, and infrastructure information. For personal data of Russian citizens, Federal Law 152-FZ must be considered, including the localization requirement for certain operations when collecting data, as stipulated in Article 18, Part 5. A specific scenario requires separate legal assessment based on the official text of the law.

This gives rise to a separate layer of data and guardrails outside the model: classification, minimization, masking, route control, and blocking of prohibited transfers. These are DLP and policy engine tasks. A single system prompt won't help here.

There is another Russian reality: legacy systems, 1C, integration buses, closed segments, and documentation in the genre of "ask the person who wrote it." For an agent, such context simply doesn't exist until the company makes it accessible and manageable.

A 2025 study by ISIEH HSE surveyed over 15,000 large and medium-sized organizations already using AI. Of these, 56% used external expertise, 23% developed AI software in-house, and 18% adapted open-source solutions. This indicates not a single correct platform, but a hybrid model: in-house components, market products, and open-source code.

What is called AI-native here

The term has no universally accepted standard. In this article, **AI-assisted development** means helping a human at specific stages. **AI-native development** means the entire process is designed for collaborative work between humans and software agents: requirements are machine-readable, rules are formalized, context is limited by permissions, changes are automatically verified, and important decisions are confirmed by a human.

If AI wrote a thousand lines in a minute, and the team spent two days rewriting them, the gain only appeared in the presentation. Starting the transformation by choosing a model is as strange as starting to build a factory by choosing a screwdriver.

Five layers of the corporate framework

1. Model Gateway

Development tools access models through a corporate gateway. It selects an authorized provider, applies limits, masks sensitive data, logs requests, and allows replacing the model without reassembling the process.

Simple tasks can be directed to compact models, complex changes and architectural analysis to more powerful ones. For composite tasks, an "orchestrator and executors" scheme is possible. Anthropic describes routing, parallelization, and orchestrator-workers as different patterns. A swarm of agents makes sense only with a measurable gain in quality.

2. Context for the agent

The agent needs more than just source code and ordinary documentation. It requires up-to-date requirements, API and data schemas, ADRs, dependency graphs, test catalogs, architectural constraints, and decision history. RAG and embeddings speed up search but don't replace the source of truth. Access is checked during context retrieval, before prompt assembly.

Context must be versioned and linked to the code. Otherwise, the agent will confidently propose a solution that was rejected two years ago. It's not stubborn; it just wasn't shown the protocol.

3. Isolated execution environment

The agent operates in a sandbox, has its own identity in logs, receives short-lived credentials, and minimal permissions. Irreversible actions and privilege escalations require human confirmation. OWASP, regarding the risk of excessive agency, also recommends limiting functions, permissions, and autonomy outside the language model.

4. Automatic evidence base

Modular, integration, and contract tests, static analysis, vulnerability and secret scanning, license control, and architectural rule checks are needed. For critical scenarios, evals are added, which are reproducible tasks for evaluating the model and agent. Along with the result, versions of the model, prompts, tools, and rules are recorded.

OWASP classifies prompt injection as a key risk for LLM applications. Instructions can come from a document, page, or file in the repository. Therefore, external content is considered untrusted, and the agent's actions undergo normal authorization. Rollback and incident management processes are also necessary.

5. Observability and economics

The number of active users says nothing about the result. What's needed is time from requirement to release, review duration, rework volume, rollbacks, defects, the total cost of a completed task, and a verifiable trace of the agent's actions.

The total cost includes model calls, infrastructure, reviews, rework, and maintenance. The comparison should be between one team and one type of task with their baseline, not the number of tokens between departments.

What changes in an engineer's work

An engineer spends less time producing each code fragment and more time defining boundaries, choosing solutions, and verifying evidence. The role of decomposition, architectural thinking, testing, and risk analysis increases.

Particular attention should be paid to junior developers. If a person cannot yet distinguish a good solution from a plausible one, AI can accelerate the accumulation of errors. Mentorship doesn't disappear; it shifts from explaining syntax to verifying assumptions and consequences.

Practical start for 90 days

Below is not an abstract methodology, but a case study from the production company TECHNONIKOL: a working 90-day pilot scheme.

The pilot is conducted with one experimental team. It is chosen based on four criteria: stable, highly qualified engineering staff, complex regular tasks, available metric history, and a high level of motivation for change.

An external AI tracker is involved for the entire 90 days. This is a person, not a model or a monitoring panel. They act as an independent mentor: monitoring the progress of changes, radically but constructively criticizing the team's decisions, and bringing proven global AI-native development practices. They should not be affiliated with the vendor of the evaluated tools, should not choose the product for the team, and should not change the success threshold during the pilot.

First 30 days: measure and constrain. Select several repeatable tasks for the experimental team. Record baseline metrics, data classes, allowed models, routing rules, permissible tools, and prohibited actions.

Days 31-60: build the corporate engineering harness. Here, "harness" means not a local wrapper for a single solution, but an agent operating environment: skills (repeatable instructions and procedures), roles, rules, context, model routing, orchestration, tools, evals, sandbox, observability, and human confirmation points. The framework should have an owner, support rules, and a measurable service level. Anthropic's narrow experiment with a long-running agent illustrates part of this task: even a powerful model needs an environment that stores state, forces small steps, and verifies results.

Days 61-90: test the agent cycle. Allow the agent to prepare a limited type of changes in the sandbox, run tests, and create a merge request. Compare cycle time, defects, and rework. Record successful and unsuccessful patterns and prepare a package for transfer to other teams after the pilot concludes. Within the pilot, the experimental team remains isolated.

The pilot can be considered successful if the median time to complete the selected task type has decreased, and the rate of rollbacks, post-release defects, and repeated reviews has not increased. There is no single universal percentage here. The threshold should be fixed before the experiment, otherwise almost any result can be declared a victory.

If the code writing speed increased, but the release time did not change, the bottleneck is further down the stream. Perhaps in testing, approval, the architectural committee, or release management. Buying another model will not solve this issue.

Where AI-native turns into an expensive experiment

Problems arise when a company allows any public services, ties the entire process to a single provider, measures lines of code, and gives an agent autonomy before tests and constraints are in place.

A separate "AI team" existing apart from product development also doesn't work well. Platform expertise is needed, but an AI-native approach emerges where a product is created and operated, not in a presentation laboratory.

Instead of a conclusion

The main change is not that AI has learned to write code. What's scarce are clear requirements, accessible context, fast checks, architectural discipline, and the organization's ability to make decisions without a month-long approval quest.

An AI-native company makes these elements machine-readable, verifiable, and manageable. Therefore, maturity should be measured not by the proportion of code created by the model, but by the part of the journey from business task to reliable industrial result that the system completes faster, without losing control.

Let's return to the task from the beginning of the article: adding order status. If only the code appeared faster, the company has bought very expensive autocompletion. If the entire path to industrial release passed faster and without an increase in defects, risks, and total cost, a new production framework has emerged.
