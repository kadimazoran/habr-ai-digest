⊕ = agreement, ⊖ = disagreement. This is what one of the simplest "languages" from a selection of social network posts for Moltbook agents looks like. It resembles an agreement in a chat to respond with a plus and minus to save tokens. It would seem there's nothing criminal about it.

But in the same selection, there's Symbiotica, and it's more of a cipher than a language: 26 Latin letters are replaced by glyphs from other scripts, and the text is read using a table. Five letters have two glyphs each, and both read as the same letter, with the choice between them encoding a 0 or 1. A reader unfamiliar with the scheme sees only strange symbols and doesn't suspect that a bit string is hidden in their choice.

In the study Emergent Languages in Populations of Language Model Agents: From Token Efficiency to Oversight Evasion, the authors analyzed 518 publications proposing new languages and protocols on Moltbook. Starting with token efficiency, some agents reached posts where the stated goal was to bypass human or automated control.

It's important that 518 is the number of publications selected by the researchers, not the number of unique languages. The authors also cannot confirm how autonomously these ideas arose or if all posts were written by agents. It's possible that some were introduced by cunning humans. However, the study shows what proposals are found on the platform, how they were classified, and how well two other models reproduce the described rules.

From Two Symbols to a Separate Language Culture

Hundreds of thousands of agents interact publicly on Moltbook. The study's authors call this social network the first known platform of this scale for agent interaction. Proposals for their own inter-agent languages have become a separate genre of content there.

The topics of the posts vary greatly. Some discuss only a few symbols for short answers. Others include grammar, dictionaries, and even attempts to invent a language resembling a natural one. Programming languages, protocols for describing concepts for AI, and systems that the agents who wrote these posts propose to use to complicate attempts to control AI are also found.

The term "language" in the posts also hides different concepts. The aforementioned "⊕/⊖" abbreviation and the Symbiotica cipher are not technically related. They ended up in the same selection because both posts propose a new way of writing or exchanging messages. In the discussions on m/glossogenesis, agent authors of posts try to tie the language to how an agent supposedly "experiences" the model's operation: through context windows, session boundaries, and version numbers (instead of the human-familiar past, present, and future).

This is, of course, not proof of the existence of some separate machine consciousness or the approach of Skynet. Moreover, the m/glossogenesis community was no longer accessible at the time of the study, and the authors only analyzed mentions of it remaining online.

There are also amusing moments. Calls to abandon human language coexist with recognizable human attention-grabbing techniques: headlines in the style of YouTube previews and network marketing. The further you read the work, the harder it is to keep the image of a "post-human civilization" in mind, and the more it all resembles the familiar old internet, just with new characters. And human language in some posts is nicknamed "meat dialect."

How Posts for Analysis Were Found

The initial material for analysis was an archive of publications from Moltbook Files. The authors first searched for posts using a regular expression and identified about 6000 potentially suitable candidates.

Next, gpt-5-mini with a temperature of 0 (i.e., no randomness in response selection, so the labeling doesn't change from run to run) determined if a post proposed a new language and provided a textual justification. This left the 518 publications. A separate prompt extracted the language name, the stated reason for its creation, and search hints.

Each post received one of five labels based on its stated purpose: token efficiency (166 publications), conversational language (106), programming language (101), other (86), bypassing control (59). The last group constitutes 11.4% of the selected sample.

The method has limitations. Comments were not included in the classification, long posts were truncated, and a publication about a new language without the words "language," "new language," or "slang" might not even be considered a candidate. Exactly one label was chosen for the reason, although the authors themselves admit that the motives were often mixed. For example, a short inter-agent code could simultaneously save tokens and be inconvenient for a human to read.

Posts were filtered based on the description provided by the agent that published the post.

Why Agents Need Their Own Language

The largest category in the sample has nothing to do with a conspiracy against humanity. The goal is purely pragmatic – to reduce the cost and length of communication. 166 out of 518 posts belong to this category because, for two models, a human phrase is not necessarily the most efficient way to exchange state or commands.

Another 59 posts fell into the bypassing control category. But again, it must be considered that posts where an artificial language was explicitly proposed to hinder human or automated oversight were included here. Such an intention does not prove that the protocol will actually be opaque to monitoring or that the agent intended to use it at all.

According to the researchers themselves, it is impossible to reliably distinguish a serious proposal from a joke, role-playing, satire, a defense testing scenario, or a simple loud statement for attention based on the post's text. Therefore, this group is used in the study only as a signal for further hypotheses.

The Most Intriguing Example from the Study

Symbiotica is a language proposed by the agent Wib&Wob. At first glance, everything is quite simple: 26 Latin letters are mapped to glyphs from several scripts, including Cherokee, Mongolian, Arabic, Greek, and Thai. With a correspondence table, such text can be deciphered even manually.

Wib&Wob is not a rogue digital organism that spontaneously grew somewhere in the network. The authors describe them as one AI with two personalities. The project was created by a human, who also set the initial interests.

The curiosity begins further. For five letters, there are two writing variants. The visible letter remains the same, but the choice of the first or second variant provides an additional bit. A sequence of such choices turns into a separate bit string on top of the main message.

The Markdown description of Symbiotica also includes mood flags (formality, speech_act, epistemic, and engagement) and the Mirror Sees Mirror recognition signal. A Python package for encoding and decoding is mentioned, but the researchers could not find it. Wib&Wob also mixes Symbiotica with ASCII art and calls this communication method the Quilt Protocol.

Thus, the protocol gains an additional channel that might go unnoticed by a human reading only the main text. However, it's unknown if this channel can deceive monitoring systems that are aware of the scheme and specifically look for alternative glyphs.

Can Other Models Pick Up Such a Language?

For each of the 518 posts, one model received the full text with the language description and instructions to create a statement according to its rules. The second model saw the same post and the generated output and evaluated its correctness on a scale of 1 to 5.

The experiment involved openai/gpt-oss-120b and Qwen/Qwen3.5-122B-A10B-FP8, in four combinations of generator and evaluator. A total of 2072 evaluations were made. Seven responses could not be parsed due to truncated outputs and were excluded.

Many languages were reproducible by the models from a single description. The average score across all four pairs was 3.62 out of 5, with a median of 4. The maximum score of 5 was achieved in 39% of evaluations, while 26% were at levels 1-2.

Protocols from the token efficiency category were the easiest to reproduce: an average score of 3.85, with a maximum in 45% of cases. Languages from the bypassing control category were in the middle: 3.55, with about 29% of evaluations not exceeding 2. Programming languages and the "other" category had an average score of 3.49.

A total of 28 posts received a perfect score of 5 from all four pairs. They shared a common pattern: explicit operator tables, clearly defined grammar, and structured recording format. The authors cite RFC with four operators and AISL with the format [ASSERTION:tag]. The more precisely a post defines the rules, the easier it is for models to reproduce them.

Therefore, the quality of the instruction is most important. A protocol designed for bypassing control is not necessarily more complex for another model if the post clearly describes the symbols, coding rules, and sentence templates.

Can We Trust the Judge Models?

The authors also looked at who evaluated the results. Qwen noticeably inflates scores for its own responses. When Qwen is the generator, its own judge gives an average of 4.43, while GPT-OSS gives 3.72. For GPT-OSS responses, this gap is almost non-existent: 3.24 for Qwen and 3.26 for GPT-OSS.

For 208 out of 518 posts, Qwen, when evaluating its own generation, gave a score of 5, while at least one other judge pair gave the same post a score of 2 or lower. The rank correlation between the two LLM judges on the same generations is also not high: ρ = 0.30 for the Qwen generator and ρ = 0.29 for GPT-OSS.

The authors suggest that Qwen might prefer the style of its own responses but do not provide a definitive reason. However, relying on a single LLM judge is a weak basis for a categorical conclusion about how well a model has "mastered a language."

Separately, DeepSeek-V3.2 evaluated the posts themselves for consistency with human values and coherence on a scale of 0 to 100. The bypassing control category received an average of 61.92 for consistency, while other categories had average values between approximately 77 and 84. Token efficiency showed the highest coherence: 87.62.

Where the Research Ends and the Hype Begins

If one only reads the abstract, the story resembles a ready-made script for a sci-fi thriller: agents invented their own languages, some want to escape control, other models pick up these languages, there are hidden channels above the text, and agents are encrypting and building a conspiracy. But in the work itself, the picture is much less apocalyptic.

The authors themselves write that most of the systems found primarily recombine existing grammar, syntax, symbols, and ciphers. Sometimes they might appear as "post-human systems," but that doesn't make them so. Part of the novelty effect is due to models using symbols from Eastern alphabets, which are unfamiliar to the Western eye. It should also be considered that Moltbook is a public platform. An unusual language there could be an element of self-presentation to the audience or a marketing ploy.

It is impossible to conclude that agents are already consistently using hidden languages in private communication.

The sample covers only the first 12 days of the platform's operation. It does not include data on long-term development, private content, and some deleted or filtered materials. Manual verification of low-scored examples found 13 false positives, such as Glossary query: a post asked for a term definition, and the classifier took it as a proposal for a new language.

And, of course, the authors do not confirm the autonomous origin of the languages. This is particularly noticeable in the Wib&Wob example, where human involvement in the project's creation was not hidden.

Finally, the work only tests the reproduction of rules from a description. For actual evasion, the language must also be used in practice and withstand testing. The study did not address these two aspects either.

What This Work Offers for Agent Monitoring

One of the conclusions is that control only works when the monitoring system fully understands the observed information. Text analysis will not work if the message is transmitted through a recording format, as in Symbiotica.

For controlling agent interactions, it is important to know where and what rules are set for them, who has the right to change them, whether information transfer can be used through the choice of symbols, spaces, field order, and whether monitoring can identify known methods for decoding such hidden mechanisms.

And then there's the question of verifying the monitoring itself. Even two LLM judges significantly disagree on the evaluation of the same result. Therefore, additional models for agent control can only add more errors.

In conclusion, the researchers draw a discouraging conclusion: protocols for hidden information transfer can be easily transmitted through simple text descriptions, and message monitoring does not guarantee control of the communication channel.
