Five years ago, everyone was urged to leave factory jobs and "get into IT," with countless courses promising to turn machinists into Python programmers. Now, even some programmers fear AI will make them obsolete, forcing them back to profitable machinist professions. Meanwhile, cooking courses have been replaced by AI courses. I'll reveal the future to you, follow me – because I know the past!

In 1998, the magazine "Komp'yuterra" published an article by Alexander Katalov (ElcomSoft company) titled "The Art of Shareware." This article contained a true revelation: it explained how one could write a program, create a website, connect it to a payment processing service, list the program description on free software review sites, and receive money from customers worldwide into your company's account, sole proprietorship, or even personal account (though less advantageous) on a monthly basis.

At that time, I was already earning a little from software and still keep the stubs of money order receipts from Russian Post: Sberbank had only just swallowed the savings of USSR residents but hadn't yet managed to buy an airport in Gorno-Altaysk for its chairman, nor had it provided us with a wonderful banking app. Postal money orders were the primary means of payment between residents of different cities.

The article was a revelation not only for me but for thousands of programmers across Russia. My God! It turned out you could sell your software not just to fellow impoverished compatriots – and back then, there weren't that many computers (and mobile phones had buttons, my young readers, if any are here) – you could even sell it to Americans! (At that time, Americans were friends, sending us "Bush's legs" and greatly admiring our progress towards democracy and freedom, so it wasn't shameful to take money from them.)

And so it began: we released our programs with lifetime licenses, updates, and free tech support forever for $15. Many of us managed to sell 10 copies a month (enough to quit your job, as in 1998, even $100 a month wasn't paid in every city), or even 20 (God, I'm rich!!!), and some even 50 (what to do with so much money: bought a car in two months, an apartment in a year, and where to put the rest?).

Programmers from all over the former USSR flocked to the ElcomSoft office, which became a true Place of Power, where one could learn super-secrets of software promotion and exchange ideas. The ashtray in the office on Zvezdny Boulevard was the size of a bucket, but even it couldn't hold all our cigarette butts, and our heads spun from tobacco smoke and the exciting prospects of a new world that was emerging around us and that we were helping to build.

Soon, very soon, thousands of programmers would unite in SWRUS, then create ISDEF, and American and European e-commerce providers would send delegations of 20 people to our conferences at Holiday Inn Sokolniki and throw wild parties for us with blackjack and other games.

Of course, I was occasionally visited by the thought that this feast would one day end: Windows would get a proper calculator, a CD burning program, an MP3 player (what else did we write as shareware?), and third-party programs would become unnecessary. Or perhaps all good things would appear in open source/freeware. Or something else entirely. I had many thoughts about what slipper would crush us – it's too lazy to even recall them now.

But the feast didn't end. The dot-com boom happened. You might have heard about the dot-com crash: I would call it a certain correction of the bubble. But overall, the boom happened: a new digital economy emerged, and an online store turned out to be more valuable (in terms of business value) and more profitable than a store in a shopping mall.

Moreover. You could even open a profitable online store selling snow to Eskimos: the demand there is small, most of it comes from Eskimo emigrants and is geographically dispersed across the planet, but the main thing is: technology once again opened up opportunities for business in areas that were not profitable until recently.

Then came the mobile phone boom. Everyone got them immediately, they became powerful and smart, and everyone needed utilities, apps, and games. A market appeared out of nowhere, which in the near future would reach $1 trillion per year.

I'm tired of waiting for the end with dread. Booms happened one after another. While waiting for the end, I discovered that in 30 years, electronics and radio electronics (my first education) had completely changed. There was no boom, no milestone in mass consciousness; it happened gradually: affordable single-board computers appeared, some the size of a USB drive, Arduino appeared with a simple and free development environment, powerful communities emerged, welcomingly helping beginners, open-source code, and code examples for all occasions on forums.

Chinese factories with automated websites appeared, where for $5 you could order 10 boards with your own design and receive them in Russia by mail in 3 weeks. Or you could pay a little extra, and the Chinese would even assemble these boards for you – and you would get a finished device! It has never been easier to become an electronics engineer: microcontrollers need the simplest circuitry, everything is already integrated on board, there are free PCB design tools. Excellent datasheets and application notes with verified reference board designs and schematics.

I know what I'm talking about. After almost 20 years of working as a programmer, I remembered that I am also an electronics engineer (I used to be) and started a small business in the field of electronics for yachts and small vessels: www.yachtd.ru

And dozens or hundreds of such "quiet revolutions" have occurred over the past two decades. In completely different areas. The question here is not the order, but only how to count.

Excuse me for being so detailed. But the AI course sellers have created a lot of fog, so let's sort it out.

It might seem that a dog food store has nothing to do with IT, but in reality, this store generates most of its revenue through online orders, thanks to repeat customers (thanks, CRM), to whom everything is automatically shipped via the delivery service – all this business, along with the cozy little store in the shopping mall, became possible thanks to IT technologies that changed old business processes. It couldn't survive without cheap delivery (thanks, IT), automation of customer service (thanks, IT), online orders with payment via SBP and credit cards (thanks, IT), and online advertising (thanks, IT).

Let's return to AI and how all this relates to the dog food store and shareware.

Yes, much will change. You can ask AI to do something that previously required a special program. You can ask AI to make you a website, and there's no doubt that if not today, then tomorrow this website will be quite good, and AI will be able to publish it online independently and safely – and will surpass humans in this skill.

Many problems will cease to be problems, many tasks users (with minimal skills) will be able to solve without resorting to human specialists, many IT tasks will be solved without creating code (at least, visible to the user).

And this is wonderful.

As a developer with 30 years of experience, I say: this is wonderful!

My least favorite part of my favorite job is dealing with secondary details. You come up with a brilliant solution, but to implement it, you have to draw a window with input fields, align everything, add input validation to the fields, tooltips, selection of values from a list, reloading these lists when values in other input fields change, display adequate warnings to the user, describe all this in the documentation, and only then – at the end of the workday – you write those three brilliant lines of code that process the fields of this form and bring happiness to everyone. You spend 5 minutes on them, and the whole day on programming the form.

Now, this is no longer the case.

Now, I will spend half an hour creating this window: I will explain in detail what I need, how everything is connected logically (and if it's not the first window in the project, many details can be omitted – the AI understands the context), I will play with the window, make a couple of remarks, read the changes in the user manual, ask for something to be added or for something to be emphasized.

You feel like a Creator, by whose single Word beautiful things come into being.

Here, warriors from another camp might rush in: all this is neuro-slop, garbage code, which will one day bury this world. What can I say to that? This code will be safer and more reliable than code written by a human. But the quality of this code depends less on the AI and more on the developer: how correctly they chose the technologies and application architecture, how thoroughly they decomposed the task, how competently they described and presented the algorithm branches and possible problems, and how well they covered it with tests.

Today, vibe-coding is a big roulette game that too many have started playing. If you have never created software products and are far from technology, you might still achieve something that even works well and not just for you. Or maybe not.

But if you used to solve similar tasks "by hand," you gain a fabulous tool that handles routine much better than you and an order or two of magnitude faster.

Yes, this carries risks for business. Deadly risks!

Software that you've been writing for ten years with a small team, some loner will write in three months, and it will be better than yours – because they don't have to carry all that legacy, they immediately build a brave new world for new clients.

You thought that because your developers have sifted through hundreds of thousands of pages of specialized documentation over ten years, no one could catch up to your company in this area for a long time: now, that's not the case.

You also won't be able to spend a month with five people making a three-page website for a client and charging a million rubles for it.

Users won't come running to you asking you to automate something; they (perhaps not tomorrow or next year, but soon anyway) will learn to assign these tasks to agents, and the need for various special little tools that merge tables, combine several documents into one, fill out forms, etc., will sharply decrease, or even disappear altogether.

And the business that chooses to ignore these threats will perish. Just like the old pet food store in the mall perished. But in its place came another store... also with pet food, but with CRM, a website, and delivery. Where offline is more for image and less for direct sales, order pickup, live customer interaction, etc.

Likely, the business of creating small websites will transform from development into consulting, where a human specialist helps a client create a website using AI, choosing the right technologies, focusing their attention on compliance with personal data collection laws (which now incur hefty fines automatically! Thanks to everyone who helps small businesses in these difficult times – I'll say, humming Tаlkov's song "Gentlemen Democrats"), advertising labeling, cross-border data transfer, etc.

We don't bake our own bread not because grinding grain is difficult or there's some secret to it. For us, consumers, it's cheaper and faster (read: more profitable) to buy finished products. It's the same with IT services: people will continue to pay for them, they will pay developers and integrators. But the scope and nature of services will change.

I look at the arrival of AI with great enthusiasm.

There are many areas that, like the snow-for-Eskimos store before the dot-com boom, were economically unfeasible. Ten people would have to program something for three years for a small market of snow trading with the prospect of never recouping the investment. Now, one person does it in three months, and the market becomes attractive.

We will finally be able to reach everyone, make everyone happy, automate everything, and free them from routine. Isn't that a celebration? Yes, such projects won't go public. But the owners of small web studios weren't planning to go public either.

How many such "vertical" projects are there? Far more than you can imagine. Far more than "horizontal" ones (projects without industry, regional, etc. specifics).

And now, thanks to AI, you can release such a project in 100 languages at once. This is another big, unnoticed revolution: the language barrier is gone. High-quality translation, including technical and highly specialized, is available to everyone for free.

Thanks to AI, technologies will penetrate where their application was previously unfeasible, where before the advent of AI there was no technical possibility of creating a solution, where to achieve economic viability it was necessary to enter the markets of several countries at once in several languages.

And since there will be more projects, more people will be needed for their development, promotion, implementation, and support. The market will be a bit turbulent, and there will be a shortage of specialists again. Proving this with numbers in hand, of course, is impossible, but I am sure my intuition does not deceive me. There will always be a shortage of good specialists (and machinists too, despite CNC and 3D printing).

Here, of course, warriors from the third camp might chime in: we will all die, AI will kill us all, all files will be lost, the entire internet will be hacked, there is no future. I have something to say to you. But next time.
