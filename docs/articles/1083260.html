A new divide is emerging between those who create and manage AI, and those who merely use ready-made solutions.

Artificial intelligence is rapidly becoming a part of everyday infrastructure—but not everywhere.

Over the past decade, I've worked on digital inclusion and literacy projects, from Europe to sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia, and I've seen the same pattern everywhere: each new "breakthrough" technology arrives where inequality already exists—in internet access, skills, and institutional capacity. AI is no exception; it only deepens these divides.

More than just access to technology is at stake. Countries that remain consumers of AI, rather than its creators, risk:

— Failing to foster their own innovative environments;

— Failing to strengthen governance;

— Ending up with systems that do not accommodate their languages, cultures, and social priorities.

Thus, the divide in AI creation becomes a divide in economic opportunities and technological influence.

But there is another path: participating in AI development without replicating the race for cutting-edge models led by the US and China. What this path offers and its difficulties are evident in the examples of South Africa and Indonesia.

Computing power is concentrated in a few countries

According to the AI Index 2026 report from Stanford University, the US has over 5,000 active data centers—more than ten times that of any other country. AI tasks are increasingly being solved in the cloud rather than on private servers, so the concentration of power leads to increased dependence on a few providers. According to the World Bank, the US accounted for about 87% of global cloud computing and data storage service exports in 2023.

For most countries, this means that AI development is outsourced—not only technologically but also commercially and geopolitically—and they do not control it. The infrastructure that powers systems worldwide is located in a handful of states and a few companies. And systems created and regulated in a few countries and in a few languages are unlikely to truly serve the entire world.

Skills and AI literacy are unevenly distributed

Even where internet and cloud access exist, not everyone can utilize them. In OECD countries, only about 40% of adults possess digital skills above the basic level. Advanced skills in computing technologies and AI are concentrated among highly educated workers and in tech-intensive industries.

Governments are rushing to integrate AI into education, but they usually start with higher education levels. UNESCO notes that such initiatives are increasing globally, but AI literacy in primary and secondary schools and ethical training for teachers are supported unevenly.

Those with good education, developed digital skills, and stable internet benefit: for them, AI becomes a tool that expands opportunities. The higher the education level, the more frequently people learn to work with AI: according to an OECD survey, 36% of respondents with higher education and only 18% with secondary education took such courses in the past year.

Others are more likely to encounter AI as an opaque system that makes decisions for them—from algorithms in social security, as in the Dutch childcare benefit scandal, to hiring tools like Amazon's closed recruitment system. They cannot understand or influence such technology.

In the 2010s, the tax authorities falsely accused between 20,000 and 30,000 families, according to various estimates, of fraud related to childcare benefit (kinderopvangtoeslag—a payment for low-income families where both parents work) and demanded full repayment. Among the criteria used to select families for review was dual citizenship. Many ended up in debt, some lost their homes and jobs. Following a parliamentary investigation, the Mark Rutte government resigned in January 2021.

Who sets the rules

The AI agenda is still set by a few major companies and a small group of technologically advanced nations. The rest are constantly catching up: adapting foreign models, standards, and templates for "trustworthy AI" to their own needs, and they often lack their own specialists to assess the pros and cons or propose alternatives.

In Indonesia and South Africa, people generate vast amounts of data, but their voices are barely considered when AI systems are developed, regulated, and implemented. Their languages are poorly represented in training data, their institutions lack the resources to participate in international regulation, and models are tested on datasets with few examples from their lives. The role of most countries in the Global South remains limited to adapting systems developed elsewhere.

South Africa: ambitions outpaced capabilities

In April 2026, South Africa's Department of Communications and Digital Technologies published a draft national AI policy proposing the creation of new oversight bodies. The document was soon withdrawn: a journalist discovered that at least six academic references in it led to non-existent works—apparently, AI hallucinations. The minister called the incident an "unacceptable oversight."
