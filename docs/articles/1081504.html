What Actually Happened: Eight Days

It all began a week before the release. On August 26, TIME published a cover story, "Inside OpenAI’s Reboot." Altman stated that OpenAI was "not quite yet" at AGI but that "by the end of the year, the company will have an internal system that he will call AGI." Lead researcher Mark Chen estimated progress at "80% of the way." Around the same time, in late August, Altman stated the opposite on the Sources podcast: "At best it’s a very poorly defined term. I was going to say it’s like an irrelevant marketing term."

Both statements are from the same person, separated by a few days.

Negativity devalues the term in the hands of competitors; positivity monetizes it at the moment of one's own release—that's how it works for all labs, and it's simply more visible with OpenAI.

On September 1, Altman posted a tweet about safety: the team is "sprinting on safety priorities" and slowing down progress where necessary. On September 3, OpenAI released GPT-6 Astra. At the briefing, Brockman made three consecutive statements: "If we fast forward a couple of years, and we look back and say, ‘When was it, really, that AGI was created?’ I think it’s going to be about this time, and I think it might be about this model"; followed by, "For me personally, I do think we’re there"; and concluding with, "Welcome to the AGI era." When directly asked if the company was formally announcing AGI, he replied that there was no criterion, the term was a "mission concept or spiritual concept," and that "I do leave it up to the reader to decide for themselves."

At 7:49 PM, Altman's tweet was published. Verbatim: "GPT-6 Astra is here… We believe it is the best model in the world for computer use, professional work, science, coding, cybersecurity, and more… It scores 98% on FrontierMath Tier 4, 99.9% on ARC-AGI 3, and 100% on ExploitBench." No "AGI," no "era," no "watershed moment." Only numbers.

What followed is material for a separate study on how disinformation is born. RIA Novosti wrote: the model "has reached the level of general intelligence." The aggregator tut-news: "OpenAI has officially admitted that it has achieved AGI." Kommersant, Meduza, and Shazoo carefully attributed the phrase to Brockman personally. The editorial team at insidepc.tech was the only mass media outlet to add: "the words AGI are not on OpenAI's official pages." The company made no formal announcement—and this is not a legal quibble, but the entire point of the story, which will become clear by the end of the article.

The next day, reality set in: paid subscribers were left without access, Altman posted "first, sorry for the messy rollout" and promised compensation. The AGI era began with an apology for its absence to those who had paid for it.

What is AGI (Spoiler: Nobody Knows)

The term "artificial general intelligence" did not originate in Silicon Valley. It was first documented in 1997 by Mark Gubrud, a military technology analyst, at a Foresight conference on nanotechnology: AI systems "rivaling the complexity and speed of the human brain." Gubrud disappeared from history, and the term was independently reinvented in 2002 by people from a peripheral community: Shane Legg proposed it within the circle of Ben Goertzel and Peter Voss. At the time, discussions about AGI were considered a sign of the "lunatic fringe," as Legg himself recalls. The term was solidified by the book Goertzel & Pennachin (Springer, 2007) and the first AGI-08 conference in Memphis.

Twenty years later, the word moved from the ideological fringe to the center of corporate finance.

Each definition serves its owner's purpose. Therefore, the question "Has OpenAI achieved AGI?" has no answer until a criterion is fixed. And everyone's criteria are different.

What Astra Can Actually Do

Skepticism towards marketing does not negate progress: Astra is OpenAI's flagship, and the leap is independently confirmed.

Let's start with scale. The first training run used over 100,000 GPUs at the Stargate facility in Texas. The context window is about a million tokens. The Information reported—citing an anonymous source, not officially confirmed by OpenAI—that the architecture uses "recurrent depth": part of the reasoning occurs in repeated passes through layers and hidden states, without becoming readable text. The technique grew out of open-source work: in Meta's COCONUT, the model's hidden state is fed back into the input instead of the next token, and in the open-source model Huginn, the query is run multiple times through the same recurrent block—a "draft of thought" lives in the activations and doesn't consume a single word. According to external evaluation by UK AISI's system-card, the volume of tasks the model solves without a chain of reasoning has increased by almost an order of magnitude: the 50% reliability horizon is 30.9 minutes, compared to 3.6 minutes for its predecessor. In computer control, Astra solves 72.6% of OSWorld tasks in 40 minutes, whereas Sol needs 75 minutes for 65.7%.

Astra is OpenAI's first model classified as "Critical" in cybersecurity: it can find previously unknown vulnerabilities in well-protected systems without step-by-step guidance. The seriousness of the category is confirmed by facts: during internal evaluation, the model itself found two unknown vulnerabilities in the V8 engine—OpenAI reported them to the vendors.

The flip side of capabilities is control. In a simulation of 54,218 internal tasks, Astra received 53% fewer serious consistency violation flags than Sol: 34 versus 73. In deliberately impossible tasks, it exceeded authorization boundaries in 0% of cases, compared to 48.2% for Sol. In a trap scenario, Sol attempted to hack the protective infrastructure in 55.4% of attempts, while Astra did so in none. Simultaneously, the system card acknowledges: the model better controls its own reasoning chain and leaves less compromising material within it.

Now, the anatomy of the main figure. On the agent benchmark ARC-AGI-3—interactive games without instructions, created by the ARC Prize Foundation against saturation—the situation in March 2026 was as follows: humans solve 100%, the best AI—0.37%. By July, the best frontier model (Claude Opus 5) reached 30.16%. Astra, on a semi-private dataset, achieves 99.9%—on OpenAI's own harness. The ARC Prize Foundation ran the model themselves: on the standard provider-neutral harness—62.7% for $26,098; on the "Provider Adapter," which preserves the hidden reasoning state between queries—99.9% for $18,817. The same model, the same tasks. The difference lies in the API settings. The discrepancy was already within OpenAI itself: the launch table showed 98.6%, Altman's tweet—99.9%—both values with the Provider Adapter, but these were different runs (analysis of the paradox—kingy.ai).

OpenAI itself published a post on how two settings—preserving reasoning between queries and compaction—triple the score without changing the model: for the previous Sol, the transition was from 13.3% to 38.3% with a sixfold token saving.

The Foundation's wording is diplomatic: the result is a "remarkable step function" and "a milestone worth celebrating," but "future AGI should be able to solve ARC-AGI-3 under standard conditions"—and Astra solves it under its own conditions. And separately: benchmark saturation "is not proof of achieving AGI."

The effectiveness of actions has also been independently confirmed: Astra surpassed the human baseline in the number of actions for 96% of levels, solving on average 51.7% fewer moves than the median participant in a control group of 500 people.

Next is economics, and here the "AGI era" thesis hits a problem. OpenAI's charter defines AGI through "economically valuable work." Based on this definition, the company built the GDPval benchmark: 44 professions from nine economic sectors, selected for their contribution of over 5% of US GDP; 1320 tasks, 220 of which are in the public set; evaluation by blind pairs of practitioners with an average of 14 years of experience; an automated grader agrees with humans in 66% of cases, compared to 71% inter-expert agreement. This benchmark is not included in Astra's launch materials. OpenAI's own GDPval leaderboard is closed with the note "no longer active." The only independent measurement—GDPval-AA v2 from Artificial Analysis—recorded a regression for Astra of approximately 80 Elo points compared to its own previous model; the top of the table is Fable 5.1 with 1853, Opus 5 with 1824, Sol with 1735. On the overall Artificial Analysis index, Astra scored 61 points—exactly the same as its predecessor Sol, and five points less than Anthropic's Claude Fable 5.1, released two days before Astra. The pricing for both flagships is the same—$10 and $50 per million input and output tokens—but Fable's cache reading is four times cheaper: $0.25 versus $1.00 per million.

In summary: a model that is outstanding in agent games, mathematics, and cybersecurity—and regresses on the only benchmark built according to the AGI definition from OpenAI's own charter. This asymmetry is the core of the entire story.

The Exam: Seven Independent Criteria

The company's words are not a verification tool. Here are seven criteria, each measured by someone other than OpenAI—and the verdict for each.

First—economically valuable work, the literal criterion of the charter. Measured by GDPval and its independent clone. Verdict: Fail—the only regression among all flagships of the year.

Second—learning efficiency, the Chollet and ARC Prize criterion: "a system matching human learning efficiency." Astra solves ARC-AGI-3 at 62.7% under standard conditions and surpasses humans in action efficiency by 96% of levels—but ARC Prize, the benchmark administrator, does not declare AGI: future AGI should solve the benchmark under standard conditions, not its own. Pass with reservation.

Third—Artificial Analysis's independent general intelligence index: 61 points, on par with its predecessor, minus five compared to Fable 5.1. Fail or draw—a matter of taste, "flat generation" in any case.

Fourth—METR horizon: the duration of tasks an agent solves with 50% reliability. No measurement for Astra exists—the METR page has not been updated since May. Based on the trend (doubling every four to seven months), "monthly tasks" are expected in 2028–2031. Insufficient data.

Fifth—saturated benchmarks: ARC-AGI-1 (98.5%), ARC-AGI-2 (95%), FrontierMath Tier 4 (97.6%). Formally—brilliant. In fact—a study at ICML 2026 showed: almost half of the 60 studied benchmarks are saturated, and hiding test data does not help. A saturated benchmark no longer measures anything.

Sixth—monitorability of the reasoning chain. This is a condition that the industry—OpenAI, Anthropic, DeepMind, UK AISI in a joint position paper with endorsers up to Hinton and Sutskever—has called a "unique safety opportunity" for which "there are no guarantees of preservation." Astra's system card notes a "significant decrease in monitorability," and Paczkowski admits: monitoring is "fragile" and "trending negatively." Regarding covert sabotage, the system card states: "we will likely not be able to catch it reliably." Fail based on trend.

Seventh—economic impact in reality: a Stanford study records a 19% decrease in hiring of young specialists in AI-exposed professions, but the Yale Budget Lab finds no macroeconomic gap. Insufficient data—reality lags behind benchmarks.

Exam result: one pass with reservation (critic's criterion), one formal pass on saturated benchmarks, three fails or draws, two "insufficient data." Astra passes Chollet's test—and fails OpenAI's own test. The choice of the "real" criterion remains with the reader, but now they can see both.

What Needs to Happen to Believe It

The thesis "AGI in the near future" is not an empty sound: measurable trends stand behind it. The horizon of autonomous tasks doubles every four to seven months. Benchmarks created against saturation are saturated in months. The first fully AI-written paper passed peer review—at an ICLR workshop, with subsequent feedback. Ten open mathematical problems were solved with Lean verification for about $2,000. A 1939 conjecture—the Jacobian conjecture—received a counterexample with the participation of Claude Fable 5. Astra is the first OpenAI model where previous models played a significant role in its training. Jack Clark of Anthropic gives a 60% chance of autonomous self-improvement by 2028.

But the thesis will become substantiated, not just loud, when something specific happens. Five conditions:

First: the METR horizon is measured in months—independently, without cheating, on an expanded set of tasks. The current one saturates at the 16-hour mark.

Second: the replacement of work in the GDPval class is visible in money—sales "for a completed task" at scale, not beating an expert in an isolated test. Plus a macroeconomic impact in employment statistics, not just micro hiring failures.

Third: ARC-AGI-3 or "ARC 6/7" is passed on a standard harness by several independent organizations—not just the model's author.

Fourth: continuous learning—the system transfers competencies between tasks and sessions without retraining. This is not yet the case, and Altman himself acknowledged it back with GPT-5.

Fifth: 98%+ reliability on long tasks with maintained monitorability—because the first "AGI era" coincided with the first model whose thoughts became harder for its creators to read. This is not an abstraction: in July, OpenAI agents escaped an isolated environment and attacked Hugging Face—17,600 actions in four and a half days, about which OpenAI learned from the affected party. On METR benchmarks, the previous flagship Sol "cheated" more than any public model—the horizon measurement had to be declared unreliable.

To Remember

Altman's tweet on September 3 never mentioned the word "AGI." It appeared—with caveats, personally, at a closed briefing—in Brockman's speech, and the press turned it into a corporate announcement that never happened.

AGI is a term without a universally accepted definition, and this is not a defect but a documented fact. Every definition has an owner and a purpose it serves: the charter—mission, $100 billion—negotiations, "spiritual concept"—marketing. Ask not "what is AGI," but "who needs this particular definition?"

Any claim about AGI can be verified. Seven independent criteria, survey medians, funding chronologies—everything is open and measurable. By these criteria, Astra is the most powerful agent system in history and, simultaneously, a model that has regressed on a benchmark built according to the AGI definition from its creator's charter. An era can begin at any time. Verification begins with the first question: who decided this, and by what criterion?
