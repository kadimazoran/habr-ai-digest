AI Has Reached Testing

In development, generative models are already used for writing and analyzing code. Now, similar scenarios are emerging in testing: AI helps prepare unit tests, analyze complex legacy systems, and automate repetitive operations.

However, the mere presence of a model does not guarantee a useful result. AI needs context, rules, and constraints. Otherwise, instead of a meaningful set of checks, you might get a formally correct but useless construct.

This problem is particularly noticeable when generating unit tests. The model can quickly prepare the foundation, but it doesn't always understand the business logic, the criticality of individual scenarios, and the real risks of the system. Therefore, a specialist has to check not only the code but also what exactly the generated test is checking.

Automation Doesn't Start with AI

Not all of a tester's routine requires neural networks. A significant portion of repetitive operations can be reduced using existing tools: autotests, templates, checklists, and well-structured processes.

One practical scenario is combining Vanessa Automation and AI for smoke testing. Automation handles the execution of stable checks, while the model can assist with preparing scenarios and analyzing results.

The value of a tool is determined not by its technological novelty, but by its ability to be integrated into daily work. Therefore, materials that leave not only ideas but also ready-made artifacts—repositories, templates, instructions, and checklists—are particularly useful.

Quality is the Entire Team's Task

The line between testing, development, and analytics is becoming increasingly blurred.

The developer is responsible for code testability and unit tests. The analyst is responsible for the clarity of requirements. The QA engineer is responsible for the testing strategy and risk assessment. The manager is responsible for processes and specialist interaction.

Even well-configured automation will not fix the situation if team members misunderstand requirements differently or discuss solution limitations too late. Therefore, QA is paying more attention to communication, feedback, and collaborative work on product quality.

Why a Team Needs a Competency Matrix

When a company has only one tester, their development can be discussed individually. As the team grows, a need arises for a more transparent system:

  • what knowledge and skills are needed for each position;

  • how an engineer differs from a senior engineer;

  • what competencies have already been formed;

  • what needs to be learned to move to the next level;

  • what skills are truly needed by a specific company.

A matrix helps a specialist see a development path, and a manager to discuss an employee's growth based on clear criteria.

However, simply copying someone else's model is not enough. In different companies, the same positions can imply different tasks, levels of autonomy, and areas of responsibility. A ready-made matrix should be considered a foundation that needs to be adapted to the products, processes, and structure of your team.

When developing a model, you can rely not only on the experience of other organizations but also on professional standards and GOSTs. They will not replace internal work but will help verify the completeness of requirements and avoid building a competency system from scratch.

What is Changing in QA Work

Several practical conclusions can be drawn from the discussion.

  1. AI is becoming a working tool for testers but requires limitations and expert verification.

  2. Test generation does not negate the understanding of business logic and product risks.

  3. Automation is useful where it reduces repetitive work and leaves specialists with tasks requiring analysis.

  4. Quality depends not on a separate department but on the interaction of development, analytics, QA, and management.

  5. A competency matrix cannot be transferred from another company without changes—it must be linked to the team's actual tasks.
