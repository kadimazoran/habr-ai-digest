A person had an old laptop. On it, they vibe-coded an fb2 reader to read directly in the browser, with a dark theme and custom settings. They used it constantly. Then they bought a new laptop, and were too lazy to transfer the reader from the old one – just a couple of hand movements, but lazy.

They didn't transfer it. They vibe-coded a second one. "Even better."

Someone in the comments couldn't resist, "Fbreader already exists. You can just install and use it. You're welcome :) ". The reply was short and to the point, "Installing some FBreader, fussing with its quirks - not for me. Twenty minutes of vibe-coding and I have the necessary minimum, everything else is from the devil."

This isn't an argument about readers. It's an argument about what personal software has become in the last year.

Why bother when something is already available?

Over a thousand comments under the article "I Vibe-Coded Over 30 Open Source Applications, and Here's What I Learned" revolve around the same thing – people are massively creating programs for themselves that already exist in a dozen variations, and they get something from it that the ready-made options cannot provide.

The argument "just Google it" sounds reasonable right up until you remember how many times installing/uninstalling "almost suitable" software has turned into an evening, or even a week, without results. One of the thread participants put it harshly.

"Searching through installations and re-installations of strange and unsatisfactory software can take potentially infinite time, with no final satisfaction. Here, time is strictly limited, and any additional spending immediately leads to the desired features."

Twenty minutes of vibe-coding versus a week of searching for the perfect solution – with this calculation, the question "why reinvent the wheel?" ceases to be rhetorical.

But this coin has another side, and it was also formulated in the same thread – perhaps the most biting remark of the entire thread.

"You've made a toy creation, but you're already "scared to look at it." Now extrapolate this feeling to dozens, hundreds, and thousands of services where "effective managers" and "fleet agents engineers" churn out hundreds of kilobytes of spaghetti code daily. And no, no one is going to fix the found bugs; as soon as a critical mass accumulates, and so much code is churned out that a person can't handle it – they change jobs."

The author of these words was responding to the confession of another commenter who vibe-coded a Twitch client for Windows XP, "didn't write a single line myself," and honestly admitted that "it seems scary to look at it, but everything works... the feeling of anxiety from losing control over the code is frightening, but now I want to vibe-code more apps." Here it is, the entire ambivalence of the genre in two consecutive remarks – delight from the result and anxiety from the fact that you don't fully understand how it works yourself.

Gallery of Exhibits

If you collect just what people vibe-code "just for themselves" from this one thread, you get a decent cabinet of curiosities.

  • A player for guitar minus tracks – "there are similar ready-made applications, but not exactly what I wanted."

  • A personal monitoring system with DLP functionality – "there were no free open-source products that suited my specific needs... I assembled everything on a $20 plan, the code is already worth several man-years... zero commercial value, of course, but that doesn't negate the convenience at all."

  • A note-taking app that the author "has been working on for six months and can't get enough of" – there was no answer in the thread regarding performance, synchronization, or the plugin system, and this is characteristic; they work on it for enjoyment, not for features from someone else's backlog.

  • A ShareX replacement for Mac – a long dialogue with an AI instead of a Google search about a specific hotkey that "freezes" the screen while selecting an area; in the end, "I didn't find a full replacement, only a compromise," but along the way, the AI suggested a workaround through Automator.

  • A calculator with its own syntax – without a multiplication sign, with arguments passed to functions without parentheses. The idea's author admitted that they had already done something similar manually and got used to it, and raised the question of whether it's possible to vibe-code the same thing now, but for all other calculators in their life. The thread of fifty replies never resolved whether this is a good idea or hellish pain for anyone who tries to read such notation for the first time.

None of these projects will become products. Each has an audience of one person. And that's precisely why they turned out exactly as they were intended from the very beginning.

660 Thousand Lines for a Couple of Thousand Viewers

The most detailed documented case of this kind is not from the comments, but from a separate article, "How I Vibe-Coded Useless Software for 2.5 Years".

The author was known for specific hobbies before, remastering "Tom and Jerry" in 4K for two years, creating covers for TV shows they don't even watch. In 2024, they needed a tool for side-by-side comparison of images after upscaling, to see how a specific algorithm performs better or worse than a neighboring one, with a magnifying glass. They started by copy-pasting prompts into web chats with ChatGPT, then Claude, then Gemini, five hours a day. Then they moved to Codex and Cursor. Then – to orchestrating several AI agents at once through OpenCode.

After 2.5 years, the tool (now called BlinkTable, formerly Improve-ImgSLI) grew to 660,000 lines of code, its own interface library, an LOD system for giant images, a video editor, an AI gallery, a website, its own MCP server. Along the way – migration from CPU to GPU, a failed attempt to rewrite everything in Rust, and one real horror. An AI agent (DeepSeek) accidentally deleted the entire project at one point. But the same AI agent restored 97.5% of the deleted code itself, from the logs of its own previous sessions.

The author estimates the audience of the finished product as "a couple of thousand people," a narrow stratum of aesthetes involved in video restoration. The conclusion is drawn without any pathos.

"For software with an absolutely silent audience of a couple of thousand people, purely out of interest, a mini-media suite has been created over a couple of years... It was interesting, but whether I will continue it is an open question."

In the comments to this article, someone independently, without knowing about the author's project, created a similar tool for comparing images. Two and a half years of work, 660,000 lines, and somewhere nearby, the exact same thing is being born in parallel. This describes no market; it's just two people who wanted the same small thing and both decided it was easier to vibe-code it than to search.

The most accurate reaction to such stories came not from a skeptic, but from a commenter defending the author.

"Useless software solves a lot of needs for a narrow segment of video restorers. Perhaps one should not be too self-critical of oneself and one's products?"

And the most poetic, from another commenter, who quoted a poem about a Chinese dragon hunter.

"One Zhu learned to catch dragons, wasted his strength and money in vain. It's a pity that he never met a single one in his entire life."

Why They Are Abandoned

Pet projects have a common fate, and in the same thread about 30+ applications, a name was coined for it.

"But the graveyard of personal pet projects is now being replenished at an industrial speed)" - "Pet project sematary!" - "The main thing is that they don't come back in other forms."

It's not about laziness or the idea being bad. It's about vibe-coding radically changing the balance between "writing" and "maintaining." Even before AI, this was written about in relation to no-code platforms of past decades, such as Visual Basic.

"Assembling an application became easy, understanding what's happening in it became harder."

With AI agents, this discrepancy has not disappeared but has intensified by an order of magnitude; assembly has become an evening's work, while deciphering one's own code from six months ago still remains a task requiring attention, which no one has gained more of. Hence the anxiety of "scared to look at it" alongside complete delight from the result.

There is also an external source of load that is usually not considered when starting a project "just for yourself." Linus Torvalds, in a recent dispute about the permissibility of AI in Linux kernel development, specifically noted the problem of "drive-by" bug reports, where someone uses AI to find a bug in someone else's open-source project, reports it, and disappears, leaving the maintainer to fix the discovery.

"A person works on a project for ten years... and then they really burn out" - referring to maintainers of small projects, who, with the advent of AI bug finding, are now overwhelmed with discoveries they cannot handle.

A personal pet project with an audience of one person shouldn't have this problem. But as soon as you put the code in the public domain, just because, just out of politeness to your past self, one project can happily turn into a full-fledged source of burnout. Therefore, a significant part of the graveyard consists not of abandoned ideas, but of consciously never-published ones; the author is satisfied, the issue is closed, and there were no plans to release anything publicly.

To Find Something Unnecessary, You First Need to Create Something Unnecessary

This is perhaps the best formulation of the entire phenomenon, a short remark from the same thread, without any context, sounding like an aphorism in itself.

A vibe-coded pet project was almost never intended to be a product. It has no business plan, no users other than the author, often not even a repository anywhere other than a local disk. It is evaluated not by the market, but by how well it solved one specific small need of one specific person. By this criterion, most of them are a resounding success, even if the author forgets about them in six months, and in another six months, vibes-codes a replacement because they were too lazy to transfer the old one.

The graveyard is indeed growing at an industrial speed. The question is not how to stop it, but that this seems to be the first graveyard in the history of software where almost no one is upset.
