On August 11, 2026, Florian Herrengt, an engineer who works with AI agents daily and writes about it on his blog, published a text that went viral in English-speaking circles: AI is removing the middle class of software engineering. The article is highly recommended, but if you don't have time to read it, don't worry, I'll break down everything important below. I've translated the title a bit freely, but the core thesis is short and unpleasant - AI is destroying the middle class of developers. What follows is not a translation, but a debate:

  • where the author is undoubtedly right,

  • where I disagree with him,

  • what aspects of his conclusions work differently in our country.

Let's start with the source's theses - including those that are usually lost in retellings.

The Main Thesis of the Original Article's Author

AI has removed the bottleneck on code production speed, but not on code understanding speed. Previously, a weak engineer physically couldn't do much damage quickly. Now, they produce AI-generated nonsense code very quickly. The author puts it this way:

Before AI, a bad engineer would struggle to produce code that even compiled. <…> Now a bad engineer can produce 10,000 lines of working code before lunch.

Before AI, it was difficult for a weak engineer to produce code that even compiled. Now, they produce ten thousand lines of working code before lunch. Following this logic: projects with weak engineering cultures collapse not in years, but in months, and the value of someone who understands the system as a whole increases.

This leads to the conclusion that it's about seniors, and this is how the text is usually retold. The author himself does not draw this conclusion. This is an important detail, and it gets lost in retellings because it's hidden in spoiler sections with answers to objections. For Florian, the line is drawn not by grade. That is, it's not a question of junior or senior. He explicitly states that he would prefer to work with two juniors who are trying to understand rather than just produce code, than with seniors who have simply given up and stopped trying to understand the code. Quote:

I have also worked with senior developers who basically gave up and stopped trying to understand the code. They became much worse engineers as a result.

It's not about hiring expensive people. It's about hiring those who want to understand the code at the level of the entire project.

I read this and thought: the diagnosis is correct, the division is correct, but what follows from it is more complex for us. Not because we in Russia are special, but because the market is structured differently, and this is evident in the numbers. But I'll start not with the market, but with what I have measured, not just an opinion - the author is not entirely right.

First, Where Florian is Right

His strongest metaphor is not about code, but about technical debt:

You don’t see the debt. You just see the car that looks great.

You don't see the debt, you see a car that looks great. The car drives, it's clearly expensive, and what it cost will be revealed later. Earlier, he mentions that the expensive car was bought on a credit card. The interest on this debt, I think, everyone understands.

And here, I have not just a feeling, but a measurement: we gave an AI agent the same task on two different stacks and checked the results manually. From this, we got two insights that form the basis of the second half of this article, so I will elaborate on them.

First: agents often structurally fail to see their own errors, for example, in access rights. They test their work as an administrator because that's the access they have. A role-specific bug is fundamentally invisible from an admin session: under administrator privileges, restrictions don't trigger because they don't apply to the administrator. Agents aren't lying when they write "rights work, tested." They simply cannot physically see that they don't work.

Second: the same loophole in access rights appeared in the experiment on a clean stack every single time without exception. Not sometimes. Every time.

This is exactly what Florian writes about, but in the form of a benchmark: the car looks great, drives fast, and passes superficial functional acceptance testing. The debt is not visible. But in reality, the code doesn't work as it should (under a regular user), and the tests are green. How many similar defects are in the code of a weak engineer? How many incorrect decisions? How many other pieces of code will break?

But before we go further, let's look at the labor market: it's precisely what explains why the same diagnosis leads to a different treatment for us.

This is no longer a candidate's market, but a fight for a job.

And at the same time, all IT industry reviews repeat the same thing: the shortage of strong expertise has not disappeared. At the bottom, there's a queue of people wanting to get in; at the top, there's still no one to hire (everyone is busy). This is not a contradiction, but a description of the very stratification by expertise level that Florian talks about.

Juniors Aren't Being Hired, But the Reason is Different Here

It's even tougher at the junior positions, and this is now measured not by resumes per vacancy, but by unanswered applications:

A hundred applications without a single response is the average statistic for the junior market in 2026

Here, we and Florian are in agreement, and it's worth stating directly because his point is often misinterpreted at this stage. He is not happy that entry into the profession is closing. He writes that the industry, by reducing entry-level positions, is sabotaging its own future, and adds: fewer people learning now means fewer people capable of maintaining systems down the line.

By reducing the number of entry-level engineering roles, the industry is sabotaging its own future. Fewer people learning means fewer people capable of maintaining systems down the line.

And then he adds a cold observation:

But the industry does not really owe people opportunities.

In fact, the industry owes no one any opportunities. And that's true. However, the bill for this debt comes to us faster than to him, and I'll explain why.

First, a clarification on the reasons. Florian's logic is as follows: AI has taken over junior tasks, so juniors are no longer being hired. In our country, the market for beginners began to shrink before AI agents became widespread in development. And the reason was different: the market was overheated by "get into IT" courses, resulting in a significantly larger number of graduates than available positions. The departure of large custom development vendors, who left their own interns and ready juniors in a shrinking market, also contributed.

The difference is not cosmetic. If it were only about AI, one might expect demand to return when the hype subsides. But demand won't return: there were few entry-level positions even before any automation. This means the sabotage of the future, which Florian writes about, started a couple of years earlier for us and goes deeper.

Work in Russia is of a Different Nature, and This is Key

Florian writes about a world where teams primarily build new products. In our country, the bulk of engineering work is currently elsewhere.

More than half of companies are still on SAP. Typical ERP migration takes tens of months - the ERP import substitution review for August 2026 states it this way:

58% of companies still remain on SAP

a typical migration takes 18–36 months

Haulmont replaces foreign systems with domestic ones: SAP, Salesforce, Pega, IBM FileNet, Lotus Notes, Documentum, Alfresco, OpenText, SharePoint, Visual FoxPro, and so on. And the difficulty there is not in writing new code:

Replacing foreign software can be complex, as the foreign system has been used and developed for several years, key business processes are tied to it, a large volume of historical data has been accumulated, and unique customizations have been implemented.

These "unique customizations" are precisely the work that cannot be accelerated by typing speed. In numbers, it looks like this: replacing CRM at EuroChem - over 650 features in eighteen months. Such systems are backed by hundreds of man-years of development, and before replacing them, one must understand what exactly has accumulated within them.

Hundreds of man-years. Not "drafting a service over a weekend."

This is work where one must understand a twenty-year-old third-party solution, figure out why it was done that way, and reproduce its behavior, including aspects that were never documented and which accounting considers sacred. The speed of writing new code is not the bottleneck here at all. The bottleneck is understanding, and it's not the programmer who hits it, but the one who understands how the system has worked all these twenty years.

And AI helps the least with this kind of work, and is most willing to cause harm: it will explain to you with equal confidence both the real logic of the legacy system and the logic it just invented itself.

Where I Beg to Differ with the Author

Florian concludes that value is shifting to people who understand the system:

At some point, someone still has to know what is going on. And that’s the most valuable person on the team.

At some point, someone still has to understand what's going on, and that's the most valuable person on the team. And he suggests selecting such people based on their attitude towards work: hire those who want to understand, regardless of their grade.

It's impossible to argue with this, and I won't. I will argue with the idea that this is sufficient.

First objection, arithmetic. You cannot hire the right attitude in the required quantity. The path from junior to senior is measured in years, not months, entry into the profession has narrowed, and the volume of work on legacy replacement is measured in hundreds of man-years. Even if every company tomorrow started selecting exclusively curious individuals, their total number in the country wouldn't change (I hope). They would simply redistribute among employers, increasing their own price. As a hiring policy for one company, this works. As a response to a market with a shortage, it doesn't work at all.

Second objection, and it's more interesting. The desire to understand is useless if the tool provides false information.

And here I have data that is not in the original article.

Go back to the research mentioned at the beginning of the article: the agent checks rights as an administrator and therefore doesn't see role-specific bugs. Now add to it the most motivated engineer you can imagine. They open the agent's report and read: rights implemented, tested. The report doesn't lie in form; all checks (tests, Playwright, etc.) have indeed passed. To doubt it, the engineer needs to know in advance that they need to check differently, by logging in with each role separately.

Curiosity prompts asking questions. It doesn't prompt which specific question to ask in a system whose structure you don't yet know. Experience prompts this, but experience is precisely those years we don't have.

Here, it's worth immediately addressing an objection I would raise myself. Can't you train the agent to check under each role? Yes, you can, and that's the right approach: give it accounts, define the procedure, require a report for each role separately. But notice what happened. The problem was solved not by someone becoming more attentive, but by introducing a procedure that works regardless of how attentive the performer is. This is exactly what I propose as an answer to the question "what to do when there aren't enough people?"

And one more twist, which is only hinted at in Florian's text. He praises juniors who use AI to understand, not just to vibecod write code. For us, this takes on additional meaning: if entry into the profession is blocked, and a mentor isn't provided with the offer because there's no offer, then AI remains the only available interlocutor for someone learning on their own. The tool that reduced the number of entry positions turns out to be the only entry for those who still manage to get in. We'll find out how well this works in about five years, when these people start fixing what we're currently migrating from SAP.

What This Means for an Individual

Briefly, as this is a topic for a separate discussion.

If you are in the middle. Your protection is not in typing faster than an agent; you lost that race last year, if not earlier. It's in being the person who understands the system as a whole: why it is the way it is, what will break if it's changed, where the catch is, so to speak. Notice that the fastest-growing role in the table above is called "architect," not "developer who writes a lot of code."

If you are hiring. Select based on the desire to understand; Florian is entirely right about this, and a junior with such an attitude is more valuable than a burned-out senior. But don't think that solves the problem: the desire to understand doesn't replace rules that make errors visible without epiphany.

If you are working with agents. Three rules that cost nothing and don't depend on who is on the team today:

  • Rights are checked by logging in under each role, not under the administrator. This is not "desirable"; it's the only way to see a role-specific defect. An agent structurally cannot do this on its own unless given accounts and required to provide a report for each role separately.

  • Acceptance is based on a list written before the task. Otherwise, the acceptor reads the performer's report and finds exactly what the performer decided to show.

  • At least one scenario is checked bypassing the interface. A hidden button looks like a working rights restriction only until the first direct API request.

These three rules have a common weakness: they require monitoring. A rule that a person executes is different from a rule that executes itself.

And here, one should look not at people, but at the tool. The first rule exists because rights in most projects are scattered throughout the code: a check in the controller, another in the service, a third hidden in the condition for rendering a button. To answer the question "who can see what here," one needs to read a dozen places - both the agent and the human equally cannot keep this in mind.

However, if rights in your stack are declared, not hand-written in each handler, the question changes. The answer to "what this role can see" lies in one place; it can be read in its entirety and checked automatically, rather than figured out by logging in with each account and clicking through the interface. Role-specific defects then don't hide - there's nowhere to hide them.

As mentioned at the beginning, I work for a company that makes exactly this kind of tool, so consider this a statement from an interested party. Yes, yes, the crow praises its own swamp, I'm aware. But it can be verified without me, and you should check not the advertisement, but your own code: open it and count how many files you'd need to open to answer what exactly a user with a specific narrow role can see. If it's more than one, you have the same problem that the agent won't show you.

And if you are deciding whether to hire juniors. Our market differs from the Western one in that we still have legacy systems to maintain for several more years. This will be done by the generation that is not being hired today. "The industry owes no one anything" sounds convincing right up until the point where, in five years, there's no one left to fix the systems we are currently migrating from SAP.

Where I Might Be Wrong

Figures on vacancies from different sources. "Minus 36%" is February to February according to hh.ru; in other reviews for the same period, "around minus 20%" is mentioned using a different calculation method. The direction is the same - downwards, the magnitude depends on what is considered an IT vacancy.

My measurement is one model, one task, one domain. CRUD with roles and rights is precisely the type of task where a strict stack wins. The result might differ for another task.

I am not neutral - this was stated at the beginning. I'll only add the least favorable number for me: in terms of money, we found no difference between stacks in our measurement. I predict a significant increase in cost when delving deeper into the task in favor of Jmix. We plan to continue the benchmark, but haven't gotten around to it yet. Stay tuned, as they say.

The cause-and-effect relationship regarding juniors is my interpretation. The data shows that hiring junior developers has fallen; that it has fallen precisely due to the overheating of courses, not due to AI, does not strictly follow from these figures, although the chronology suggests it.

I am arguing with part of the author's position, not the whole. Florian does not call for abandoning juniors and does not reduce selection to grade, which is important because his text is often retold that way. My argument is precisely about one thing: is selection based on attitude towards work sufficient when there are physically not enough people?

Instead of a Conclusion

Florian is right about the main point: AI has indeed accelerated code production and not its understanding, so the cost of misunderstanding has increased. He is also right that people should be divided by their desire to understand, not by a line on their resume. Plus, in our country today, I am sure you can find not just dozens, but even a hundred seniors on HH with completely fabricated but unverifiable experience. Unfortunates got into the wrong place at the wrong time. Those who understood, understood.

We have a different task. Our middle tier is supported by those very systems that will need to be migrated for several more years, and there is no one to replace this middle tier: there is a shortage of seniors, entry into the profession has narrowed to a crack, and the volume of legacy is measured in man-centuries.

Therefore, the question to ask is not "how to hire those who don't make mistakes," or even "how to select the curious." It is: how to organize work so that the errors of an ordinary person and an ordinary agent become visible before users find them, and so that no one needs to be particularly clever for this.

This is less exciting than talking about the elite. But it doesn't require a scarce resource, meaning it's available to everyone, not just a dozen companies that can overpay.
