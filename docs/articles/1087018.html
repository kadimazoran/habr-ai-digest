A Short Prologue: 8th–12th Centuries. When Knowledge Crossed Language Borders

Before the main chronology, it is worth noting one important prologue. The translation centers of Baghdad during the Abbasid era show that the acceleration of knowledge begins not only with the creation of new things but also with the reduction of the distance between existing intellectual worlds. In the 8th–10th centuries, large bodies of Greek, Syriac, and Persian works were translated into Arabic; later, part of this corpus entered Latin Europe through the translation centers of the Iberian Peninsula. For our topic, what is important is not the beautiful image of a single "House of Wisdom," but the mechanism itself: translation creates new opportunities for comparison, continuation, and reuse of knowledge.

However, the translation of scientific knowledge immediately reveals a fundamental problem: it is not words that must be transferred, but concepts. If different schools use the same words to denote different constructs, and the same constructs are given different names, then meaning begins to be lost precisely at the border of languages and traditions. Mature scientific cultures therefore create dictionaries, academies, terminology commissions, and standards. This problem is old, but today it has become particularly acute.

Today, new scientific terms, meanings, and local conceptual systems arise faster than their definitions, translations, and correspondences between scientific schools can stabilize. If this gap is not controlled, the researcher begins to lose meaning already when transitioning between languages, disciplines, and cultural traditions.

This problem will return at the end of the article. In the 21st century, artificial intelligence has extremely simplified the transition between languages, but rapid text translation does not guarantee the preservation of the original concept. Therefore, the historical story of the translators of Baghdad is not an excursion into antiquity for us. It is the first appearance of a problem that is once again becoming central today.

1200–1660. Preserving Knowledge and Learning to Reproduce It En Masse

It makes sense to start the main chronology around the year 1200. By this time, the university had already become a stable institution for transmitting complex intellectual traditions. The medieval university was not yet a modern research organization, but it performed a fundamentally important function: knowledge, texts, methods of argumentation, and curricula ceased to depend entirely on a single holder and gained institutional continuity. The next generation could start not from scratch.

The limitation remained the manuscript form. Books were expensive and slow to copy, the number of copies was small, and the dissemination of complex texts was limited. The printing press in the mid-15th century drastically changed precisely this part of the system: one intellectual result could be reproduced in a large number of copies and delivered to a much larger number of readers. Printing does not "invent science," but it increases the number of potential continuators for each result and accelerates the circulation of concepts, methods, and debates.

After this, the limitation changes. As long as texts were difficult to obtain, the problem was access. When texts became abundant, another question arose: which ones to trust, how to distinguish a stable observation from an error, and how to ensure that a successful result not only spread but also underwent collective verification. History again shows a familiar pattern: removing one limitation does not complete development but makes the next one visible.

1660–1850. Learning to Collectively Verify and Link Knowledge with Practice

In the 17th century, scientific societies and scientific journals emerged, transforming personal correspondence and individual observations into a more stable collective loop. The Royal Society, founded in 1660, and the "Philosophical Transactions," which began publication in 1665, became part of a system where results could be described, discussed, criticized, and attempted to be reproduced. For the generator of inventions, this was a qualitative enhancement: the system learned not only to produce and disseminate variants but also to discard non-working ones more quickly.

In the 18th and first half of the 19th centuries, another link intensified: theory, instruments, engineering practice, and industrial application increasingly formed a repeating cycle. This does not mean that the Industrial Revolution was a simple consequence of science; it was influenced by markets, energy, capital, trade, institutions, and many other causes. But for our perspective, the loop is significant: theory allows for improvement of the instrument, the instrument provides a new measurement, the measurement changes the theory, and the new theory opens up the next technical possibility.

At this point, a result begins to do more than just solve the current problem. It begins to improve the conditions for obtaining the next result. It is here that it becomes particularly clear why it is useful to consider the history of inventions not only as a sequence of individual achievements but also as a gradual strengthening of the search machine itself.

1840–1914. Reproducing the Research Capability Itself

In the 19th century, the research university and laboratory in the modern sense emerged. By the end of the century, disciplinary programs, laboratory schools, journals, academic degrees, professional societies, and permanent scientific positions were established. This was no longer just about preserving knowledge. Society gained a mechanism for reproducing people professionally trained to create new knowledge.

Professors taught students, some students became researchers, they established their own laboratories, methods were incorporated into training programs, and the new generation started from the accumulated level. Scientific inquiry became parallel, specialized, and institutionally reproducible. For the first time, the research capability itself became a massively supported social resource, rather than a rare combination of talent, patronage, and personal library.

And again, the field of continuations itself changed. If the training of a new researcher is turned into a stable process, then each obtained result can potentially be continued not by one student or accidental follower, but by an entire generation of specialists.

1900–1970. Organizing Large-Scale Research

In the early 20th century, the corporate research laboratory became an important new mechanism. Large companies established permanent scientific divisions and linked research with invention, product development, manufacturing, and feedback from operation. Research ceased to be solely an external source of ideas: it became an internal capability of the organization to create the next generation of its own technology.

During and after World War II, an even more powerful loop emerged – government scientific and technical programs. Universities, industry, defense and medical laboratories, large-scale funding, and thousands of specialists could work within a common program. The Manhattan Project, space programs, and major medical projects are important here not as methods of invention, but as examples of how powerful the organization of research becomes when it has a huge number of people, time, instruments, computations, and material resources.

This era is particularly important for today's question. In a large program, a researcher is not required to personally perform the entire cycle. One team handles literature, another handles calculations, a third handles experiments, a fourth handles testing, and a fifth analyzes results. There are project managers, archives, standards, internal libraries, and procedures for transferring results. The quality of the research process is ensured not only by the intellect of a specific scientist but also by the enormous organizational apparatus around them.

This is fundamental. When we discuss methods of scientific research today, it is easy to forget that many 20th-century practices were formed within systems where an individual researcher was merely one element of a large machine. They had colleagues, infrastructure, and the opportunity to delegate part of the task to another specialist.

1960–2015. Making Knowledge Digital, Networked, and Almost Instantly Accessible

The next transition was created by computing networks. Computers accelerated calculations, and networks allowed data, programs, models, and other digital objects to be transferred between organizations. The result of one group quickly became a tool for another, and part of the knowledge acquired an executable form: it was possible to transfer not only the description of an algorithm but also the code that implemented it.

Then came the World Wide Web. The Internet connected networks, and the World Wide Web made documents and other information objects linked and addressable. Scientific publications, datasets, documentation, or software libraries could now be found and accessed in minutes. The cost of information transfer dropped sharply, and the distance between the creator of a result and its potential continuator decreased.

After this, scarcity again changed its form. Previously, a researcher suffered from a lack of information; now, they increasingly suffer from the inability to process its volume. There are more publications, data, languages, scientific schools, and related disciplines than one person can encompass.

And here arises a strange historical turn. Humanity has built a colossal external memory, but the individual researcher again finds themselves facing a personal problem: how to extract precisely what is relevant to their task from this memory?

2015–2026. Great Research Power Is Once Again in the Hands of an Individual

Since the mid-2010s, artificial intelligence has emerged, beginning to perform some of the cognitive operations of the search itself. It can help find publications on a specific topic, read and compare large bodies of text, translate scientific terminology, compare ways of describing the same object across different disciplines, write and analyze program code, build computational models, and propose working hypotheses.

For the first time, an individual has access to an intellectual amplifier of this scale without being part of a huge state or corporate research program.

And here lies the paradox, especially noticeable to the Habr audience. A modern researcher often works alone or in a small team of two or three people. They do not have a scientific information department, dozens of laboratories, a separate translation group, their own computing center, or a multi-year budget. But with the help of AI, they can explore dozens of subject areas, languages, and possible research directions in a single day.

The input power has sharply increased. The organizational apparatus around the researcher has not.

Therefore, the quality of the search becomes not a preparatory operation that can be done "as it turns out," but one of the main conditions for the result. If irrelevant sources, incorrect translations, outdated information, or mixed concepts are fed in, artificial intelligence is not obliged to automatically detect the problem. On the contrary, it can process poor input material faster and more convincingly.

The old engineering principle "garbage in, garbage out" does not disappear in the era of artificial intelligence. It becomes more dangerous.

However, qualitative search is potentially infinite. One can always find another article, another language, another scientific school, another related discipline, or another verification. Therefore, it is not enough for an individual researcher to have a good search tool. They need to understand what exactly is still worth searching for, how much time can be spent on it, and when the research should be stopped.

For research work, this is a fundamental formulation.

The search should not be maximal, but sufficient to obtain a well-founded result within the available time horizon.

A limited resource is not just money. It is the time until publication or practical solution, the researcher's attention, computational resources, experimental accessibility, the possibility of testing a hypothesis, and the cost of pursuing another side branch. Each additional week of search must be justified by its potential to change the conclusion, open up a truly new option, or significantly increase the reliability of the result. Otherwise, research turns into an endless accumulation of materials instead of progress towards a verifiable outcome.

The problem of terminology also returns here. Artificial intelligence allows one to switch in minutes from a Russian-language engineering school to an English-language article, then to a Chinese publication or an Indian research tradition. However, machine translation only partially removes the language barrier. The researcher still needs to understand whether the same concepts are behind similar words, whether the boundary of the term has changed, and whether the found construct is indeed transferable to another subject area.

In the era of artificial intelligence, it is not enough for a researcher to quickly find and translate information. They must be able to preserve meaning when transitioning between languages, disciplines, and scientific schools; otherwise, the speed of search grows faster than the quality of understanding.

There is another problem – the cooperation of small research groups. If one person manages sources and terms within one coordinate system, another within another, and a third cannot reconstruct the origin of the first two's conclusions, then combining their work becomes almost as expensive as re-researching. This means that small groups need more than just a common chat and a folder of files. They need a compatible research memory: subject boundaries, origin of statements, terminology used, competing versions, and the ability to continue someone else's branch without fully repeating the entire path.

A separate problem is competing explanations. Artificial intelligence can very quickly make the first plausible hypothesis extremely convincing. Therefore, it is necessary to preserve alternative versions and ask not only "which one looks better?" but also "what fact can truly distinguish these explanations?". In "The Firm's Brain," this discipline is formulated precisely this way: contradictions and different versions should not be erased until a result capable of distinguishing them appears.

And finally, human choice remains. The machine can collect sources, translate texts, find connections, highlight contradictions, and help build variants. But it should not imperceptibly replace the researcher's decision about what evidence is sufficient, what risk of error is acceptable, where to direct limited time, and when further search is no longer justified.

What Has Truly Accelerated in This History

If we assemble the chosen trajectory as a whole, it is not a list of great inventions, but a sequence of enhancements to the ability to obtain the next one. The university preserved and transmitted intellectual tradition. The printing press expanded the number of potential continuators. Scientific societies and journals created collective verification. The research laboratory reproduced researchers themselves. The corporate laboratory linked research with technological implementation. The government scientific and technical program provided scale and specialization. The computing network accelerated the transfer of executable results. The World Wide Web created a globally addressable memory. Artificial intelligence transferred part of the operations of this generator directly to the individual researcher.

This is precisely why it is useful to talk about a generator of inventions. It is not a specific organization or a single methodology, but a combination of memory, language, specialists, verification rules, tools, computations, institutions, and resources upon which the path from a question to a new working result depends. History shows that the power of such a generator grew unevenly: each enhancement removed one limitation and made the next one visible.

Today, a particularly interesting turn has occurred. A large part of the informational and partially analytical power of the generator has become available to a person who does not have the apparatus of a large research program. In the past, a significant part of organizational discipline was provided by the structure itself: departments, specialization, archives, verification procedures, and work distribution. Now, an individual researcher receives a significant part of the intellectual power of this system on one computer but must organize the search, terminology, verification, time, and stopping of the research themselves.

And this is no longer a historical question.
