How the idea for the platform was born

Initially, searching for growing products on marketplaces was almost entirely manual. Category managers and analysts individually studied MPStats, Wordstat, product cards, compared sales, reviews, search demand, and availability, and then decided whether to add a product to the assortment or develop it.

If it was necessary to analyze not one card, but a whole category, the work took several hours. Moreover, the problem was not only in speed: on different platforms, the same product can be called differently, cards can be duplicated, SKUs don't match, and demand growth often becomes noticeable too late, when the opportunity has already been missed.

This led to errors:

  • the same product was considered different entities on different platforms;

  • promising products were lost in the noise of similar cards;

  • decisions were made too late;

  • some trends were noticed only after they became popular with buyers.

Ready-made services partially covered individual tasks but did not give us a complete picture. One tool could show sales, another — search demand, a third — stock levels or reviews, but connecting it into a single workflow was difficult.

We lacked a system that helps make a decision: what exactly is growing, why it is growing, and how much it can be trusted.

Why existing methods didn't work

We tried to assemble the process from ready-made services: we downloaded data, compiled it into tables, and analyzed it. But we realized that the main problem is not in collecting information, but in correctly correlating data from different sources.

The same product on different platforms could have different names, different descriptions, or even be split into several cards. While an analyst manually sorted this out, the market had already managed to change. And for trending products, speed is critically important. Therefore, we decided to develop our own platform.

How we searched for a solution

  • we needed to combine identical products from different platforms into a single entity;

  • classify products by category;

  • track the dynamics of sales, search demand, reviews, and availability;

  • collect all this in one place so that a manager could understand: is the product growing, stagnating, or already losing audience interest.

We tried these approaches:

  • rules based on regular expressions and dictionaries;

  • strict manual normalization;

  • classic ML approaches without LLM;

  • LLM approach for semantic merging and classification.

The weakest option turned out to be just rules. They work for simple cases but break down on live e-commerce content, which has a lot of noise, synonyms, abbreviations, and marketing jargon.

Classic ML models helped better, but they also didn't solve the problem completely, especially where it was necessary to understand the meaning of the product name, not just compare strings.

As a result, we kept a hybrid approach: rules — where strict logic is needed; ML — where there are repeatable patterns; LLM — where semantic understanding and merging of similar but not identical objects is required.

Why LLM was chosen

We needed LLM for tasks where conventional methods started to get confused. The most illustrative example is merging product cards. It sounds simple in words, but in reality, different platforms write the same thing differently, and sometimes a card lacks some attributes.

We compared several options:

  • rules only;

  • embeddings + cosine similarity;

  • classic ranking model;

  • LLM with additional constraints and checks.

The embeddings option was not bad as a preliminary filter, but in complex cases, it confused similar products. This was especially true for categories with many closely related items: different sizes, configurations, sets, series, versions of the same model. A classic ranking model also helped, but it lacked flexibility with non-standard phrasing.

LLM proved more useful because it can consider the meaning of the name, attributes, context, and indirect signs simultaneously.

Architecture and approach

From a technical point of view, the platform solves the problem of: combining disparate marketplace data into a unified, analyzable model.

Here's what it consists of:

1. Merging product cards (entity resolution)

The same product exists in the system under a dozen different names and SKUs on different platforms. You can't combine this with a simple SQL join, so we used LLM to match cards: it semantically compares products and merges them into a single "global product." From then on, all metrics — sales, reviews, dynamics — are calculated not per card, but for the product as a whole.

Solution:

  • LLM is used for semantic product matching;

  • cards from different marketplaces are aggregated;

  • all cards are merged into a single record.

Result: all metrics (sales, reviews, dynamics) are calculated at the level of the actual product, not an individual card.

2. Categorization and mapping to the catalog

One of the first tasks is to understand which category a product belongs to on each platform. In practice, this is not always obvious: the same product on Ozon, Wildberries, and Yandex.Market can have different names, different attributes, and even different markup logic. Therefore, our categorization is built not on a single feature, but on a set of signals:

  • name;

  • description;

  • characteristics;

  • context from the card.

Here, LLM helps to combine all this into a stable classification, rather than relying on rigid rules. As a result, the same product falls into comparable categories, and you can then look not at the chaos of cards, but at a normal picture for the segment.

3. Availability check

Next, it's important to understand not only that the product exists, but also how visible it is in the market. Sometimes a product is in the catalog, but it's impossible to find it through search, it's poorly represented regionally, or it's deep in the search results.

To do this, we check availability on different platforms and compare where the product is actually available and where it is effectively not for sale.

This layer of analysis helps distinguish a product that has started to grow from a product that simply appeared in the catalog once. Thus, you can see where there is a shortage, where the card gets lost in the search results, and where a competitor's product has already taken a prominent position.

4. Search demand analysis

We separately look at how customer interest is changing in search. Unlike sales, search demand shows not only the fact of purchase but also what people are starting to search for more often than before. This allows us to identify trends even before the growth becomes noticeable in revenue.

We collect search suggestions, query frequencies, and interest dynamics for key phrases to understand which categories are gaining momentum. If queries are growing, and sales are just catching up, it's a good signal to pay attention to the product before competitors do.

5. Time series analysis

Once the basic data is collected, the next step is to look at it over time. It's important not just to see that the product is selling, but to understand whether it's a one-time spike, a seasonal effect, or sustainable growth. For this, we build time series for sales, demand, and other metrics to track dynamics and identify trends.

Then, ML models help separate noise from real movement. As a result, you can not only describe what is happening now but also estimate where the product is going next: growth, plateau, or decline.

As a result, the manager receives not scattered numbers for each platform, but a single product card with a ready verdict: continue to grow, test, or remove from the shelf.

Product description: what's on the main screen

The main workspace is the "Financial Indicators" page. It displays a list of products that can be filtered by category, sales, revenue, reviews, search queries, rating, and trend stage.

Filters work on an intersection principle: a product remains in the results only if it meets all conditions simultaneously. This is convenient when you need not just to compile a long list, but to find a specific segment, for example, products with sales above 1000 units and a rating of 4.5 or higher.

How the system understands that a product is growing

One of the most interesting parts of the platform is determining the trend stage. The system assigns a product to one of five phases: emergence, growth, seasonal growth, explosive growth, or decline.

From a practical point of view, this is very useful. If a product is in the "Growth" or "Explosive Growth" phase, it's worth looking closer: demand is already confirmed, and the window of opportunity is still open. If the product is moving into "Decline," it's a reason to lower expectations or exclude it from consideration altogether.

What dynamics show

The platform can compare data in several modes: year-over-year, period-over-period, and through manual selection of intervals. This allows us to separate seasonality from real changes in demand and look at the product not statically, but in context.

For example, a year-over-year comparison helps understand if the category is growing long-term, while a period-over-period comparison helps detect a rapid surge in demand. This is critical for product analytics: the same product can look good in the short term and completely different on a year-long horizon.

Product card as the next step

If a product is of interest, it can be opened from the table and go to the product card. More detailed information is available there: photos, marketplace ratings, sales, reviews, queries, and dynamics for the selected period.

For products available in "Lenta," competitive analysis with prices from other retail chains is additionally shown. This is already similar to a normal workflow for a commercial team: they looked at demand, compared it with competitors, evaluated the price, and only then made a decision.

SKU search and verification

In addition to the main table, there is a separate search by keywords and an SKU verification screen. This is useful when you need to quickly find a specific product or check a list of items without manually browsing the entire database.

In the SKU verification, you can upload a list of products in xls format, up to 100 items at a time. This mode is convenient for category managers and analysts who need to quickly compare their own list with market data.

Product results

To date, the platform has already been integrated into the SKU introduction workflow and is used as the main tool for finding products and making decisions about their introduction.

  1. Analysis time has been reduced. If previously it took hours for manual compilation by category, now a preliminary assessment takes no more than 10 minutes. This means that a category manager spends less time collecting data and more time on the decision itself.

  2. The number of decisions that can be made in one window has increased. The platform helps not just to see a product, but to immediately understand what to do with it: test, strengthen, monitor, or exclude.

  3. We have found products that, without automated search, might not have made it into the assortment. This is important for us, as sometimes one early signal of demand growth has more impact than a late and expensive assortment expansion.

  4. Team workload has decreased. What previously required manual cross-referencing of several sources is now done automatically, and a person only gets involved in disputed cases.

Today, the tool is used by over 200 employees of the commercial department. With its help, more than 150 trending SKUs (which they call "treats" in the company) have already been selected and launched. The project has confirmed its effectiveness, ensuring a 10% increase in RTO.

Product development plans

We continue to develop the platform and plan to expand its functionality by parsing foreign marketplaces and enriching data. This will allow us to identify trending positions not only in the Russian market but also to find unique products to introduce into our network's assortment.

The first is improving the quality of merging in complex categories with many similar products. The second is a more accurate assessment of early trends, so we can notice growth even before it becomes obvious to everyone.

Conclusion

Looking at the platform as a whole, its value lies in shortening the path from idea to decision. The user doesn't just search for "what's selling," but selects products based on demand growth, quality assessment, dynamics, and competitive position.

The most complex and costly part of the project is the accurate merging of cards and verification of disputed cases using a human-in-the-loop principle. The category manager's trust in the data, or their need to manually re-check it, depends on the quality of this work.

The "AI Trends" tool saves hours on data collection — which means the team has time left for what it was all for: for the hypothesis about the product itself, not for piecing it together.
