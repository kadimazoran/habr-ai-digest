Building a Raspberry Pi 5 Robot for My Daughter: More Than Just a Smart Speaker!

I wanted a robot that could help my child and talk without the internet. This led me to test nearly twenty local AI models, select a voice, add a camera to a bow, and teach its eyes and head to track people. In the first part, I detail how a sketch became a working prototype and what went wrong along the way. Read on.

I wanted to make a robot assistant for my daughter with local AI, so she wouldn't get distracted from her studies but would instead engage with them enthusiastically.

But I had nothing to start with. I only had an idea: to create a robot for my daughter that she could talk to even without the internet. And I wanted it to not look like just another speaker with a clock or a black face with blue eyes. So, the first thing I did was create an animated face on my PC – a standard robotic one, without hair, a bow, or other embellishments; I wanted something appealing.

I showed it to my daughter. She looked at it and asked, "Can it be an anime catgirl?"

Thus, the initial robot began to transform into a character. I started asking her what exactly she liked: eyes, hairstyle, clothes, facial expressions. The first version wasn't enough. Her ideas were quite specific, and it turned out that just sticking ears onto a screen wasn't sufficient. As a result, two detailed sketches of the character emerged. One with dark hair and a pink kimono, the other in lighter colors. From that moment on, I was no longer building an abstract robot, but a little companion for my child.

From Image to a Living Face

I started with what could be tested without hardware. I wrote a Python application with a screen-based face: eyes, pupils, eyebrows, mouth, blinking. I added a smile and several expressions. When the robot spoke a response, the mouth should move with the sound. If the program freezes for a long time between a question and an answer, no amount of pretty eyes can save it: the child will think it's broken or, worse, just ignoring them. So, gradually, the face acquired states like "listening," "thinking," and normal calm expressions. But I also kept the other, earlier faces, which can be changed in the settings.

For the interface, I used Python and Tkinter. I separated the face rendering from voice processing and model response: waiting for the network or text generation shouldn't stop the blinking. This separation later proved useful for head movement as well.

Concurrently, I realized that animation alone wasn't enough. The robot needed a brain, and a local one at that. But what should I build it on? Arduino wasn't suitable for this kind of dialogue. I looked for a single-board computer, compared capabilities, saw what people had already launched and on what, and found a Raspberry Pi 5 with 8GB of RAM on Avito at a reasonable price and ordered it. I wanted to run a model of about 4 billion parameters on it. While the package was en route, I continued writing the program on my PC and selecting a model.

Twenty (!!!) Models and One Small Computer

I downloaded about twenty LLM candidates. Some were quickly eliminated: some poorly maintained the assigned character, others gave unclear answers to simple questions or got confused in long responses. For the remaining ones, I assembled a set of tests – arithmetic, explanations, coherent speech, following instructions. The "smartest" answer in a separate test wasn't a victory: the child still had to wait to hear it aloud.

In the finals, I was left with four GGUF models. On the Pi 5, the primary choice became Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507: in my set, it offered the best balance of quality and response time. Gemma_4_E2B responded faster and was useful for short conversations. Gemma_4_E4B performed better in some explanations but was slower. Qwen3.5_9B_DeepSeek_Distill I kept as a backup experiment: it's interesting, but for a small robot, its long responses seemed excessively time-consuming (I even thought it had frozen).

In one of the tests on the Pi itself, the generation speed was approximately 4.3 tokens/s for Qwen3 4B and 7.1 tokens/s for Gemma E2B. These are measurements from my specific task set and settings, not a universal model ranking. For a child, the difference is simply felt: they ask a question – they wait. Therefore, a fast answer is sometimes more useful than a slightly more detailed one.

I also kept the speech local. Vosk recognized the microphone input, and Piper voiced the response. The basic "Irina" voice was good, but my daughter's character needed a more cartoonish or even anime-like sound. I didn't train a separate voice model, as I didn't have the time: I slowed down the original synthesis and changed the pitch of the existing sound. In the preset, it looks like this:
