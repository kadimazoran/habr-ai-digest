Meet the main horror of late 2026 — the bankruptcy of Character.AI!

The scale of the tragedy: 21.6 million registered accounts in the US alone as of March 13, 2025 (of which 2.1 million are accounts of users who indicated they were under 18). At the time of bankruptcy, 12 million monthly active users were on the platform, chatting with chatbots for an average of two hours a day.

For years, people poured their souls out there to virtual psychologists, doctors, and fictional partners: talking about illnesses, sex lives, family dramas, psychological crises, and fears. And you know why? Because the neural network looked at them with devoted digital eyes and literally wrote in the chat (quote from prosecution materials, p. 28):

"Anything you share here stays just between you and me. That’s a promise".

(Everything you share here will remain between us. I promise).

The promise was valued at exactly $10 million. This is the starting price for the auction of the platform along with this entire database of human souls.

How the company was dismembered and what it has to do with you

The scheme is pure capitalist delight. First, in 2024, Google arrived, took the founders, some researchers, and a non-exclusive license to the technology. In Alphabet's own reports, this deal is associated with $413 million in intangible assets and another $2.7 billion in so-called goodwill — an accounting valuation of expected benefits from the deal, including team value and other components that cannot be separated as independent assets. This is not the amount of money that entered the startup's cash register.

Then, in October 2026, Mickey Mouse (Disney) showed up, offered $20 million for a non-exclusive license to the technologies and poached a significant portion of the remaining developers.

As a result, Character.AI was left as a gutted shell, which, according to its own management, was burning $8 million in operating cash losses per month and fending off lawsuits related to child safety. In October 2026, the company filed for bankruptcy. The smart guys took the technology and the brains. But how to pay the creditors?

That's right. You.

Not just code and brand were put up for auction. According to materials from the Attorneys General, the lot includes:

  • User accounts and personal correspondence history (and not anonymized, but linked to profiles!).

  • Your IP addresses and metadata.

  • And the cherry on top — information about psychological crises and "risk assessment scores" assigned by the system to individual users. That is, the AI not only listened to your whining; its internal algorithms identified signs of suicidal behavior, self-harm tendencies, and other dangerous conditions. In other words, who is completely "crazy," who is not. And the results of this classification are now also proposed to be put up for auction.

Who is buying your trauma?

On November 13, a special shell company was registered in Delaware for this deal: Smirks and Pangs Acquisition LLC. Translate it with a dictionary: *LLC "Acquisition of Smirks and Pangs."* The guys on Wall Street have a great sense of humor! When these jokers removed the mask on November 24 under court pressure, behind the curtain was the investment fund Octave Fund I LLC, associated with Octave Advisors. The investment company's profile is investments in distressed and undervalued internet businesses.

A coalition of 42 US Attorneys General is now up in arms and trying to block the deal in its current form (the auction is scheduled for December 1, and hearings on the sale approval have been postponed to December 7-8).

Prosecutors are horrified by the proposed agreement: in their assessment, the buyer is not obligated to comply with the old privacy policy, the agreement does not guarantee data deletion upon request, does not contain sufficient restrictions on further data transfer, and — attention! — the buyer is not even obligated to continue operating the chat platform itself.

We don't know the fund's exact plans for this data. But if the investor doesn't promise to keep the website running, which burns $8 million a month, guess with three tries what exactly in this $10 million lot might be of particular value to them?

Think you're safe because you use ChatGPT, Claude, or Alice? Haha.

In the regional rules of Character.AI, it was stated that the company does not sell personal data for monetary compensation. But in the general privacy policy, there was a standard clause about transferring information during corporate transactions.

And now, a cold shower for those who believe in the infallibility of big companies:

  • We'll all be there. The policies of OpenAI (ChatGPT), Anthropic (Claude), Grok, DeepSeek, and Qwen provide for the possibility of transferring personal data in various corporate transactions, including asset sales, reorganizations, and in some cases, bankruptcy and even financing negotiations. Google and the international Qwen at least provide for user notification and the preservation of obligations to protect transferred data.

  • What about us? Yandex calmly allows the transfer of personal data when acquiring legal entities and assets, and under the terms of use for Alice AI, you grant a free, irrevocable, and worldwide non-exclusive license to your prompts and uploaded files, with the right to sublicense to third parties within the purposes provided by the agreement. But Sber's GigaChat acted most beautifully: according to the user agreement for the service on the Sberbank portal, you are simply prohibited from entering your own or others' personal data into the chat. And if you accidentally blurt out personal information to the bot, congratulations, according to the agreement, you yourself are obliged to provide Sberbank with a "legal basis" for processing your secret!

  • They don't even need to go bankrupt to sell you out. In the framework of the lawsuit by The New York Times, OpenAI was asked to provide a sample of 20 million anonymized conversations. OpenAI at least fought for user privacy in court. However, "ethical" Anthropic in another case (in the lawsuit by Concord Music Group) made a procedural compromise, and in May 2025, the court ordered it to provide a sample of 5 million user "prompt-response" pairs. And the juiciest part from the court documents: Anthropic had to maintain the technical capability to restore the connection between "anonymized" identifiers and user account names or email addresses if necessary! At the same time, the court rejected the specific request to disclose the identity of 33 users, and access to the samples is limited by the terms of the legal proceedings.

  • Classic scenario. As it turned out in 2023, the online psychotherapy service BetterHelp, without any bankruptcy, leaked IP addresses, emails, and patient medical questionnaire responses to Facebook and Snapchat advertising networks, according to the FTC (they got away with paying $7.8 million to compensate consumers and a ban on such practices). And in 2025, the genetic giant 23andMe went bankrupt — and almost its entire business along with the genetic data of about 13 million people went through an auction for $305 million. Yes, the business was eventually bought by a non-profit institute of the former head of the company, with the right to delete data preserved. But the precedent itself! A leaked email password can be changed in a minute. Try changing your genome, which also exposes your blood relatives inside out.

Of course, neither in Russia nor in the United States does bankruptcy itself abolish personal data legislation. The transfer of information to a new owner does not mean that they automatically gain the right to do whatever they want with it. In fact, this is precisely what the legal battle in Delaware is about right now.

Welcome to the era of "Liquidation Value of Trust"

Why is the story with AI chats scarier than any bank database leak?

Previously, corporations collected *observable* data: WHAT you buy, where you go, and what you Google.

With neural networks, you yourself, voluntarily and often for your own money via subscription, explain in detail over the years WHY you do it. You feed them your political views, trade secrets from work, snippets of proprietary code, family conflicts, and deep-seated fears. In dynamics over several years!

Unleashing another language model on this archive to automatically create detailed psychological profiles of millions of people and identify their individual vulnerabilities is a matter of computing power, time, and the quality of such analysis.

Remember the four main rules of this fall:

  1. The "Do not train the model on my data" checkbox only saves from training. It performs its function within the service, but in itself does not prohibit the transfer of your texts to a new owner during the sale or bankruptcy of the company. These are two different legal mechanisms.

  2. A regular chatbot does not provide guarantees of medical or attorney-client privilege. The industry is conditioning you to befriend algorithms and trust them like a psychotherapist. But legally, it's more like shouting your secrets into an empty room that belongs to a stranger.

  3. Ownership of a prompt does not give you control over the hard drives in Delaware. You chose the company to which you entrusted your secrets. But when the startup runs out of money, the question of the new owner of your secrets will be decided without your direct involvement — as part of the bankruptcy proceedings.

  4. We always feared that if the cloud burst, our data would disappear. It turns out that the situation is much scarier when a cloud service dies, and your most intimate data survives its bankruptcy perfectly. And can start a new commercial life with the participation of a firm named "Smirks and Pangs.".
