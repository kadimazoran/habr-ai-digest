I love photography. There's something thrilling about looking through the lens, composing a shot, and working on the resulting photo. I've gone through DSLRs and mirrorless cameras until I finally fell in love with the Canon AE-1P, an analog SLR my father used to travel the world with before settling in the US.

Shooting film is simple yet specific: reflected light hits plastic film, burning a chemical image into it. The combination of lighting, film type, and lens creates magically unique compositions.

For years, my workflow looked like this: load film, take photos, send film to the lab, wait for development, pick up film, cut negatives into strips, insert them into a holder, scan each frame with a special scanner, open each file in Darktable, manually invert, make edits, and then decide where to store and/or publish the exported image. At first, this process seemed delightful, but the more film I shot, the more time it took to process all 36 frames.

On average, scanning a 36-frame roll of film and making edits took me about four hours. Working with film requires attention to detail, especially to avoid dust and scratches on the final image. Naturally, this process quickly became tedious. I was spending more time editing photos than shooting them, so I started looking for ways to save time by streamlining operations in my personal digital lab.

Giving Claude the Power to Destroy Hardware

My first hypothesis was that this cycle could be made fully autonomous. Let Claude control the scanner via SANE (an open-source scanner driver stack) and handle the entire scanning, color conversion, and editing process.

On the first run, Claude used SANE with inverted X/Y directions, nearly breaking the gear mechanism inside the scanner. Unfortunately, this forced me to spend time on emergency surgery to save $200 on a new one.

Studio

Fortunately, the failed attempt made me look at the problem from a different angle. I work with VueScan as I always have, so I need to optimize scan processing somehow. After conducting several tests with image data, I created a whole suite of tools to help me with the process.

Negative Conversion

Essentially, converting negatives to positives only requires code. Inversion is implemented as a small numpy program that works with the film's optical density and measures the color characteristics of the entire film before correcting each frame according to the constraints of its negative. This ensures that the 36 frames look uniform across the entire film, and, for example, a sunset isn't subjected to radical color correction. I usually struggled with this even during manual processing. My agent determined that an empty initial film slot would be a good reference for color correction weights relative to the other frames. Before doing anything else, I tested the system on old film to decide whether to continue.

Dust Removal and Editing

Models come to the rescue when code capabilities are exhausted. Dust is detected by analyzing the negative image and marked for removal. This is trickier than it sounds: on a beach shot, the first detector marked about two thousand sun glare spots as dust. The solution was to consider the context and apply processing only where the surrounding area was sufficiently uniform. Detected spots are filled using LaMa (a model for image inpainting). Details from adjacent film areas are added to the inpainted section so it doesn't look blurry. This is similar to the Healing Brush tool in Photoshop.

Not every area needs automatic filling. I set my own threshold where grainy areas can be set aside for review, and then their processing can be confirmed or rejected individually or in batches.

This created a need to adjust the light and color properties of each image considering the grain, so I added convenient controls for exposure, contrast, temperature, and other parameters.

Metadata

Indexing analog film data can be time-consuming, especially if you have to work with multiple separate tools yourself. This was one of those tasks where you think, "Why not?" Now that I'm using technology, I can go further and add the ability to recognize photos to make them easier to search later.

Therefore, when new scans arrive in Ollama, Qwen3-VL runs locally, analyzes each processed frame, and generates a set of tags for it in about five seconds. Combined with data about the camera, lens, shooting date, and film type, this creates a comprehensive photo archive that makes searching for specific images easy.

Now, only the part of the work that I genuinely enjoy remains for me: I study the photos taken and seek inspiration to create new ones.

Gallery

After I figured out the pipeline, the question arose of where to store all the images.

They usually reside on my 4TB HDD, which is always connected to my machine. But now that I've eliminated the need to create corporate software for personal use, I wanted to explore web storage options.

Publishing Process

I have a Cloudflare account that gets activated from time to time by visitors browsing CDN content from my personal website. Is there a better way to get the most out of the free tier than uploading all my photos to my own R2 bucket? So, I expanded the studio to include publishing capabilities.

Displaying Photos by Roll

A simple grid of thumbnails seemed too unappealing. I continued scanning films, remembering how I used to take films for development. When I saw lab technicians unspooling the film itself and feeding it into a C-41 processor, a simple idea came to mind—to recreate the film itself digitally.

I understand this isn't the most realistic way to recreate the process, but the idea turned out to be very engaging, and I wanted to develop it further. As a result, I created a felt table with Three.js modeled film canisters featuring real label images and random scuffs. You pick up a canister, pull out the film, and the frames appear on it. Then the film simply falls onto the table.

My Corner of the Internet

In total, this project took 11 days and required 64 commits. The gallery currently has 22 rolls and 314 photos, the first of which were taken back in 2020.

I will continue to add photos and spend money on film and development. This is my corner of the Internet where visitors can relax.

Creating such a website turned out to be no less engaging for me than working with the scanner and developing film.

The manual process allows a person to derive pleasure, which they won't want to give up for automation. At least for me, it means an opportunity to step away from the computer and focus on enjoying the time I spend exploring the world and capturing it from unusual angles.
