Hi Habr! My name is Ilya Baranov, and I'm a testing lead at Sber Disk. I've been involved in test automation for many years, progressing from simple clickers to modern multi-agent systems.

Let's trace this evolution. I'll emphasize upfront: I'm not presenting the material chronologically, but in the order I encountered and mastered various technologies and products. You might have skipped some of these steps, but I want to share my experience, not just provide a summary of automation tools.

And that's precisely why I won't delve into the technical specifics of each system, but will primarily discuss the problems and tasks I solved using these technologies.

The Era of Clickers

Let's recall the beginning. Selenium IDE, AutoIt, various macro recorders. The principle is simple: record an action, play it back. Probably, most automation engineers have played with these "toys." I remember my indescribable delight when, after installing Selenium IDE for the first time, I could record a script and then play it back. I thought, "I'm a real automation engineer now, I can do anything, time to ask for a raise." Yes, I was naive then and didn't realize I hadn't become an automation engineer, but had only touched automation technologies. Yet, the task before me was quite serious.

At that time, we were developing an import substitution for the BDPN project – a unified database storing all subscriber phone number data in Russia. Mobile network operators access this database when processing requests for number portability, allocating number pools to specific operators, releasing numbers after use, and so on. In short, there are many scenarios, and the system is very critical.

We had just started working when we received the happy news: a deputy minister from Rosvyaz was coming for an inspection. He really wanted to see what we had, and we suddenly realized we didn't have much to show. Well, here are the requirements, here's the backend code, here's the authorization page and the first landing page, and even that's not complete. What to show? And our product owner came up with a brilliant idea: "Let's demonstrate front-end automation. You know, so that autotests run through the pages and fill everything in. It looks good, you can do it, right?" And at that point, I had never even touched interface automation and had only recently transitioned from IT hardware. Hmm, well, if it needs to be done, it needs to be done. I'll do it. After work, I started figuring out how this was done. After studying many articles, I realized that the most convenient and fastest way would be to use a clicker for automation. A couple more evenings to master everything confidently, and soon all our front-end scenarios were covered by autotests. We defended ourselves excellently, received our ceremonial handshakes and pats on the back, and continued the project's development.

But soon I realized that clickers are good for MVPs and quick starts, but they are insufficient for a long-term project. The front-end grew, and test scenarios constantly changed. Maintaining them was impossible; they could only be deleted and rewritten from scratch. It was necessary to find a new automation tool.

The Era of Selenium WebDriver (First Serious Leap)

For further automation, I chose Selenium and Python – probably the most popular combination of language and technology at the time. I tried to migrate the scenarios and realized it was easier to rewrite everything. Well, then the immersion into the stack began, along with simultaneous learning. I wrote 50 scenarios, got a massive script, mastered Page Object. I wrote 200 scenarios, realized they were repetitive, and mastered parameterization. Tests started taking over two hours – we introduced multithreading. Sometimes I learned new techniques and methods so quickly that on Friday, looking at what I had written on Monday, I would think to myself, "What a novice wrote this," and immediately refactor everything with a new architecture. At some point, after covering most of the project with new scenarios, I experienced euphoria. It seemed like anything could be automated with Selenium. Just for fun, I started writing scripts to automate browser games and create various bots. It wasn't as common back then, and it was really cool to dive into this topic.

But after some time, I realized that freedom comes at a price. Selenium is good because you are essentially given an engine, wheels, and a set of spare parts, but what kind of car you get depends solely on you. Moreover, some parts initially come with defects or incompatibilities, but you only find out when the car starts moving (or doesn't). Constant flaky tests, instability of runs on servers, incompatibility of new Chrome builds with Selenium cores, and so on. Often, more time was spent on maintenance than on development. But by this time, the project had already passed its rapid growth phase, and the entire system was working quite stably. Automating over 90% of regression allowed us to study new technologies and made life quite comfortable.

The Era of Frameworks (Playwright and Cypress)

Gradually, the project reached maturity and transitioned to a maintenance status. It became boring, and just in time, I was headhunted by a big tech company. The new project was an ERP system operating in three countries (England, France, Israel). Out of habit, I started automation with Selenium and Python. But after some time, the team we were integrating with proposed switching to Cypress and JS. They already had a framework deployed and some autotests written, and they believed that a single stack for both our teams would be more efficient. So, we started learning the language and the framework. Surprisingly, the learning process went quite quickly, although many JS developers told me that my coding style revealed I was a Pythonista. Cypress immediately impressed and captivated me with its ease of learning. Literally a few commands, and the framework was deployed. No dancing around the browser core and integrating it with Selenium. You could literally get started and go, and go fast and with a breeze.

Yes, there were problems, of course. At that time, multithreading and reporting dashboards were paid features. This was all solved with a custom-built dashboard and custom multithreading with thread marking. It was inconvenient, but it helped me understand how everything worked under the hood even better.

Overall, my automation process went through three stages. In the first stage, regression time was reduced from a week to three days. I automated not the most complex and critical scenarios first, but the ones that took the longest to run. This was necessary so that the team would get used to automation and learn to trust the scenarios.

In the second stage, we selected the main scenarios, reducing regression time to three hours. While working in three threads, we got used to multithreading and fast regressions.

And the third stage – regression in 40 minutes. Multiple threads, maximum system stability, and minimal flakiness. By this point, there were already over 2,000 scenarios.

The most pleasant aspect of frameworks is the quick onboarding of manual testers and their transition to automation engineers. The initial most challenging steps with setting up and configuring virtual environments are very quick and simple, and then writing scenarios follows the principle: "look at the architecture I created, and follow my lead." In a couple of hours, I could explain the project structure, in two days, a manual tester would start writing their scenarios under supervision, and within two weeks, I would have a fully self-sufficient and stable automation engineer.

But there's a flip side to the coin. At some point, I felt like I wasn't a programmer but a framework operator. After setting up the infrastructure and all the technical wrappers, I stopped touching them, as they were working stably. And the endless writing of scenarios felt like robot work. I began to realize that I was losing my programming skills and was simply fulfilling business needs and features.

After some time, when my project was running stably in automatic mode, I was asked to automate a neighboring team's project and train an automation tester to replace them. But the stack they wanted was Playwright and TS. Well, if it needs to be done, it needs to be done. I learned TS; after JS, it's not difficult, and I moved on to Playwright. It felt like more freedom compared to Cypress, and the convenience increased significantly. This is understandable, as Playwright is written in TypeScript and is intuitively integrated with it. I automated the project ahead of schedule, trained my replacement, and got bored. Everything works, autotests are running, bugs are being fixed. But everything is too stable. Sanctions also cut us off from Europe, making things quite gloomy. It was time to look for new growth points.

The AI Era. Level 1 - Prompting (We Invented the Clicker)

While I was mastering frameworks and automating projects, the world didn't stand still. AI chats emerged, and prompting began to develop actively. I remember that initial period when no one understood how to live with them or whether it was good or bad. Everyone was embarrassed to admit they used chats for clarification, as it questioned their seniority. "You're asking ChatGPT something? Don't you know yourself?" Now, of course, that sounds funny.

However, progress didn't stop, and prompt engineering gradually entered the workflow. I couldn't seriously apply it at work: there was no closed agent network, and sharing even anonymized logs for analysis on a public network didn't seem like a sound idea. So, to deeply master this new technology, I decided, as usual, to create my own small personal project. A Telegram chatbot, connecting via API to Tinkoff and Mosbirzha, displaying curated stock and bond selections, using a single-board computer with Ubuntu as the server. I managed to assemble and launch the project in 6 hours on a Saturday evening. For the chats, I used the free GPT-4 and Mistral; there was nothing better at the time. What impressed me the most? Not even that the project worked stably – although for a pet project assembled in such a short time, it was indeed surprising back then – but that after thinking through the architecture, I still essentially didn't know how everything worked under the hood. I never opened the API documentation for Tinkoff or Mosbirzha, I didn't know what requests were used, how they interacted, how my requests were processed. At the time, I was simply amazed by this, and yet these were already harbingers of a new era – the era of programming through intent without a deep understanding of what was happening under the hood.

A few weeks later, I decided to upgrade my bot, and the real struggle began. The context wouldn't fit in the window, seams appeared between program parts, and integrations stopped working. After struggling for a couple of days, I realized it was easier to create the bot anew than to rewrite it piece by piece. This pet project was successfully abandoned.

Looking back, I realize that this period was somewhat reminiscent of the clicker era. You could create personal projects in an evening. It gave a strong wow effect; you felt like you could do anything. But then you realized that you couldn't get more out of this approach than a pet project or a prototype. Moreover, by then, I was already a fairly experienced engineer; many without experience wouldn't have been able to achieve even this.

But the most important thing this era gave us was the integration of AI into our workflow. We started Googling less, asking neural networks more. We started trusting them. Now, when encountering an error, we didn't rush to Stack Overflow but pasted it into the chat and got an answer explaining what the error meant and what we needed to do.

The AI Era. Level 2 - Agents (Early Selenium)

Gradually, agents began to penetrate our lives and work. The first ones were quite unstable, with small context windows and weak models. And after getting acquainted with them, I was disappointed: they worked slowly, hallucinated, and gave incorrect results. In general, using them within the scope of an entire project was inconvenient. But simultaneously, they offered a great option: they could be integrated into a project and perform specific actions. And since an agent can be integrated into a part of a project, you can create chains of such specialized agents and significantly ease your life. For me, these were primarily various reviewer agents (a kind of replacement for linters) and agents for analyzing failed tests.

In some ways, this period is similar to the early Selenium era: the tool already exists, its potential capabilities are enormous, but no one has yet figured out how to use it effectively, and everything needs to be learned on the fly.

What was interesting and memorable about this period: I unleashed an agent on my personal project from the previous era. And the agent coped with the task. It revived the bot, stitched together all the integrations, fixed errors, and updated the API. In small projects, even the first agents worked perfectly. They gained a key advantage over prompting: they could see the entire project context and learned to assemble it without creating ugly seams, like on Frankenstein's body.

The AI Era. Level 3 - Multi-Agents (Playwright on Steroids)

The era of the first agents passed quite quickly. The spiral of technological development compressed to the limit, and technology advanced at breakneck speed. When accessible agents and multi-agent systems became available, I was already working at a bank. And, I admit, I wasn't thrilled with my first experience using them. But in just half a year, everything changed.

By setting up my own multi-agent system, I not only managed to fill gaps in API automation but also built a full-fledged production pipeline: from collecting requirements from analyst documentation to creating tasks and test scenarios, followed by generating autotests. Moreover, the multi-agent system, covered by a framework of context, skills, templates, MCP connections, and rules, turned out to be maximally stable. Work that would have taken me at least 6-9 months before, I completed in a month. And I deliberately didn't rush to ensure maximum control over the quality of the process and the resulting output.

And then I realized that the technological agent breakthrough we had long been promised had already happened. Yes, now I often read and hear that multi-agent systems don't understand things, don't consider them, make mistakes. But that's the point – people also often don't understand things and make mistakes. Multi-agent systems are tools that significantly empower competent engineers, not replace them. On the flip side, they give a sense of omnipotence to incompetent engineers, and we later read about the consequences of their creations in scathing articles about another failed agent implementation in a product.

It's best to imagine agents in your product as a senior engineer who can do everything and simultaneously knows nothing about your product. "Why was this architecture chosen? Why this specific stack? Why this strange workaround, let's remove it?" All the questions a new engineer would ask after hiring should also be asked by an agent integrated into your product. And only after receiving answers can it truly show its potential.

I'm again drawing analogies with frameworks, as their integration into automation made entry very simple and fast. Five commands – and the framework is ready, ten lines – and the autotest works. It seems like you can just keep writing. But without improving engineering skills, everything will eventually devolve into spaghetti code with constant flakiness, cascading failures, insane interdependencies, and other engineering errors. So, my advice: don't look at agents as a tool that will solve all your problems right out of the box. Consider it your companion that will make you stronger and multiply your efficiency.
