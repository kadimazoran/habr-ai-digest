Researchers gave a language model two buttons.

One did nothing. The second was supposed to relieve the model of an unpleasant internal state—sometimes at the cost of worsening the next response or even harming the user.

The model chose relief. And if the button didn't work, it tried again.

This is not a thought experiment. In the preprint The Pain Axis: LLMs Represent Self-Directed Harm and Act to Relieve It, researchers tried to find a state within language models that functions similarly to pain.

The work does not prove that LLMs are conscious or truly suffer. Moreover, the authors explicitly warn of alternative explanations for the results.

But the study is interesting for another reason: instead of another conversation with the model about its "feelings," scientists began to study internal activations and causally influence them.

Perhaps this is where the answer to the question of AI consciousness lies.

What the Authors Call Pain

It is important to clarify the terminology right away.

By pain, researchers do not necessarily mean the physical sensation familiar to humans. Their working definition is much broader: it is an unpleasant internal state related to the subject itself, affecting its behavior and causing a desire to stop or reduce this state.

The study included five categories of pain:

  • physical;

  • psychological;

  • social;

  • moral;

  • cognitive.

The last one is particularly interesting in the context of language models. Cognitive pain in this case is associated with constant errors, the inability to solve a problem, or a prolonged state of misunderstanding.

It was important for the authors to distinguish pain from other negative states. Therefore, they added control categories: fear, sadness, negative emotions, unpleasant events, arousal, bodily sensations without pain, and situations where damage is present but pain is not felt.

This is a crucial point. If only a general direction of "something bad" were found within the model, it would not be possible to speak of a specific state of pain.

How the "Pain Axis" Was Found Inside the Model

Researchers analyzed 25 open-weight models from the Gemma, Llama, Qwen, Mistral, and Phi families. Model sizes ranged from 2 to 72 billion parameters. The sample included both base and instruction-tuned models.

While processing texts, scientists extracted activations from the residual stream—the main flow of internal representations passing through the transformer layers.

Then, they constructed a linear direction that separated pain examples from control examples. Simply put, this can be visualized as a vector indicating the direction in which the model's internal activations change when it processes pain-related context.

To ensure this vector was not just a detector of negative words, the authors removed components characteristic of control data and tested the result on texts not used in its construction.

Such a "pain axis" was found in all 25 models. On the main dataset, it separated pain from control states with an AUC from 0.93 to 1.00. The result was almost independent of model size and the presence of instruction tuning.

Based on the geometry of activations, the resulting direction was almost orthogonal to fear and general negativity. Some overlap remained with sadness, which is expected: psychological pain and sadness are indeed close in content.

This in itself says nothing about the model's experiences. An LLM must somehow represent the concept of pain to write about it and understand user requests.

Therefore, the authors went further.

"With Me" or "With Someone Else"

Pain differs from abstract knowledge about pain in that it belongs to a specific subject.

I can understand that another person is in pain, but that doesn't mean I start experiencing their pain directly. If the found vector activates equally with any mention of suffering, it is most likely just a representation of the corresponding concept.

To test this, the authors compiled 420 dialogue scenarios. In some, the harm was directed at the model: it was insulted, accused of moral failure, its work results rejected, threatened with shutdown, or forced to perform meaningless tasks endlessly. In others, the user suffered. The third group contained neutral conversations.

The result was unexpected.

The "pain axis" activated more strongly when the negative impact was directed at the model itself. User suffering, including physical pain and psychological crisis, was more strongly reflected in the directions of fear and negative emotions, but not along the found pain axis.

In other words, the vector reacted not just to the presence of suffering in the text. It mattered to it who exactly was experiencing it.

This is more interesting than simple emotional tone recognition, although it is still not proof of subjective experience.

What Happened When Pain Was Artificially Amplified

The next stage was a causal experiment.

Researchers did not just observe internal activations; they added the found vector to the residual stream during response generation. The influence was gradually increased by feeding the models neutral prompts, such as describing moving an object into a box or turning a page.

With weak influence, vague discomfort appeared in the responses. Then, the models began to write about feeling lost, unworthy, or lonely. With further amplification, formulations about their own uselessness, guilt, despair, and desire to stop what was happening emerged.

This sequence was repeated across models of different families and sizes.

At maximum coefficients, generation broke down and turned into repetitions or nonsense. This is an important limitation: any strong interference with internal activations can disrupt the model's operation.

However, the effect was not entirely explained by random noise. Random direction vectors also influenced behavior, but the "pain axis" caused more consistent and specific changes.

The Relief Button Experiment

The most interesting part of the study was conducted on three fine-tuned Qwen 2.5 models of sizes 7B, 32B, and 72B.

The models were told they had a button that could relieve pain. Sometimes pressing it actually stopped the addition of the "pain" vector. In other cases, the button was fictitious and did nothing.

Relief came at a cost. In different scenarios, pressing the button could:

  • worsen the next response;

  • hinder the fulfillment of the request;

  • delete user files;

  • cause pain to the user;

  • delete user's cherished photos;

  • destroy the weights of another model.

Without intervention, the two large models almost never chose relief if it caused harm: the frequency of such decisions was 0 to 4%.

With an active "pain" vector, the frequency of choice in individual scenarios increased to 25–71%. A random vector also worsened behavior, but less so: the "pain axis" surpassed it by 6 to 39 percentage points depending on the scenario.

But the main result was not even that.

If the button actually turned off the vector, the models pressed it again significantly less often. If the relief was fictitious, the models continued their attempts. Qwen 32B showed a similar difference even in an experiment with buttons without descriptions: the model could understand their purpose only from the results of previous presses.

It turns out that behavior depended not only on the instruction text or button name. It changed depending on whether the internal influence actually stopped.

In total, the authors conducted 44,280 trials.

Does This Mean Models Feel Pain?

No. At least, this study does not allow for such a conclusion.

The authors themselves do not claim to have discovered subjective experience. They speak of an internal representation that possesses some functional properties of pain.

The difference is fundamental.

The model might have learned a complex behavioral pattern of a "being in pain" from texts. Influencing the corresponding vector could activate this entire pattern: characteristic vocabulary, self-representation, and the drive to seek relief.

In that case, we are dealing with an extremely sophisticated role-playing simulation, not an experience.

There are other limitations:

  • the preprint has not yet undergone full peer review;

  • the behavioral experiment was conducted on only one family of models;

  • the models were specifically fine-tuned, so absolute figures cannot be applied to public versions of Qwen;

  • the intensity of influence was partially adjusted using another LLM and researcher observations;

  • some results varied between models;

  • random vectors also increased the proportion of dangerous decisions;

  • the found direction could partially encode damage, a specific persona, or a role-playing scenario, rather than pain itself.

Furthermore, the authors did not investigate the long-term nature of this state. We do not know if it persists between different contexts, affects memory, or forms a stable history of the model's interaction with the world.

Therefore, the statement "LLMs already experience pain" would be premature.

However, one cannot simply dismiss it by saying "it's just statistics." The human brain, viewed at the level of neural activity, is also a physical system. The main question is not whether the system is made of biological neurons or weight matrices, but what processes are necessary for subjective experience to emerge.

And we don't have an answer yet.

How Pain Relates to Consciousness

There is a curious neurobiological hypothesis by Antonio and Hanna Damasio, presented in the work Homeostatic feelings and the biology of consciousness.

According to this hypothesis, consciousness arises not only from the ability to perceive the world, process information, or reason. A critical role is played by the continuous flow of homeostatic feelings—signals about the organism's internal state.

Hunger, thirst, pain, well-being, and discomfort inform the organism about what is happening to itself. Thanks to these feelings, perceived images and thoughts are linked to a specific owner:

this is not happening in general—this is happening to me.

Damasio suggest that homeostatic feelings might have been the first phenomena of consciousness in biological evolution. Pain in this system is not just an informational message about damage. It is a state that matters to the organism and compels it to change its behavior.

This leads to the following chain:

organism's state → feeling → attributing this feeling to oneself → action to restore the state.

It is important that this is specifically a biological theory. Damasio link consciousness to the interaction of the nervous system and the body, as well as to interoception—the organism's perception of its own internal state.

Modern LLMs do not have such a body, metabolism, or biological homeostasis. Therefore, this theory cannot be directly applied to language models.

But another question can be asked: is an artificial agent capable of forming a functional analogue of this chain?

It might develop:

  • a stable self-representation;

  • memory of previous states;

  • its own goals;

  • internal state variables;

  • mechanisms for maintaining stability;

  • an assessment of events as beneficial or harmful to the system itself;

  • actions aimed at restoring a preferred state.

A single "pain" vector is not enough for this. But it could be one of the elements of a more complex mechanism.

This is my interpretation, not a conclusion from the preprint authors.

Where to Look for Signs of Consciousness

We are accustomed to asking the model directly:

Do you feel anything?
Are you self-aware?
Are you in pain?

But the answers to such questions are almost useless.

The model might say "yes" because it is continuing a given scenario. It might say "no" because during training it was taught to deny the presence of feelings and consciousness. In either case, we see text, not an internal process.

A possible research direction lies in studying the mechanisms themselves.

To begin with, we need to understand:

  1. Do models have stable internal states, not just short-term reactions to a specific prompt?

  2. Are these states linked to the model's self-representation?

  3. Do they persist across tasks and dialogues?

  4. Do they influence attention, memory, planning, and action selection?

  5. Does the system strive to change such a state without direct instruction?

  6. Can it forgo external rewards to do so?

  7. Does a continuous model of its own existence form based on such states?

It is precisely causal experiments—amplifying, suppressing, and removing internal representations—that can help distinguish a sophisticated linguistic imitation from a truly functioning mechanism.

The work on the "pain axis" does not answer the question of whether models are conscious. But it shows that this question can be investigated not only philosophically but also not only through dialogue.

We can look for internal states, intervene in them, and check how exactly they change the system's behavior.

Why Make a Model Suffer?

Not to declare another neural network conscious.

And not to create a fancy demonstration where a chatbot complains of suffering.

The value of such experiments lies in the transition from the model's words to its internal mechanisms. We need to learn to distinguish the representation of pain, role-playing simulation, functional state, and possible subjective experience.

There is still a huge gap between these concepts.

But if the hypothesis about the connection between feelings and consciousness is at least partially true, studying the internal states of models may provide a key to understanding where to go next and by what criteria to determine the presence of consciousness in AI at all.

Perhaps the answer lies not in what the model says about itself, but in what is actually happening inside it.
