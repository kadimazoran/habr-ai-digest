The Beginning

Greetings, everyone.

I decided to start my job search. I had received my diploma, which logically meant I was a qualified specialist. I created an account on hh.ru, filled out my profile, and started applying. Out of 50 applications, I received two calls (with rejections), and for the rest, I got a templated response or no response at all. The reason for rejection was only stated once: insufficient experience.

The rejection itself wasn't surprising. It would be strange to expect every company to offer me a job right after university. However, I wasn't starting from absolute zero. I was interested in antifraud. I already understood how such systems work, had freelance experience, and my own project. This means I wasn't entering the market with just a diploma and a phrase like 'I'm ready to learn'.

For the past few years, we've regularly seen news about a shortage of IT personnel, especially in information security. Companies claim they lack specialists, the industry needs new people, and the government and businesses are discussing training кадров (personnel).

But if there's truly a shortage of specialists, why is it so difficult for someone without work experience to even get the first opportunity to earn that experience?

Moreover, in my case, it wasn't about inflated salary expectations. I didn't state in my resume that I wanted to earn a hundred or two hundred thousand rubles immediately. I would have been content with a salary of 40 thousand at the start.

This leads to a strange situation: to get a job, you need experience, and to get experience, you need a job.

After another rejection, I became interested not so much in why I specifically was being rejected, but in what was happening to young specialists in general.

Where do people go who have spent years studying, understanding technologies, working on their own projects, only to discover after graduation that the market primarily seeks specialists with ready-made commercial experience?

And I quite quickly stumbled upon a pattern: as it becomes harder for newcomers to enter IT, the involvement of young people in cybercrime also increases.

This is purely my conjecture and nothing more, but if such a pattern truly exists, why not test the following hypothesis: The fewer opportunities a young specialist has to legally enter the profession, the higher their chances of ending up in cybercrime.

From that moment on, a regular job search turned into a small research project for me.

The Research Begins

First, I decided to check if my case was just bad luck. 50 applications is still the experience of one person, not market statistics.

According to SuperJob data cited by "Expert", in 2025, the number of vacancies in IT, internet, communications, and telecom decreased by 13%, while the number of resumes increased by 11%. In January 2026, only 10-11% of IT vacancies were available to candidates without experience. For the labor market as a whole, this figure was 37-38%.

Moreover, almost half of the IT vacancies available to newcomers were in technical support and related professions. Junior programmer positions accounted for about 1 in 7 vacancies for candidates without experience. This means the problem was significantly larger than my personal sample. The market hasn't stopped hiring people. It's just much more actively seeking people who have already been trained.

There's another good data slice from Habr Career. In the second half of 2025, 10,820 vacancies were published on the platform – 38% less than in the first half. But it's more interesting to see who exactly they were looking for.

If we look only at these five categories, the difference is immediately noticeable.

Trainees and juniors combined account for 870 vacancies, while there were 6,463 vacancies for mid-level and senior specialists alone. This means that on this platform, employers were significantly more likely to seek people who had already completed the entry-level stage of the profession.

Businesses are much more likely to need a specialist who can come in and take on real work tasks almost immediately. And this is understandable; a company might urgently need an engineer who will be responsible for a working system tomorrow, and it simply doesn't have the resources to train a newcomer.

The problem lies elsewhere.

If most companies want a specialist with ready-made experience, a simple question arises: Who is supposed to give a person this experience?

This is precisely where, in my opinion, the main paradox of the market emerges. Companies want mid-level and senior specialists. But every mid-level specialist was once a junior. And every senior specialist once had no commercial experience at all.

Young Specialists and Cybercrime

Now we move on to why I started writing this post in the first place.

What happens to a person who has acquired technical skills but cannot find a legal application for them? Of course, the vast majority of such people do not become criminals. Some continue sending resumes, some go into freelancing, some change professions, and some simply move to another country and find a place there.

However, information security has a unique characteristic. The same knowledge can be applied on both sides of the law. The ability to work with networks, analyze websites or applications, find vulnerabilities, write code, work with proxies, and automate processes is inherently neutral. The only difference is what exactly the person uses these skills for.

To avoid conflating phone scammers and dropouts with people who actually interfere with PC operations, I decided to focus specifically on computer information crimes.

According to the Judicial Department of the Supreme Court of the Russian Federation for 2022, among those convicted in this category, 48.2% were aged 18-24, and another 19.7% were aged 25-29. In total, this is 67.9% of convicted individuals under 30 years old.

For this article, this age range is particularly interesting. It is precisely between 18 and 29 years old that many finish vocational or higher education, officially enter the labor market for the first time, and begin to gain the very commercial experience that employers demand.

This creates an interesting overlap: the age at which many first try to establish themselves in a profession simultaneously constitutes a significant portion of the age structure of those convicted of computer information crimes.

Next, I found a study specifically on Article 272 of the Criminal Code of the Russian Federation – illegal access to computer information. The authors studied statistics from 2018-2023 and specifically focused on young offenders. The researchers note that young people may face unemployment and a lack of prospects in the labor market. In combination with financial difficulties, this, according to the authors, can increase vulnerability to offers of quick earnings. They also specifically point to difficulties in employment and earning for individuals who may possess computer skills but work in low-paying positions.

And this is very close to the question I started with.

It's not about a young specialist necessarily becoming a criminal after several rejections. It's about the lack of a clear career perspective that can become one of the factors increasing the risk of turning to illegal activities. And this is no longer just my thought after dozens of rejections – such a factor appears in a Russian criminological study of individuals convicted of illegal access to computer information.

There is another study, this one international, where the authors interviewed ten active and former Criminal Hackers.

It turned out that initial motives were often not money, but a genuine interest in technology, the desire to solve a complex problem, a sense of mastery, control over a system, and recognition within the community. Financial motivation most often simply amplified the attraction for those immersed in cybercrime. Moreover, several study participants initially wanted to apply their skills legally. One wanted to work in CyberSecurity but couldn't afford the necessary education. Others spoke of an interest in Ethical Hacking and legal business. One participant found vulnerabilities and tried to report them to business owners, but his appeals were ignored. Therefore, the study authors draw a rather telling conclusion: one of the prevention methods could be to remove obstacles to a career in CyberSecurity and provide young people with interesting legal technical tasks.

And this is no longer just theory.

The UK's National Crime Agency uses a similar approach in its Cyber Choices program. One of its main goals is to work with young people who are interested in technology and begin to use their skills illegally, and the agency shows them legal ways to apply the same abilities. The NCA explicitly formulates one of the program's goals as promoting "positive, legal cyber opportunities."

One of the publicly published cases is particularly telling. A young man was found after hacking school IT systems. After participating in the program, he was helped to get a job in a cybersecurity organization: four hours a week during the academic semester and up to eight hours during holidays. In another example, a young man who had previously uploaded WannaCry to a computer at college, after completing the program, got a job at a cybersecurity company in IT maintenance. So, the logic is quite simple: if a person already has an interest in technology, there's no need to restrict their freedom; they need to be explained which actions are illegal.

It is important to provide a clear answer to another question: where can these same abilities be applied legally?

Moreover, the program has a larger-scale evaluation of results. The NCA analyzed 1041 referrals to Cyber Prevent from December 2017 to April 2025.

Among program participants, recidivism rates were lower than in the comparison groups used: for general crime, 13.83% versus a comparative figure of 29.35%; among minors, 15.08% versus 32.9%. However, the NCA itself emphasizes that this is a descriptive evaluation, so attributing the entire effect solely to the program would be incorrect. For this post, the principle itself is important: providing an accessible legal path for applying technical skills is considered not only an employment issue but also one of the prevention tools.

And here it's worth returning to the Russian reality.

My own story didn't start with an attempt to get a job as a pentester or malware analyst. I was looking for a job in antifraud. Antifraud and information security are not the same field, and it would be wrong to confuse them.

But there is an important overlap between them.

In antifraud, one also has to analyze suspicious behavior, work with data, automation, detection rules, bypass restrictions, and find patterns in user actions. This means a significant part of the technical thinking is close to information security tasks.

And the entry problem turns out to be very similar. A person might have their own projects, practical skills, and understanding of the subject area. But it's much harder to turn all of this into a formal indicator than a single line: "Commercial experience - 2 years.".

Therefore, the question has gradually moved beyond antifraud: how accessible is the first entry point into a legal profession for a young technical specialist?

And this is where an unpleasant asymmetry arises.

On the legal side, a person might be required to provide a resume, have commercial experience, meet formal requirements, undergo HR interviews, and technical interviews.

On the illegal side, such formal requirements simply do not exist.

This does not mean that cybercrime is simple or accessible to everyone. It's more about two different paths: to get the first legal job, a young specialist must pass several selection stages and prove experience that they often haven't had the opportunity to gain yet.

If the industry is indeed experiencing a shortage of personnel, but entry-level positions remain a bottleneck, companies are not only losing potential smart employees. They are complicating the legal career trajectory precisely at the moment when a young person is trying to figure out where they can apply their abilities. This doesn't turn a rejection into a cause for crime, but the absence of a clear career path can become an additional risk factor.

Conclusions

And here I return to my first fifty applications. Perhaps the main question isn't why I was rejected. Companies have every right to choose their employees. What's much more interesting is: by what criteria today is it decided whether a young specialist is even worth giving an opportunity to demonstrate their abilities, and what they can do?

If a company looked at my projects, talked to me, gave me a technical task, and decided my level was insufficient – that's normal selection. The problem begins when a candidate doesn't even reach HR because their resume is filtered out at the initial screening stage based on formal parameters: experience, position, education, keywords.

Automation itself is not the enemy here. A neural network can indeed help process a large volume of applications and relieve HR of some routine. But fully trusting it with initial screening in a situation where companies and the state are simultaneously talking about a shortage of specialists is questionable.

Research from Harvard Business School and Accenture shows that automated hiring systems can filter out suitable candidates due to overly precise requirements. Minimum experience, a diploma, keywords, and other formal parameters allow for a quick reduction in the number of resumes, but along with it, you might lose a specialist who is capable of performing the job better than someone with documented experience.

This is the paradox. A young specialist might have freelance experience, personal projects, and real skills, but not two years of official commercial experience. For a person, this is a reason to look deeper. For a formal filter, it's simply a non-compliance with one of the requirements.

This is where my personal opinion begins.

If the government and companies are truly interested in new specialists, I, in their place, would not try to automate the selection process to the point where the algorithm effectively decides who HR will even see. I would only keep truly essential requirements, and then give the candidate an opportunity to confirm their knowledge in practice, a small technical test, a case study analysis, a review of projects, and a conversation with a technical specialist. Yes, this will take some time, but I sat on HH and waited for rejections for up to two weeks. The task of HR in a personnel shortage, in my opinion, is not only to quickly turn 500 resumes into 10, but it is equally important not to lose among these 500 people who are capable of doing the job but simply haven't had time to gain commercial experience yet.

We have quickly learned to use technology to filter out candidates, but we understand much less about who we might lose in the process. The role of HR, with the advent of neural networks, should not disappear but change: the machine can handle routine tasks, but the final assessment of a person's potential should not be reduced to a set of formal criteria.

And here it's time to return to the main topic of the post.

International research and programs like the UK's Cyber Choices cannot be directly transferred to Russian realities; we have a completely different system of education, labor market, social environment, and the very structure of cybercrime. However, the general mechanism, in my opinion, remains important for Russia as well.

If a young person already possesses technical skills but cannot find a legal application for them for a long time, this in itself does not make them a criminal. However, the lack of work, career prospects, and a clear entry point into the profession can become an additional risk factor, especially when the illegal environment offers simpler tasks, higher rewards for young specialists, and recognition in their society.

We do not know the exact number of Russian-speaking young specialists who choose such a path: judicial statistics only show those whose cases have reached the law enforcement and judicial system, and a significant portion of cybercrime is international in nature.

But the main conclusion of the article is this: if the country needs technical specialists, it is not enough to just talk about a personnel shortage. Everything possible must be done to make it easier for a person with skills to get their first offer and to prove themselves further. A good specialist is difficult to nurture and find. It is much easier to lose them at the first filtering stage.

And perhaps, we should start with the very first barrier: whether this person will be given the opportunity to at least show what they can do.
