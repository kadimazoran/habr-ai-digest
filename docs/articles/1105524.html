1. Introduction

I am the developer of Tank General, a tactical collectible card game about tanks set in World War II. The game features PvP and several PvE modes, which currently utilize the existing bot. I've been developing the game for over 2 years and am gradually preparing it for release.

First, a rather important question: do you even need this? The game has PvE modes, the bot fights, moves, uses spells, and manages resources effectively. What more do you need? Developing a self-learning model will take a significant amount of time and effort.

But in reality – yes, it's very much needed. Primarily because PvP battles in my game are much more engaging, longer, and more intense than PvE battles against the bot. To put it simply – PvP battles are much more interesting.

And here's a nuance: not everyone enjoys playing PvP. As far as I understand modern gaming trends, more and more people prefer PvE modes over PvP. I am one of those people.

I want everyone who enjoys the game's genre and setting to play my game, not just PvP enthusiasts. This isn't just about potential revenue from more players. I love my game and want as many people as possible to share that love.

When I was implementing the bot for my game over 2 years ago, I had no idea how to do it. I simply programmed its behavior as I saw fit – defining the main behavioral algorithms for each specific situation: sequence of actions, target selection, movement direction, etc. Later, when improving the bot, I added a reward system where each action's outcome is worth a certain number of points, and the bot must always choose the action that theoretically yields the most points.

And how "smart" did my bot turn out? Despite several upgrades, it's not particularly smart. This isn't surprising, given its limited implementation. To give PvE battles a certain level of difficulty, I simply have to increase the bot's card strength, making the battles not entirely "fair.".

Because the game has hundreds of cards with abilities, and the battle is played on a grid, each battle presents hundreds of action variants that differ greatly from one battle to another. This creates a huge variety of possible action combinations.

Ultimately, I faced a choice: either continuously improve the bot's behavior by programming the best options for every situation, or find another way.

Choosing the first option would have meant endlessly expanding my scripts for the bot's behavior algorithms, trying to account for all major situations that could arise in my game. And there are thousands of such situations. I'd also have to ensure these algorithms didn't conflict with each other.

Could I have gone down this path? Absolutely. While not easy and time-consuming, it was a feasible option.

However, I'm confident that even then, I wouldn't be able to program a bot that makes battles feel as engaging as PvP battles. The bot's limited choice of action algorithms, its шаблонные actions in specific situations, and its overall predictability wouldn't provide the same enjoyment you get from facing a human opponent.

Not long ago, an idea struck me: why not try implementing a self-learning model in the game? One that could play thousands of games against itself and learn to play at a level where facing it would be genuinely interesting. What's stopping me? Nothing. So why not do it?

At least, those were my grand plans. But it turned out to be not so simple...

2. Starting Conditions.

As I mentioned, I prefer to work on the project the old-fashioned way: tinkering with everything manually, configuring each element in the Unity engine myself. The only exception is working with game code, as I don't have a formal education and am extremely slow and poor at working with code independently.

Looking at what modern "vibe-coders" or AI-sloppers are doing, I've long felt like an outdated grandpa who understands nothing about modern technologies and has hopelessly fallen behind the more advanced youth. And I don't particularly want to catch up, because I enjoy the process of creating games with my own hands and don't want to replace it with something more advanced, faster, but soulless.

However, I really liked the acceleration in coding that modern agents provided. At the time, I was absolutely delighted by how much it sped up overall work. And I continue to be pleased to this day.

What do I use? Since I don't need a fancy agent with an insane number of tokens, I use the free Big Pickle model (who knows what's under the hood, but it works well) or Deep Seek V4.1 through the open-source AI agent OpenCode. This setup looks like some kind of archaism from the 90s. Although OpenCode has a more familiar modern interface, as a true grandpa, I'm simply too lazy to figure it out, as this ancient tool perfectly handles my coding tasks.

Each task is a new session. I think it wouldn't be too difficult to configure OpenCode to remember previous sessions. But I don't need that, as the project itself is already in my head, and I perfectly understand where to direct the agent to implement a particular task. For coding tasks, the daily free limits of Big Pickle are usually sufficient. As a backup, I have a second PC where I can continue working on the project. And as a last resort, I have a local Qwen 3.8 27B installed on my PC, which, although very slow, also works well through the same OpenCode.

But, I'll say right away, implementing a self-learning model is far beyond my knowledge, and I wouldn't be able to do it on my own. Just look at my current reward formula to understand what I'm dealing with:

R_total = R_terminal + R_intermediate

R_terminal = +100 (win) / −100 (loss)

R_intermediate = clip( R_combat + R_damage + R_tactical + R_board , −80 , +80 ).

Impressive, right? It all becomes crystal clear when you look at such a formula.

These are the starting conditions. Unfortunately, even over two years of working on the project wasn't enough to understand how to implement this self-learning model. I had to involve the AI agent and neural network more deeply in this task.

3. Implementing the Self-Learning Model.

First, my agent was tasked with a complete analysis of how the opponent bot conducts battles in the project. After the analysis, the same agent created a 20-stage work plan for implementing the self-learning model into the project. This plan was then fed to ChatGPT, which refined and approved it. After that, I began the actual implementation.

The work proceeded as follows: I fed ChatGPT the overall plan, reports on completed stages, and the current project status, then asked it to create the necessary prompt for my agent for the current stage, which I then passed to the agent. The agent would then complete the assigned task and provide a report upon completion. Then, the process would start anew. All stages followed this scheme.

I won't say it was easy, smooth sailing, but overall, the work progressed, identified errors were fixed, and the project moved forward step by step.

First, all combat mechanics were completely copied and moved into separate scripts: attack, movement, damage processing, card play, etc. Essentially, a simplified version of the game was created within the game, but without visualization.

The next step was creating a script that could run battle simulations using all the copied mechanics. This script allows running battle simulations at the code level, without visual display, making simulation battles very fast (each lasting a few seconds) yet fully functional.

Only after this was the trainable model added to the project. At this stage, the model could correctly interact with game logic, follow rules, and choose valid actions for attacking or moving. However, full training hadn't occurred yet: the model knew the ultimate goal – winning the battle – but couldn't yet effectively strategize to achieve it.

The next crucial step was implementing the actual learning mechanism for the model. The battle simulation was already in place; now, the model needed to not just play battles but actually learn from them.

After completing this task and receiving confirmation that the model was indeed learning from the simulation battles, I was overjoyed, believing the hardest part was over.

How naive I was. It was just the beginning...

4. The Main Bottleneck

Let me highlight an important point. The self-learning models in my project are not large language AI models (like Qwen, ChatGPT, or Claude) locally integrated into the game. Those giants have entirely different functionalities that are simply overkill for my needs. They are versatile, but their capabilities are useless in my game.

For my project, I use classic Machine Learning (ML). To explain the difference in simple terms: unlike versatile neural networks, my models are exclusively tailored for a specific task. The world outside my game doesn't exist for them. They are based on mathematical formulas and algorithms that are given access to game functions. During training, the program finds optimal patterns and forms its own database – this is the finished model.

However, within my game, they will perform better than versatile models (unless those are also specifically trained on my rules). The fundamental principles of learning are the same for classic ML and modern large AIs – the difference lies only in architecture and the amount of information used for training.

So, for training, two parallel base models, A and B, were created, each trained separately. The training process itself proceeds as follows:

- Base models A and B are frozen as fixed models.

- Candidate A (a copy of model A with self-learning enabled) plays many games against fixed models A and B;

- Candidate B (a copy of model B with self-learning enabled) plays many games against fixed models A and B;

- Finally, the 2 trained candidates A and B play against each other;

- If progress is recorded, these trained models replace the frozen models A and B, and the cycle begins anew.

The key problem arose when the first serious run of several thousand simulated battles showed that the models were indeed learning. But they were learning to lose, as the win rate of the trained models against the base models plummeted (from 50% wins at the start of training to 30% wins at the end).

The experiment clearly demonstrated that the models were learning based on battle results. But they weren't learning what I expected.

I started investigating. I discovered that there was an incorrect sign at the end of the formula, which reversed the learning objective. That is, the model was actually learning to lose, not win. I corrected it. The win rate of the trained candidates against the base models returned to 50%.

But then – everything stalled. Thousands upon thousands of battles, numerous adjustments to my initial formula led to nothing – battle analysis showed that self-learning was occurring (control model weights were changing), but it yielded no visible results – the win rate didn't increase, remaining around 50%.

While implementing the battle simulation and preparing the models for training took about 10 days, attempting to make the models show real results, reflected in wins, took about the same amount of time, but with zero success. No matter what I did (and I'm persistent), the result was the same – simulations run over thousands of battles showed no progress: the win rate remained stubbornly unchanged.

I think those who have dealt with self-learning models already understand where I messed up. And I didn't even suspect it...

And here, the meatbags once again proved that it's too early to count us out. We still have fight left in us and can still be useful in this new world!

5. Exiting the Dead End

The bottleneck was entirely due to my own stupidity. But even the praised neural networks couldn't tell me where my main mistake was. I even consulted various neural networks (I gave Claude the task, hoping it would figure it out and point out the reason for the lack of learning progress), but to no avail!

These "smartest" neural networks, which know thousands, even tens or hundreds of thousands of times more about game development and working with learning models than I do, couldn't identify my key error.

And the error was very simple: I made the win rate the sole criterion for successful learning.

Initially, I thought this was the correct criterion. How else to evaluate learning results if not by the most visual representation – an increase in the win rate of trained models compared to base models?

And this turned out to be fundamentally wrong. Why?

I received a hint from an unexpected source. While talking to a friend who has no connection to game development but enjoys watching videos about scientific progress trends (including videos about neural networks), I complained to him about the learning deadlock. At the very end of the conversation, my friend recalled watching a video about an indie developer integrating a self-learning model into his racing game. This developer had initially set the goal of winning the race as the criterion for successful model training. And he also got nowhere because he followed the same path as me.

Things only started moving when he began breaking down the model's training into separate stages. That is, the model received rewards not for completing the entire race, but for successfully navigating each individual turn or obstacle. In the initial version of training, when the model was rewarded only for completing the race, the cars didn't even reach the finish line.

That's it! It clicked in my head immediately. That was my main mistake!

The problem was that the model received the primary and only reward for the final outcome – victory or defeat, achieved by destroying the enemy headquarters. However, between the start of the battle and the destruction of the enemy headquarters, a huge number of intermediate actions occur: placing cards from hand, choosing where to move and whom to attack, playing spell cards, using card abilities, accumulating resources to play cards, etc. In my initial implementation, none of these actions received any additional evaluation.

And why did I assume my model would engage in all these maneuvers if its ultimate goal was victory through destroying the headquarters? The model simply pursued this goal in the simplest way – it attacked the enemy headquarters directly, completely ignoring tactics or other mechanics: it just always attacked the enemy headquarters with its own, and all placed cards blindly moved towards the headquarters and tried to attack it.

Because my training was conducted on training headquarters with identical damage and durability stats for all nations, the headquarters that started first and dealt damage first won. Since my simulation mechanics alternate which side goes first, the models won alternately, depending on whose turn started first.

This explains the lack of progress and the overall 50% battle win rate for all training variations.

I don't understand myself why I thought the model would be inherently intelligent and use more complex strategies to win than direct headquarters destruction. As if I didn't understand that AI brains are not full brains, but merely imitations and vast knowledge bases? I understood, but still wasted a lot of time on completely useless work.

Yet, a meatbag, without even being an expert in the field, could immediately identify the potential cause of the error just by recalling an episode from a video he once watched!

6. What's Next?

In my game, the enemy headquarters can be destroyed to win the battle in various ways. For example, by assembling a deck specifically designed for destroying the enemy headquarters. But the most popular method (and my preferred one) is controlling the battlefield, achieved through effectively destroying the opponent's deployed tanks.

According to my new plan, the model should learn to win precisely through effective battlefield control. Therefore, I adjusted the reward formula by adding rewards for the number of enemy units destroyed and penalties for losing my own units. My reward formula now looks like this: R = episode_reward + 0.6 * clip(kills - 1.2 * losses, -3, +3), which rewarded models for a positive kill-death ratio.

At the same time, to ensure the model didn't forget the main objective of the battle – winning – the additional reward for kills was set to be significant but less than the reward for destroying the enemy headquarters. Otherwise, there was a risk that the model would endlessly destroy deployed enemy cards to gain points, without aiming for victory.

And this yielded results. Now, during training, models consistently started showing a positive balance in kills versus losses compared to base models. This finally allowed me to replace base models with trained ones, thus achieving progress in training.

However, despite the results, I knew these changes weren't enough. And using a positive balance of destroyed and lost units as the primary criterion for training effectiveness didn't give me confidence that the final outcome of model training would satisfy me.

The result of another brainstorming session was a further adjustment to the reward formula, which now looks as I showed earlier. The new reward system accounts for various in-game situations:

- Unanswered destruction (target destroyed, attacker unit lost no HP);

- Destroying an SPG from a distance (when the attacker is an SPG and the target is destroyed);

- Destroying the headquarters from a distance (same, but for the headquarters);

- Killing an AT-SPG with the first hit (this rewards using the AT-SPG game mechanic, which takes no return damage if the target is destroyed during the attack);

- Killing a heavy tank during cooldown (another reward for using heavy tank game mechanics);

- Failed attack (own unit destroyed as a result, with no enemy units destroyed/damaged);

- Damage to enemy headquarters (constant small bonus);

- Controlling enemy territory (to make it harder for the player to play cards from hand).

The results of runs with the new reward system seemed quite encouraging. All additional metrics are steadily increasing after the runs, and the trainable models show an advantage in these metrics over the base models.

Of course, it's too early to draw definitive conclusions about the future success of creating truly "smart" models. But now, there's much more confidence in a positive outcome.

It would seem I've finally figured out the main training problems and can now move forward calmly. But what's stopping me from stepping on the same rake again? Exactly – nobody!

7. Bottleneck #2

Where would we be without another bottleneck? And what do you think caused it? Correct – I started thinking too late again! I trusted the neural network again, and again, it was in vain. Don't be like me – think about what you're doing! I hope this will be another clear lesson on how not to do things.

As I mentioned, I initially chose the wrong criterion for successful model training: the growth of the win rate of trained models against base models. Later, with the change in the reward system, the models began to train more precisely, and their progress became visible.

But I still focused on the win rate as the ultimate confirmation of training effectiveness. However, the win rate of trained models against base models, as before, fluctuated within statistical error margins.

And each time after another training session, the neural network pointed me to the final report, insisting that there was still no proven effectiveness of model training, as the win rate had barely changed. And the neural network and I spent a lot of time discussing what could be adjusted in the training settings to increase the win rate of the trained models.

We "discussed" collectively! "Colleagues," damn it! Fine, the neural network has no brain, what can you expect from it? But how could I be so dense!

The main analytical error was that the comparison of trained model effectiveness was always done in battles against base models, and the base models were updated daily after each successful training!

Thus, in effect, each new version of the model was compared not to its initial capability level, but to a practically identical model that was just one training cycle behind.

And naturally, the difference between a trained model and an identical model that had undergone just one less training cycle was negligible! I should have used my brain a little and thought, why would there be a noticeable difference if they are practically the same models, since I replaced the base models with the trained ones after each new training?

Naturally, after realizing this not-so-complex fact, I immediately created separate control models that would not be updated and would serve as test subjects. These control models were integrated into my training system, and it is against them that the effectiveness of the trained models will be tested.

8. Conclusion

Currently, the model training process is in full swing (and it's not a fast process). In parallel, I am integrating the training mechanism directly into the project, allowing the training process to be launched directly from the game engine. I also periodically adjust the reward system so that the model learns to properly use various game mechanics, rather than just making movements during combat. And I truly hope this brings me closer to the main goal – obtaining a sufficiently "smart" model that can give a real fight to a human player.

I think many will wonder – why did you even write this article if your model training isn't finished yet?

In earlier articles, I've expressed the idea that developing any game project is too individual to learn specific game mechanics from other projects, even in similar genres. Therefore, providing a detailed guide on integrating a self-learning agent into your game is pointless: it's unlikely to be useful for your specific project.

However, the mistakes I made are universal and very illustrative. They can occur for anyone who relies too heavily on neural networks' "brains" in their work.
