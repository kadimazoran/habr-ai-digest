AI has given everyone a superpower: the ability to generate a beautiful image in a minute. But this superpower has fallen into the hands of people who often don't know what to do with it. This includes me.

Anyone can ask an AI to 'draw in the style of Impressionism.' But only someone who knows that Neoplasticism or Vaporwave styles exist, and how they differ, can ask for 'in the style of Neoplasticism' or 'in the style of Vaporwave.' It's like being given the ability to build any house with a snap of my fingers. Any. But which one? I haven't studied architecture and will likely build what my neighbor has, not what truly resonates with me.

I have no art education. So, I decided to create a map for myself: what visual styles have people invented, where did they come from, and how are they related? This resulted in the Aesthetics Atlas.

What's Inside

The atlas currently contains 195 entries: from hand stencils that are at least 67,000 years old to internet aesthetics like weirdcore. This includes not only painting but also architecture, design, and things that have emerged online.

Each style has its own "hall": the main work with a label, the characteristics that identify the style, its origins and influences, sources, and further reading links.

You can explore it in various ways. On the map, styles are gathered into 25 "seas" and 74 "parts," with individual aesthetics and their connections appearing as you zoom in. There's a timeline of twelve eras, each with its own color. There's also a grid catalog with filters and a search by name, artist, or year.

The most interesting part for me is the connections. It's that "Wow, so this actually comes from that" moment.

Unexpected Connections

Here are a few examples that surprised me. Each of these connections in the atlas has a source that confirms it, and a disclaimer about its limitations.

Egypt and Greek Kouroi. Early Greek statues of young men stand in an Egyptian pose and with Egyptian proportions. The influence is limited to this specific sculptural corpus, but it is documented.

Assyria and Scythians. The Scythian animal style from Kelermes features Assyrian and Near Eastern motifs, such as compositions around the tree of life on weaponry.

Chinese Landscape and Persian Miniature. In Ilkhanid manuscripts of the 13th–14th centuries, Persian artists borrowed trees, rocks, water, and clouds from Chinese landscape painting.

Yamato-e and Ukiyo-e. Japanese prints, including the famous "Great Wave," did not appear out of nowhere: the Metropolitan Museum of Art directly cites the influence of old Yamato-e court painting on them.

Icon Painting and Palekh. In the 1920s, former icon painters began creating Palekh lacquer miniatures, and the Russian Museum links its techniques to the local icon-painting tradition.

You can also trace a path from Ancient Egypt to Abstract Expressionism in twelve steps: Egypt, Greek Archaic, Classical, Neoclassicism, Romanticism, Realism, Impressionism, Post-Impressionism, Cubism, Futurism, Dadaism, Surrealism, Abstract Expressionism. I must clarify: this is not a proven "main line of art history," but a route through the initial connections in the atlas. Still, five thousand years in twelve steps is impressive.

How Not to Let AI Invent Art History

I assembled the atlas with the help of AI. AI agents wrote the code and draft entries, while I set tasks, debated, verified, and decided what to keep. We put together the first version in about a week.

The main challenge wasn't the code. AI is very eager to draw arrows: who came from whom, who influenced whom. It sounds convincing, but it's hard to verify. An atlas with fabricated connections is worse than an atlas without any.

Therefore, the atlas categorizes connections into four types: "evolved from," "influenced," "reaction to," and "revival." Each connection has a status: confirmed by a source or a hypothetical editorial conclusion. There's a simple rule: don't invent an ancestor for the sake of a neat diagram. If the origin is unknown, we state that.

On October 16th, we audited all the arrows. Out of 256 initial unverified connections, 151 remained, now with sources and boundaries. 44 turned out to be context rather than origin, such as belonging to a school. 30 became visual comparisons: they look similar, but one did not originate from the other. 31 connections were removed entirely.

Currently, the atlas has 162 historical relationships, all with sources. There are also 56 contexts and 74 comparisons. This doesn't mean everything is verified: descriptions, dates, and images in the entries are only partially checked, and each page honestly states what has been verified and what hasn't.

How It Works

The website is static: HTML, CSS, and ES modules, without frameworks or build tools. Entries are stored in YAML files by era and can be edited manually. A script compiles them into a single JSON file that the website reads. There are 155 tests for data, connections, and the map.

Images are mostly from Wikimedia Commons, with author and license attribution. The code is open-source under MIT, and the texts and data are under CC BY 4.0, so they can be used for your projects with proper attribution.

"Draw with AI"

Since everything started with a super-brush, the atlas includes a "Draw with AI" button. On the main page, it's called "Surprise Me": a random style from the atlas plus a random object, resulting in a ready-to-use prompt. For example: "Draw a train in the mountains in the style of 'Japonisme': flat color planes; cropped, asymmetrical composition; fans, kimonos, blooming branches..."

Style characteristics are taken from the entry. The prompt opens in ChatGPT or Shedevrum, where it's automatically inserted, or can be copied for Gigachat. Artist names from the author entries are not included in the prompt: the AI is asked to draw in the spirit of the tradition, not of a specific person.

This is a small feature, but I like it the most. You spend five minutes looking at the map, then press the button, and a minute later, you see a cat by the window drawn in the style of Ukiyo-e. Your visual literacy immediately turns into action.

What's Missing from the Atlas So Far

The atlas is selective and uneven. Europe and the 20th century are described in more detail than other regions, and it shows. The repository includes a coverage matrix and an expansion plan: there are hundreds of candidates for new entries, and not all of them deserve a separate hall.

And there's an idea I don't yet know how to implement. If we have a graph of influences, reactions, and revivals, can we use it to predict what aesthetics will emerge next? Not in terms of an exact forecast, but at least plausible options with a lineage: what might grow out of Vaporwave as a reaction, or what will come back next. Compiling the past is straightforward. How to invent the future, I don't know. If you have any ideas, I'd be glad to hear them.
