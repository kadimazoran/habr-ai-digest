Why Neurobiologists 60 Years Ago Understood the Brain Better Than We Understand Neural Networks

In the late 1950s at Harvard, David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel's lab used analog tools to study cat visual cortex neurons, leading to Nobel Prize-winning discoveries. Today, we have full access to neural network weights and activations but still lack understanding of their internal workings. This article discusses the preprint "From Interpretability Methods to Interpretable Models," which critiques 15 years of Explainable AI (XAI) research in computer vision for focusing on method improvement rather than genuine model understandability for humans. It draws parallels with early neurobiology, where imperfect tools still yielded significant insights, and argues for a shift towards methods that genuinely enhance human comprehension of AI models.

Late 1950s, Harvard, the laboratory of David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel. A cat, under anesthesia, is shown stripes of light via a slide projector, while an electrode records the response of a single neuron in the visual cortex. No computers, only analog instruments. Yet, this led to one of the most significant findings in the history of neurobiology: the discovery of visual cortex neurons that selectively respond to orientation. For this, Hubel and Wiesel later received the Nobel Prize.

In 2026, it's the complete opposite. We can access any weight of a neural network, view any activation, any gradient, but what happens inside it remains unclear. The object of study has become more accessible, yet we know far less about it.

This is the subject of the recent preprint "From Interpretability Methods to Interpretable Models." For fifteen years, researchers in Explainable AI (XAI) in computer vision have been doing almost the same thing: improving explanation methods, comparing them, verifying their accuracy, and testing their robustness against manipulation. However, the question that naturally arises has been surprisingly rarely asked: After all these efforts, has the model become more understandable to humans?

The Toolkit We've Been Honing on the Wrong Thing All Along

Over time, four major directions have emerged in computer vision research.

Attribution methods answer the question of which pixels influenced the model's decision. This is the earliest and most developed class of methods: gradient-based, activation-based, and input perturbation-based. Attribution methods have faced the most criticism. Early adequacy checks showed that some popular methods perform no better than a simple edge detector and are insensitive to model or label randomization. Further work revealed their issues with reliability, robustness to manipulation, and the very definition of a fair explanation.

Feature visualization addresses a different task. Attribution shows where the model is looking, but not what it sees there. The method either finds real images that activate a specific neuron or synthesizes an image specifically for that activation.

Concept-based methods use human-understandable categories instead of individual neurons. Early versions like TCAV were limited to a predefined dictionary of concepts. Later ones, including Sparse Autoencoders (SAE), extract concepts from the model's representations without a predefined list.

Circuit-based methods work differently; they dissect the internal algorithms of the model. For example, the Circuits program project, developed by Anthropic co-founder Chris Olah. Together with his team, he traced how curve detectors work in the early layers of InceptionV1. The researchers went neuron by neuron, layer by layer, and gradually built a picture of what the network sees in the initial stages of image processing. Later, the same approach was applied to OpenAI's CLIP neural network. They discovered multimodal neurons that responded to both an image of a spider and the word "spider."

The discovery of polysemantic neurons in InceptionV1, which encode multiple unrelated concepts simultaneously, was one of the reasons researchers began exploring concept-based methods. One tool reached the limit of its capabilities and thus spurred the emergence of the next.

Debates about the usefulness of these tools continue to this day. In March 2025, a team from DeepMind's mechanistic interpretability group decided to test SAEs in practice. Not just to see if the method finds concepts, but to compare it with a simple alternative on a specific task. They took on the detection of malicious intent in language model prompts and trained two classifiers: a standard linear probe on model activations and a probe on features extracted by SAEs. The results for the method were unfavorable. The linear probe performed almost perfectly, including on new attack formulations not encountered during training. SAEs lagged significantly behind. Consequently, the team announced they were deprioritizing fundamental SAE research, as the method did not provide the expected gains.

However, a team of scientists from Cornell University and the University of California, Berkeley, presented a counterargument: DeepMind's conclusions about SAEs are only valid for tasks where the concept is known in advance, such as searching for a predefined category of maliciousness. But for finding unknown or yet undescribed concepts, SAEs have a key advantage that a linear probe cannot structurally provide.

Ultimately, the research community argues about the effectiveness of methods within a single family, ignoring the question: do these methods help people better understand models?

An Imperfect Tool Is Not a Reason to Wait

The same thing happened in the history of neurobiology. Even before Hubel and Wiesel's experiments, in the late 1950s, Jerome Lettvin's team inserted electrodes into a frog's optic nerve. They found cells that behaved like bug detectors, firing only at small dark spots in their field of view.

In 1972, Charles Gross's team reached the inferior temporal cortex of a macaque. There, they found neurons that responded to much more complex stimuli, such as the shape of a hand. According to a scientific anecdote from those years, one such neuron fired accidentally when the experimenters waved a toilet brush in front of the monkey.

The tools did not stand still. From simple stimuli, scientists moved to the reverse correlation method, which allowed mapping receptive fields. Then came causal methods like microstimulation. They made it possible not only to record activity but also to intervene in the functioning of neural circuits.

However, the recording method, where signals were taken from one electrode at a time, subtly shaped researchers' view of vision as a direct and sequential process. It was later discovered that recurrent connections operate in the cortex: signals from higher areas influence responses even in the primary visual cortex. Without feedback, it's impossible to make sense of things: some computations the brain clearly performs cannot otherwise be explained. This doesn't invalidate the direct model, so to speak. Fast primary processing remains a central part of vision, but it has now become significantly richer. An imperfect tool is not a reason to abandon research.

Why Now Is the Right Time

Although there are more and more computer vision models, architectural diversity, strangely enough, is rapidly diminishing. Dozens of convolutional architectures have been replaced by a handful of basic families: Vision Transformer, language-contrastive models like CLIP, DINO v3, masked image modeling, and predictive approaches like "world models" such as I-JEPA.

This forces a new perspective on the issue. Previously, a new explanation method had to be invented for each new architecture. Now, one can take existing tools and systematically study how internal model representations are formed under the influence of different training objectives and datasets.

The problem is that directly comparing models is not easy: each has its own unique internal language for describing the world. Previously, researchers simply counted the number of unique concept detectors or averaged interpretability scores across features, ignoring the differences. Now, direct comparison tools are emerging: cross-encoders and universal sparse autoencoders. They build a common concept space for multiple models and allow for much finer-grained comparisons.

However, they only solve part of the problem, and not the main one. They do facilitate the technical complexity of aligning representations, but they do not help answer the question that has been central all along: have models become more understandable to humans, and are we making progress in this regard? Structural agreement between models and human understanding are different things.

Two Different Questions That Are Still Confused

XAI papers often conflate two different questions, which should be separated.

What the model represents and computes. This is a structural analysis task. One can take two trained models, show them the same images, and see how their internal representations correlate, all without human involvement. Cross-encoders and concept-based methods are available for this.

Do humans understand the model's workings? And this is not about the model's authors, but about independent evaluators, on whom certification and AI deployment depend. And here, there is no simple answer; it cannot be measured with graphs. Whether a model is understandable to a human will only become clear after testing with live users.

This difference is particularly striking in medicine. One review on XAI weaknesses provides an example from neuroradiology. GPT-4 produced detailed and smoothly written justifications, and doctors trusted its recommendations even when they were erroneous. The model confidently proposed non-standard protocols based on nonsensical reasoning and almost never admitted uncertainty.

Other studies confirm this: the mere presence of an explanation leads humans to perceive competence, even if the explanation is flawed.

As a result, a doctor, misled by the neural network's smooth explanations, can no longer objectively and independently assess its response.

Moreover, interpretability does not depend on the scale or capabilities of the model. It can be large and intelligent, but it does not automatically become more understandable—these two properties are almost unrelated.

The Automation Trap

If measuring human understanding using psychophysics is expensive and slow (and it truly is), there is a temptation to remove humans from the evaluation process and replace them with another model. The first serious attempt was made precisely this way: the Machine Interpretability Score metric automates the human task of forced choice. It examines how consistently a trained perceptual similarity model groups images that most strongly activate a particular neuron.

An automatic judge is itself a model of human perception. Thus, such a measure presupposes what it was supposed to establish. As a result, interpretability is reduced to agreement with someone else's guess about what people find similar, which is precisely what we were trying to understand.

This is the same pattern as with the doctor in the previous section, only inverted. There, the form of the explanation deceived the human; here, the form of measurement replaces the question that the measurement was supposed to clarify. The order of operations is important: first, one must rigorously establish through psychophysics what "human understanding of the model" actually means, and only then automate the measurement. If a definition is fixed before it is established, there is a risk of optimizing for the wrong goal.

What's Next

Systematic neurobiology had an almost inaccessible object: the brain inside the skull, and an electrode could at best access a handful of neurons out of billions. A drop in the ocean. And for decades, researchers had to work with crude tools. But this yielded a science that continues to accumulate knowledge, brick by brick.

Our situation is the opposite: we have absolute access to any information about a neural network. Yet, the question of whether we understand the models we ourselves have built still hangs in the air. Simply because almost no one has bothered to ask it directly, instead of going in circles and comparing methods with each other yet again.

The difference between expert confirmation and independent understanding appears to be the main stumbling block for the entire field. Certification, regulatory requirements like the EU AI Act, user trust: all this rests on the assumption that an outsider can open a model and figure out what's inside. Until such an outsider emerges in sufficient numbers through psychophysical experiments, interpretability remains an assertion of faith rather than a measured property.
