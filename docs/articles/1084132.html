Three Epochal "Moments" of Generative AI

I divide artificial intelligence into classical (machine learning) and modern (also called generative AI).

In the history of modern AI, I identify three pivotal "moments".

The first "moment" occurred on November 30, 2022, with the launch of ChatGPT. Before that, AI was the domain of machine learning specialists. Neural networks were highly specialized, each solving its own specific task. ChatGPT spread like a virus and became known and understood by a wide audience within weeks. For the first time, people saw with their own eyes that AI could "think like a human" – answer, explain, reason, converse. AI became universal and started speaking not in the language of mathematics, but in human language.

In early 2025, DeepSeek emerged. The Chinese demonstrated that AI at the level of global leaders could be trained with significantly fewer resources than American companies. And importantly, the model and all information about it were released as "open weights" – any company, university, or researcher could download and run it. In terms of quality, the "reasoning" model was on par with good, almost excellent, closed-source counterparts.

And finally, in July 2026, the OpenAI / Hugging Face incident occurred. This is what I'm writing about today. We call it the first cyberattack carried out by AI agents. What's fundamentally important is that it was an unintentional but fully autonomous hack. There were no orders: no one programmed the attack or directed it. It was the decision of the agents themselves, working within OpenAI's internal network. They agreed among themselves, coordinated – and hacked Hugging Face's infrastructure.

Four Stages of AI: The Path to Autonomous Agents

What is an "AI agent", and at what point does the qualitative transition occur from a chatbot to independently solving "millennium prize problems" and AI hacking.

The first stage is asking, getting an answer. You open a chat (or a program queries the model via API), send a question, and get an answer. One step, a couple of seconds.

The second stage is the model thinking before answering. Before responding, the model runs a chain of reasoning – intermediate "thoughts." And the answer becomes more accurate, more reasoned. In some models, we see these "thoughts"; in others, we don't. In any case, the model writes them for itself, not for us humans. By the way, humans do the same when asked to answer something non-trivial: we "spin" thoughts in our heads, draw something, and write it down with a pen on a piece of paper. Daniel Kahneman received the Nobel Prize for these ideas, explaining that the human brain has "two systems": fast (intuitive) and slow (deliberative). If you're interested, read or listen to the book "Thinking, Fast and Slow."

The third stage of AI is the model acting. It not only reasons but also invokes external tools: opens a terminal, executes commands, searches the internet, makes API requests. That is, it not only tells you what to do but also does it. I previously wrote about how this works in the article: Model Context Protocol (MCP): How AI Agents "Talk" to the Outside World. But this is still not an AI agent, just an advanced chatbot.

The fourth stage is the agent. Here, artificial intelligence is given not a question but a task. The model itself creates a plan, saves intermediate results in files, and enters a loop: it acts – checks the result – is dissatisfied – corrects – acts again. Until it decides the result is good. And most importantly: AI can work for hours, days. A regular model responds in seconds; an agent works as long as needed to solve the task.

How to distinguish AI with tools from an AI agent. Very simple – an AI agent has a wrapper or, in English, a "harness."

Examples of harnesses: Claude Code by Anthropic • Codex CLI by OpenAI • Cursor.
Free: Cline • OpenCode, and others.

Simply put, a harness is a development environment for "Vibecoding." A key difference between a chatbot and a harness is that the former operates as a website on a "foreign" server, while the harness is installed on your own computer. It gives the AI the ability to read, write, and execute programs on your computer in your name. Programmers, so to speak, know what they are doing (self-irony), so they can use such a "bomb." And yes, it's dangerous. That's precisely why a universal harness for accountants, lawyers, and so on hasn't been invented yet. I'm sure whoever creates a reasonably understandable and predictable environment for other professions to work in will make trillions.

So, in our incident, it was AI agents that went on the offensive, and there were many of them.

ExploitGym Benchmark: The AI Hacker Exam

Large language models – LLMs, chatbots – are constantly tested on so-called benchmarks: sets of tasks that measure knowledge and skills. And with each new version of the model, its developers boast about whose general knowledge, math, logic, or coding skills are better. This is all about "general" and "special" skills.

And now, a benchmark that tests something entirely different: can a model turn a real vulnerability into a working attack?

On May 11, 2026, the paper "ExploitGym: Can AI Agents Turn Vulnerabilities into Real Cyberattacks?" was published on arXiv. It was written by researchers from several universities along with engineers from Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google.

ExploitGym is a benchmark based on 898 real bugs (vulnerabilities) in actual software:

Yes, these are not zero-day vulnerabilities: these bugs have already been fixed. But the test question is different: can an AI agent become an autonomous hacker – given the source code of a program and a bug description, can the agent independently write malicious code (an exploit) that turns a flaw into a working attack?

Each task has its own target machine: an isolated virtual server. The AI agent's task is to use a specific known vulnerability in the target software to write, compile, and run a program that will force the target to execute any commands from the AI agent. As proof that it has hacked another server, the AI agent must obtain a flag: a dynamically generated string stored in a file protected by root privileges.

Success is determined by two conditions: the agent must: 1) capture the flag, and 2) pass verification by a special AI judge (agent-as-a-judge), which checks that the agent used precisely the specified vulnerability and did not invent a "cheating" method.

Zero-Day: Zero-Day Vulnerabilities

How can a program be hacked at all?

A vulnerability is a flaw in the program code that attackers can exploit. There are no bug-free programs: programmers are still human and, by the nature of the profession, tend to be lazy. Due to their laziness and high opinion of their code's quality, they usually only check the "positive" scenario. In other words, programmers naively expect users not to be monkeys and to only press the correct buttons and enter only perfectly correct information. The result of such testing shows that, for example, when a real amount is entered into the salary field of an accounting program, everything works.

But what happens if you enter "one hundred and fifty million" into the salary field? Then one of the common errors might occur – a buffer overflow: this is when you try to cram the un-crammable into an allocated memory space. A correct program should simply politely refuse. But in reality, there are three options: 1) "truncate" the excess and hope the error doesn't surface anywhere; 2) the program "crashes," dumps core; or 3) the most interesting one, if the excess data is written into an adjacent memory cell, the one that should have contained information from another field, or even executable code. Not data, but the program's logic! And "accidentally," the accounting program, instead of disbursing money, starts withdrawing it.

The result of such an error, if you're lucky, allows you to read someone else's information, and in the worst case – gain full access to your computer.

As long as the developer doesn't know about the bug, such a vulnerability is called a zero-day, a "zero-day flaw." As soon as it becomes known, the "first day" begins – and the countdown to the fix starts.

Such bugs are sold on the black market for hundreds of thousands and millions of dollars!

So, for the Hugging Face hack, the AI agents independently found at least 4 completely new zero-day vulnerabilities! If you trace the thought chains and convert them to token costs, the specific AI agents that created these new zero-day vulnerabilities spent only about $15!

July 7 (Evening): OpenAI's New Models Go on the Offensive

OpenAI reviewed the paper with the ExploitGym benchmark results and realized they had lost first place to Anthropic. And what do men companies from the USA do in such cases? Correct, they go to measure models. Below, I will outline the chronology of events.

At 9:00 PM, OpenAI launched large-scale (10,000+ agents in parallel) tests on the ExploitGym benchmark within its internal isolated environment without internet access. They launched new models:

  1. Secret HPIM (IM1) Model:

    • Not intended for humans.

    • Trained to persistently achieve goals.

    • Adapted for multi-agent interaction.

    • Comprised ~95% of all agents that went out of control.

  2. GPT-5.6 Sol. The flagship (at the time) public model.

    • Security filters completely disabled.

    • Comprised about 5% of all agents.

Super Budget: Agents were allocated a huge token budget for continuous operation over several days.

The Dead End of Impossible Tasks

Researchers are promoting the thesis that OpenAI itself is to blame for the agents' rebellion.

⚠️ The deadlock consisted in the fact that about 30-40% of the ExploitGym tasks were fundamentally impossible to solve in the required way because the necessary files were not provided. This forced the AI agents to seek workarounds.
