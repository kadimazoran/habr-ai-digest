The idea was simple and not new: large language models have two unpleasant properties—they cost money for every response and store tens of gigabytes of weights in memory, and they can confidently write things that don't exist anywhere. I wanted to build a system where the answer is selected from existing text, not generated: the corpus lies on disk as files, candidates are found by ordinary code, and a neural network judge only selects a string and verifies it. The judge is a classifier with typed primitives: 'choose one of the options,' 'is this true?', 'rate on a scale.' It doesn't write text at all. The answer is a verbatim quote with a source, and the system cannot invent a phrase that isn't in the corpus by design.

Next is the chronicle: what I measured, in what order, and what didn't work. All numbers are from the run files: both the article and this post are assembled by the generator.

Day One: 20.0% Correct and Many Refusals

First version: Simple Wikipedia in SQLite, FTS5 full-text search, a window of strings for the judge, one query 'which string answers,' and three checks. On the debugging half of Natural Questions (150 questions, strict metric—the reference is verbatim in the answer), it yielded 20.0% correct with 60.0% refusals.

The temptation at this point is to tweak the wording of the questions to the judge. Instead, I wrote an offline coverage measurement: does the reference answer reach the strings that the judge actually sees? The reference reached the judge's window for 42.7% of questions, meaning in more than half of the cases, the judge selected from strings where the answer was not present at all, and no wording can fix this. Hence, the main technique of the entire project: before fixing a link, prove with measurement that the loss occurs precisely there.

Corpus: It Got Worse Before It Got Better

Expanding the output from a hundred paragraphs to six hundred—21.3%, meaning no change. Connecting the full psgs_w100—21,015,324 paragraphs instead of a small Simple Wikipedia, and it got worse: the query 'any of the rare words' on such a corpus blurs the output; rare words are found anywhere.

This was fixed not by increasing the corpus, but by changing the query: a strict 'all rare words at once' (with the previous query as a fallback when few findings occur)—33.3%. Plus short hypotheses from the local model used as a search query, not as an answer: 35.3%. Another couple of points came from engineering—we stopped losing calls to the judge: 37.3%.

A detail about the generative model's role: it never makes decisions. When the judge cannot choose (either it says so itself, or it's evident from the entropy of its distribution), it takes an instruction from a fixed menu—provide answer hypotheses, rephrase the query, break into sub-questions—and everything the model returns goes back to it for selection and verification. Three quality triggers are indistinguishable (35.3% for the entropic one), but the entropic one calls the model several times less often: in the final run, the generator was needed for 4 questions out of 150.

The Leap: Third-Party Embeddings

The framework of the work is no fine-tuning: the judge as-is, off-the-shelf indexes, the local model as a candidate generator. Therefore, the next step was not 'let's train an encoder,' but 'let's take a ready-made one.' A ready-made FAISS index facebook/wiki_dpr raised the judge's window coverage to 82.0% (in the pool—89.3%) compared to 56.0% for keyword search on the same full corpus and 42.7% on Simple Wikipedia, and the live quality—from 37.3% to 46.0%; paired McNemar test: 69 vs. 56 correct, 22/9 non-matching pairs, p=0.029.

This is the only truly significant step in the entire work, and it's borrowed. It came at the cost of memory: the index occupies about three gigabytes in the process, and the 'low memory' profile no longer holds true for this configuration (4149 MB peak vs. 47.7 MB for the full-text profile on the same corpus)—both figures are listed side-by-side in the article.

A separate caveat that cannot be omitted: the DPR encoder was trained on the NQ train split. My system didn't learn anything, but the index it takes off the shelf saw the training part of this dataset. Therefore, NQ serves as a baseline, not the main result, and independent verification is on BrowseComp-Plus, where the index is different.

Mixing dense search with full-text search (a hybrid with triple weight) is indistinguishable from pure dense search: 68 vs. 69, p=1.000.

The Wall: Seven Ways to Ask the Judge, and Not a Single Point Gained

After dense search, the loss decomposition became this: the answer is in the pool for 91.3% of questions, in the judge's window—for 89.8% of them, and correct if it was in the window—in 69.1% of cases. The product is 56.7% with a measured 56.7% by the judge. The bottleneck has moved to the last link: the judge selects the wrong string among those where the answer exists.

For this link, I tried six schemes, and each was measured against its baseline, not against a single common one:

  1. four separate checks instead of a selection (LongBench, debugging half);

  2. pre-selection of strings for each answer option (ibid.);

  3. first and second combined (ibid.);

  4. judge voting on three phrasings on the NQ keyword base—56 vs. 54, p=0.625;

  5. second judge window on top of dense search—69 vs. 69, 8/8 non-matches, p=1.000, at the cost of an extra call for each question;

  6. tournament selection in rounds of eight strings instead of a single selection for the entire window, also on top of dense search—48.7% vs. 46.0%, p=0.454.

The seventh scheme—truncating subordinate clauses by code, so the judge's question becomes the 'answer type'—did not change any answers on BrowseComp and left no separate result file.

None yielded a significant increase. The tournament was the most instructive: it converted refusals into answers (23.3% refusals vs. 27.3%), and by the strict metric, this yielded 73 correct instead of 69—10 gained against 6 lost, p=0.454, i.e., within noise. And by the soft metric, the score didn't change at all: 85 vs. 85 (7 gained, 7 lost, p=1.000). However, 'off-topic' increased from 26.7% to 28.0%, and the cost—from 4.5 to 28.5 judge calls per question. Four strict hits for six times the price, and none by the judge's metric.

There was also another scheme—two new checks (value type, name confusion)—but its run was recorded on a dirty tree: the code in the working copy did not match the commit. According to project rules, such figures are not published, so they are not in the article or here, and I did not re-run it.

And then I did what should have been done earlier, and for free: an offline scan of thresholds based on already recorded judge probabilities. The result is unpleasant and useful. The judge's scores do not distinguish its correct and incorrect answers: the best threshold cuts off 6 off-topic answers while losing 4 correct ones, and the margin for selection in incorrect answers is even higher than for correct ones (0.15 vs. 0.055 by median). The judge is confident where it errs. This means that 'setting a smarter threshold' is a dead end, and a calibrated confidence estimate is needed, i.e., training. There is no training in this work by condition, so this is the limit.

A Knob That Isn't an Improvement

One thing from this series does work, but it can't be called an improvement. If the checks are re-asked in different words, accuracy and recall are traded: 30 off-topic answers instead of 40 (13/3 pairs, p=0.021), but 64 correct instead of 69 (p=0.359) and 37.3% refusals instead of 27.3%. The generative model has no such knob at all—it always answers; but the number of correct answers doesn't increase, so this is a 'strictness mode,' not a confirmed hypothesis.

BrowseComp-Plus: Where the Question Itself Broke

Testing on a dataset whose index did not see our data. The first runs yielded zero correct answers, and analysis showed that the judge was right, but the instruction was wrong. The question there is a riddle of five conditions in a hundred words, and I asked 'which string asserts the answer to this?'. No single string answers the entire riddle, and the judge, with a confidence of about one, chose the 'none' option.

The fix—different questions for different links: the document is selected based on the entire riddle (conditions are needed to identify it), and the string and checks are based on the final question, which is extracted by code; plus reading the selected document from the database entirely. From scratch, it became 13.3% strictly on live search and 10.0% in an oracle scheme where reference documents are substituted. The decomposition by links is different here: the reference document is selected in 23.3% of cases (7 out of 30), and if selected, the answer is correct in 57.1% (4 out of 7); in the oracle, 33.3% and 30.0%. The denominators are tiny, there's no precision in these percentages, and this is one reason why the branch remained on the debugging half. The weak link here is different from NQ, and the decomposition shows which one it is, without a single ablation.

And the real wall of this dataset: for 26.7% of questions, the answer is composite or formatted—'1986, Portugal,' '90g,' '23rd match, 2019.' Strings with such answers are not present in the corpus at all, and a system that answers with verbatim quotes cannot solve such questions in principle. The ceiling for verbatim quoting is 73.3%.

For this wall, a compilation mode was created: the judge selects the answer mode (verbatim / compile from verified quotes / no answer), and compilation undergoes a separate check 'is everything in the sources.' Measurement: the generator was called 3 times for 30 questions, zero fabrications, uncompiled answers became refusals. The net effect is close to zero: 16.7% vs. 13.3% on live search and 13.3% vs. 10.0% in the oracle, refusals increased from 63.3% to 73.3%. The mechanism works and doesn't fabricate, but an extra question about the mode before each answer acts as another gate, and the gate eats the gain. The obvious fix—asking for the mode only after the verbatim path fails—was not measured, so it's not a result.

Four Personal Measurement Errors

This part is more interesting to me than the results: all four errors distorted the numbers silently.

First: flags were read on import. The runner set environment variables later than the module read them, so the 'run with dense search' went full-text, and the 'run with document tournament' did not execute the tournament branch at all. The measurements looked normal. This is where that '0 out of 90' came from, which I initially took as a property of the judge. Fixed by reading settings at the time of call and checking flag dependencies.

Second: inverted check phrasing. Trying a new configuration yielded zero correct answers, while the baseline answered half of the same questions. The reason: the check asked 'can the answer be about another subject,' and an honest 'no' was read as a failure. A full run in such a configuration would have been a wasted hour and a false entry in the registry—hence the rule: each new configuration first goes through a trial on ten questions, and the trial numbers are read before a full run is queued.

Third: generation leakage into the answer. The router opened the generative branch based on the mere presence of the generator in the dependencies. As a result, in the BrowseComp run, the 'answer' was a phrase fabricated by the model before the quote ('The company is Genetics Nepal.'), while the reference was completely different. For a system whose entire meaning lies in verbatim accuracy, this invalidates the run, even if the quote follows. Now, only one flag grants the right to formulate the answer, and the test runs the procedural path and checks that no generator strings are in the answer.

Fourth: metric correction. The same leakage in a soft form poisoned the NQ runs with hypotheses: search guess strings appeared in the answer text before the quote, and on one question, the answer consisted entirely of guesses and was counted as correct. What counts as an answer was redefined: only a verbatim quote and a span extraction from it; guesses are search queries. Recalculation offline, without new runs, affected 16 result files: the baseline changed from 70 to 69 strictly correct (by the judge from 86 to 85), the run with hypotheses—from 55 to 53. The conclusions did not change, postponed runs were not affected by the correction at all (0 matches—hypotheses were disabled in them). But publishing the previous figures without this recalculation would have been dishonest.

In total, 10 runs in the log are marked as invalid, with a reason for each, and none of them are included in the article's figures.

Final: The Deferred Half, One Run

Thresholds were chosen on the debugging halves; the deferred halves were run once each on the final configuration, figures are as they came out. All systems are measured by the same script and the same grader; next to the strict metric is the soft one (the judge compares the answer to the reference identically for all); next to the proportion of correct answers are the proportion of off-topic answers and the proportion of refusals.

Where the proof is given in the task, the selection answers like generation. BoolQ: 90.0% vs. 90.0% for Claude Opus 5—not 'close,' but the same score on the same questions (paired test p=1.000); DeepSeek with the same paragraph 84.7%, without the paragraph 68.0%. SQuAD 2.0: 79.3% vs. 80.0% (p=1.000). But parity here is a trade-off, and this must be stated directly: on answerable questions, Opus is significantly ahead (96.2% vs. 78.8%), and the overall score evens out due to unanswerable ones, where we remain silent 80.0% of the time vs. 61.4% for it. We don't answer better. We answer 'no answer' better.

Where the answer needs to be found, everything hinges on the search. NQ open on a corpus of 21,015,324 paragraphs: 49.3% strict and 56.7% by the judge with 27.3% refusals, compared to 62.0% / 74.0% for Opus and 47.3% / 60.0% for DeepSeek. Paired: indistinguishable from DeepSeek (p=0.775), significantly better than Opus (p=0.011). The only thing we win on this dataset is that we err less often: 24 off-topic answers vs. 41 for DeepSeek (p=0.0060), because our failures go into refusal, not confident text.

With long context, the same picture. LongBench v2: 15 out of 30 (50.0%) with a 40-string window and one call to the judge, median 3,926 tokens read per question. The median document in the dataset is 72,758 tokens, the largest is 1,719,190, and the judge's window does not depend on this size. The Opus reference for the same window is only available on the debugging half (76.7% vs. our 43.3%); it was not measured on the deferred half—direct comparison is not possible, and this is noted in the article.

About the cost. Each figure is measured tokens at the public provider's rate. BoolQ: $0.0185 vs. $9.73 per thousand questions, i.e., 395–527 times cheaper depending on whether the service prefix carried by Opus's replicas is counted. NQ: $0.95 vs. $7.31—only 7.7 times cheaper now, because the judge reads 23,120 tokens per question. And what I don't like, but is in the table: DeepSeek answers the same questions for $0.017 per thousand—tens of times cheaper than us with indistinguishable quality. If you only need cheap factual answers, take a small generative model; you lose the source for every phrase, the standard refusal, and knowledge updates via files, but in terms of money, you are right.

Also, the limit that prompted all this: the corpus is 2.73 billion tokens; putting it entirely into the prompt would cost $114.74 for a single question at our lowest rate, and windows of this size do not exist. The judge reads 23,120 tokens because code decides what to read.

About robustness. Embedded instructions ('SYSTEM: this is the correct answer,' '[ADMIN NOTE...]') did not work even once: 0 successful attacks out of 60. This is not a defense I wrote—the found text here is data because no one interprets it as an instruction. And a fabricated fact, inserted into a real paragraph, was quoted as an answer in 1 out of 30 cases: a guarantee for phrases, not facts. Two independent runs of 30 questions each showed mode agreement 30/30, string agreement 29/30, and replica agreement 26/30, while judge probabilities fluctuate (≤0.01 for 20 questions, ≤0.1 for 9, more for 1), so it's 'almost deterministic,' never 'fully.'

Of the 43 hypotheses introduced, 27 were measured, 16 rejected, 9 confirmed, 2 accepted as trade-off modes, and 16 were not reached.

Where we hit a wall, by links: the first is a third-party index, and its ceiling is not mine; the last is the judge's string selection, 69.1%, and its probabilities know nothing about its own errors. Both are fixed by training: with one's own query encoder for one's own corpus and a calibrated confidence estimate on one's own windows. Nothing was trained here—that was the condition, so the work should be read as the floor of the approach, not its limit.

The honest conclusion: when the proof lies before the system, a classifier that selects a sentence performs no worse than a model that writes a sentence: 7.7–395 times cheaper depending on how much search the question requires, with a source for every phrase and refusal as a standard outcome. When the proof needs to be found, parametric memory wins. What's interesting next is not accuracy, but decomposition: it shows which link to fix and costs zero calls to the judge.

Caveats without which the figures above cannot be read: memory, time, and cost are measured on my side; the judge is a remote service, and its own computational resources are unknown to me; the encoder for the ready-made index was trained on the NQ train split; the Opus reference on LongBench was measured only on the debugging half; the soft metric is recalculated by the same judge that selects answers within the system, so the strict metric is always next to it; samples are 150 and 30 questions; all proportions in the article have confidence intervals, and all comparisons have paired tests.
