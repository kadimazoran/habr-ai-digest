How I Taught Myself Like a Neural Network for Two Years

A dog training collar on your forearm, a wire to your laptop's COM port, a Portuguese phrase in your headphones. Make a typo, and you get a shock.

I do machine learning, and one day I asked a question that would make any teacher squirm: what's the fundamental difference between how humans learn and how models learn? Then, for two years, I tested the answer on the most complex subject of all—my own brain.

It's half past one in the morning. A dog training collar is strapped to my left forearm—the one with the metal contacts. A wire runs from it to the COM port of an old laptop. On the screen is a Portuguese phrase; I hear it in my headphones and have to type it blind. My finger hovers over the key. I know: make a typo, and I'll get a shock. Not a strong one. Just enough.

Why an adult man, an engineer, a father of three, sits up at night and electrocutes himself for typos in Portuguese is a long story. It began with desperation, went through several wild experiments, and ended with a working system that my whole family is now learning with. I'll tell you the whole story.

And let me preface this by saying, so the collar doesn't seem like a mere gimmick for a dramatic opening: without this madness, none of the rest would have happened. It was this that showed me why the signal stops working altogether—and from that grew everything that truly keeps me and my family at the table today. So, we'll come back to it, more than once.

Where It All Began: Language as a Wall

Let me be clear from the start: I'm not a linguist or a teacher. I work in machine learning. And foreign languages have always been my personal wall. I struggled for years to learn English, and with great difficulty. And that's not an exaggeration. Later, I'll show graphs and a table for my whole family—on them, I'm the bottom line: I learn four and a half times slower than my own eldest daughter, using the same system and the same language.

My wife tried to learn English and Spanish several times—and gave up each time. Not because she's lazy, but because it was boring, slow, and she couldn't see the results.

Then we decided to move to Portugal. And Portuguese went from a "nice to have" to a "must have, and preferably yesterday." We tried everything everyone tries: apps with a green owl, flashcards, phrasebooks, tutors. After a month, I caught myself with a familiar feeling—the very one that made my wife give up on Spanish. You seem to be studying every day, but the language isn't getting any closer. Words don't stick. Motivation wanes.

And at this point, the engineer in me kicked in instead of the student. I stopped asking "which app should I choose?" and asked a different question:

What, fundamentally, is the difference between how a human learns and how a neural network learns?

I know how to train models. That's my job. So why can't I apply the same principles to the most complex subject—my own brain?

Hypothesis: If the Brain is a Model, How Do You Train It?

The idea sounded audacious, but if you break down neural network training into its components, you find a suspiciously human analogue for each one.

Why "Just Listening to Podcasts" Doesn't Work

Before a model starts learning a language, the stream of text is broken down into tokens—the smallest units. A person listening to an unfamiliar language hears not words, but a continuous stream of sound. The first thing you need to learn is to segment this stream and associate sounds with symbols.

But here's the problem: all languages have different sounds and different symbols. I don't even know the Portuguese alphabet. On the other hand, we all have a general idea of how the Latin transcription of any language sounds. So why not associate the sounds of a new language with sounds I already know?

The solution was ridiculously simple. Take a Portuguese phrase—audio and text. Play the audio, and you have to type the text. And you can't proceed until you type the correct Latin symbol. The brain hears a stream of unfamiliar speech, can't understand it—and ideally, should ignore it as noise. But it needs to input a symbol. What's left? To find patterns and systematize unfamiliar sounds. This is how the seed of language is born.

And here, I should explain why this works—and why "just listening to podcasts" doesn't. The brain doesn't remember what it doesn't pay attention to: without attention, there's no memory recording. And what does it do with a stream where it doesn't understand a single word and nothing depends on it? It marks it as noise and discards it. You can play TV shows in a foreign language in the background for years and not make an inch of progress: to the brain, it's the babbling of a brook, and you don't remember brooks.

But as soon as there's a task, a different mechanism kicks in. As early as 1996, Saffran, Aslin, and Newport showed that eight-month-old infants segment "words" from continuous speech simply by tracking which syllables follow each other most often—pure transition statistics, without any meaning. This machinery doesn't disappear in adults; it just sleeps until the brain is given a reason to activate it. My character-by-character dictation is that reason: you can't skip a sound because you have to press the right key right now. Attention is forcibly riveted to the stream—and the brain starts to build structure out of the "noise."

Next comes feedback. Press the correct key—you get reinforcement: "heard this—typed that—got a treat." Make a mistake—you get punished. Do this a hundred times, and the "sound → symbol" connection becomes clear. Then one symbol grows into a word, words into phrases, phrases into sentences, and sentences into a language.

Where to Get a Signal the Brain Can't Ignore?

A model won't converge without a signal: a plus for correct, a minus for incorrect. And the signal needs to be given precisely—at the exact moment of the mistake, so the brain clearly understands what it did wrong. This is why character-by-character checking is necessary. Plus, our brains love to predict the future: guessing the next character is a dopamine rush in itself. And if you amplify guessing with a "treat"—the system hooks the brain by its strongest instinct: the drive to anticipate. The only question is: where to get this treat? More on that below.

Why Don't Words from an App Stick?

A model is fine-tuned on data from the target domain, not on random data. This means a person should also learn not an abstract "top 1000 words from a textbook," but the vocabulary from the content they actually watch and will actually encounter.

The brain is a lazy thing. It forgets anything unnecessary and doesn't remember anything unemotional at all. And then it dawned on me why the owl app doesn't work. What does it teach? Things you don't care about, that mean nothing to you, and that you're unlikely to use anytime soon. Why was I surprised that the words didn't stick? It's as clear as day. So, you should only learn what you need, and in a way that involves emotions.

What Does "Learning a Word" Even Mean?

Here, I'll have to dive under the hood for a moment—both of language and the brain. Let's agree on this: from now on, I'll use my working model, a convenient metaphor for what's happening, not strict neuroscience with proven "clouds" and "encoders." But it explains a surprising amount, which is why I stick with it.

You don't have "objects" in your head as neat points. You have areas of meaning—clouds spread across neurons. "Home" is not an entry in a dictionary, but a whole cloud: walls, warmth, "going home," door, smell. And a word is a vector that points to this cloud. The more precise the word and its context, the more accurate the hit. Your native language is already deeply ingrained in these clouds: you hear "home," and the cloud lights up on its own, without any translation.

Now the million-dollar question: what happens when you learn "mesa" (table) from a dictionary? You associate "mesa" not with a cloud, but with another word—"table." A label with a label. The meaning cloud itself isn't involved in this chain at all. And to use it later, the brain has to follow the route: mesa → "table" → cloud. Each time through the Russian crutch. That's why dictionary words are so slippery: it's not knowledge, it's a translation table stuck on the side. It doesn't stick to meaning—because there was no meaning in it.

Now, the same word, but in context. You see a phrase in your native language, understand it—and the relevant cloud is already lit. And while it's lit, you type the phrase in Portuguese. What does the brain do? It etches the Portuguese word directly into that lit cloud. Not into the Russian label—into the meaning itself. You're building a second encoder, a second input to the same areas of meaning that the first language is already connected to. Not a translation table—parallel access to common meaning.

And here comes something that's initially frightening, but then you realize it's the sign of victory. You start to understand a word without being able to translate it. It happens to me with "pelo": I recognize it, I know where and why it's used, but if you ask me to "translate it into Russian," I freeze. For a dictionary learner, this is a failure. But for me, it's the opposite. You know the translation but don't feel the word in a live phrase—you've learned a label. You feel the word but can't translate it—you've etched it into meaning. A native speaker possesses their language exactly like this: they don't translate it for themselves; they simply access the cloud.

Disclaimer

This works perfectly for all words. For concrete things—a table, a house, an apple—the clouds are cut the same way for everyone because perception itself, not language, carved them. But where languages cut the world differently (Russian divides blue into "siniy" and "goluboy"—two clouds, while English has one "blue"), they will never perfectly align, and a dictionary is useless here. Contextual learning gives you the cloud as the language itself cuts it. Therefore, the entire system is built around phrases, not words: a word in a column is a label in a void, a word in a phrase is a vector hitting a live meaning cloud.

So Why Do I Need a Dictionary at All?

And this is where it got interesting for me. Okay, my own content. But I still thought in a school-like way: here are the words to learn, and here are the rules for putting them together. But do we train a model like that? We don't give it an alphabetized dictionary and a grammar book in a separate file. We give it text. Just a lot of text. It figures out everything else from there.

So why do I need a dictionary and rules?

In normal text, it's all there—everything together and ready-made. And the frequency is naturally correct: what appears everywhere in life will appear a hundred times in the text. And the constructions are natural, not artificially placed—they are there simply because otherwise, you can't form the sentence. No one wrote this program; it formed itself from how people speak.

Before, this was a lot of work: annotating text, counting, selecting, arranging in sequence—a methodologist's work for half a year for one book. But now, I have something at hand that does it in minutes. So why am I resisting? I take what I need, extract the patterns—and here's my learning program, which no one specifically created for me.

The only thing to watch out for is that the source matches the goal. If you want to chat on the street, use real-life speech, not detective stories from a century ago: they'll give you grammar, but their vocabulary and intonation will be from that era.

Not a Pioneer

I'll say this in advance so I don't seem like someone who invented the wheel and is proud of it.

Teaching with phrases, not words, wasn't invented by me: Zamyatkin has his "matrix," Birkenbil has decoded texts, Glossika uses ready-made sentences, Pimsleur uses spaced repetition, Refold and sentence mining use phrases from one's own content. I learned about some of these only after I had developed my own.

Separately about Krashen: comprehensible input is his idea, and I agree with it completely. But he also believes that correcting mistakes is useless, while half of my system relies on instant correction. Here, I'm more aligned with Swain: language emerges at the output, not just at the input.

What I consider my own is not the details, but the fact that they are closed in a loop.

  • Content you already watch provides phrases;

  • Phrases are entered character by character to audio;

  • An error is linked not to the phrase, but to a grammatical property;

  • New phrases are generated based on properties with the most errors, using already familiar words;

  • These are returned at intervals;

With the aforementioned methods, each piece lives on its own: the corpus is fixed, errors lead nowhere, grammar is a separate program from a textbook. I haven't found a closed loop of "error → material for that error" anywhere else.

The Zoo: In Search of a Loss Function

I needed a reinforcement signal. A strong one. One that the brain couldn't ignore, like it ignores "you missed a 3-day streak" in an app. And here, the engineer in me took the path of least resistance—that is, the path of maximum blunt force.

The Collar

EXPERIMENT #1 · Negative Reinforcement

Remember the laptop with the COM port from the beginning? We're back. The idea was wild and straightforward: make a mistake in dictation—get a mild shock. It worked perfectly. The feeling is like skydiving: adrenaline, crystal-clear concentration, you sit over every letter like a sapper over a wire. When the cost of a typo is tangible and immediate, the brain engages fully.

There was only one, fatal downside: habituation. After a while, you simply stop noticing the shock—your body has calibrated, the signal merged with the background and dropped to zero. To maintain the effect, you'd have to constantly increase the voltage, and "increase the shock to continue learning" sounds less like an educational product and more like the beginning of a bad movie. The collar went back to the dog.

The Vape

EXPERIMENT #2 · Positive Reinforcement

This happened at a festival we attended before moving. I got my hands on an e-cigarette—and another "brilliant" idea was born about where to get a measured dose of dopamine. Correct answer = a puff. It worked frighteningly well. And I quickly understood why it was frightening: I linked learning to the dopamine loop of nicotine, and it's stronger than any gamification. This is no longer "learning a language"; it's "forming an addiction disguised as learning." Building a product on a substance is a dead end. Scratch that.

But this dead end highlighted something. Both reward and punishment in physical form are crutches: you get used to one, and you can't build on the other. But what if the signal carrier shouldn't be physical at all, but abstract? Something that a person assigns value to themselves?

Money

Family Values, or Why Pay a Tutor

EXPERIMENT #3 · Abstract Value

The idea was simple: use money. But how to test it was unclear. Paying yourself is pointless: you're just moving money from one pocket to another, with no excitement, no effect, no pleasure. I needed a person for whom the money was real. And then I looked at my own children. I have three of them—4, 14, and 21; the youngest doesn't count yet, but the daughters are ideal test subjects. The eldest isn't with us now, but she continues her studies. Plus, the math worked out: I pay a tutor, I pay pocket money. The goals are different, of course—the tutor for results, pocket money just because—but I have to pay twice. And I still need a test subject. Let the pocket money be earned through study—in my opinion, the perfect theory test.

I made coins and two wallets. You can withdraw from the gold one, but not from the silver one. I put money into the silver one, and it's earned by completing tasks: +10 coins for a correct phrase, -1 for each mistake. And to provide an incentive not to miss sessions—the whole system relies on regularity—for each day without a miss, the bonus grows: +1, +2, +3… up to a maximum of +10 coins. Miss a day—the ten turns back into one, and you start accumulating again. What's transferred to gold is yours, and what's lost on mistakes returns to me. Then I changed one detail: what's lost goes not to me, but to a randomly chosen opponent—it's always more regrettable to lose money to an opponent than just to someone. I wrote a P2P bot in Telegram, put money into silver—and the system started working. No coercion, no homework checks. Everything is automated. And essentially, you don't need to learn anything: complete the task—get coins. It's that simple.

And this is what truly worked. Without adrenaline, without dopamine loops, without wires. Simply, a person doesn't want to lose what they have—and sits down to study. Here, I experienced firsthand what psychologists have known for a long time: loss aversion is a stronger motivator than the anticipation of gain. The fear of losing a hundred rubles is a more reliable motivator than the hope of earning a hundred. The system works perfectly to this day, and the money that would have gone to a tutor stayed in the family—plus our own little economy: who earned more. In my opinion, it's simply great. Of the three tested motivators—this is the best.

Three experiments—three conclusions. Physiology doesn't work (habituation). Substances don't work (obviously). But abstract value that can be lost—works perfectly. The loss function for a person is literally a function of loss. Of money.

Postscript: What If You Get Shocked Rarely?

The collar went back to the dog. But, while I was writing this article, it occurred to me that I had diagnosed the problem incorrectly.

I thought I had gotten used to the shock. But I think I got used not to the shock itself, but to its frequency. Dictation means one mistake after another, and I was shocked repeatedly throughout the lesson without a break. With such a flow, the stimulus eventually fades into the background: the body calibrates to what happens constantly. This is not a property of the current, but a property of the rhythm.

But what if you get shocked rarely? Three to five times per lesson—and you don't know when. Then each shock remains an event, and between them, sensitivity has time to recover. And there's nothing to get used to: people get used to background noise, not to something that happens five times an hour.

And what works here is not the shock itself, but the anticipation. The brain isn't an autonomous machine; it knows the rule: if you make a mistake, you might get zapped. It's this "might" that keeps your finger over the key. And if anticipation is what keeps you going, you need few actual shocks—just enough to keep the threat real.

However, probability alone won't work. Set it to 10%—and the number of shocks will depend on how many mistakes you make today: on a difficult lesson, you'll get thirty and fall back into the same grind you were trying to escape; on an easy one, none. So, it's not probability, but a counter: a fixed number of shocks per lesson, randomly distributed. And don't announce the exact number or guarantee that it will be reached by the end—otherwise, by the end of the lesson, you start counting down the remaining ones and realize it's safe now.

What I particularly like about this scheme is that since I keep the probability in mind, I start playing with it: I'll take a risk here, and I'll concentrate there. It introduces excitement—and that's my most effective driver in the whole system, it's what keeps the family dialogues with stakes going. Here, it fits right in.

So, I'm going to take the collar away from the dog again—this time with this scheme. I'll write about what happens—whether it works or not.

What I Was Missing

Here, I must admit my own mistake, otherwise, you won't understand what follows.

For almost a year, I studied exactly like this—with phrases, without a single rule. The patterns were indeed picked up: I started recognizing constructions, feeling how people speak, understanding by ear. But then I tried to say something more complex than what I had memorized—and I floundered. I understood, but I couldn't form my own sentences. I lacked grammar, and it would have been foolish to deny it.

Sit down with a textbook? But that's exactly what I was running away from.

And I started thinking about what grammar is, looking at it as a task, not a school subject. And it's simply a way for language to convey meaning. When an action occurred. Whether it's real or desired. Whether it's a statement or a question. Whether I'm addressing you informally or formally. The meanings are the same across all languages—people talk about the same things everywhere. Only the ways of expressing them differ.

Therefore, the meaning of a phrase can be recorded separately from the language. Not "a Russian sentence and its translation," but a structure of meaning that each language unfolds in its own way.

So, I started building this. The format is JSON: it's equally understood by AI and me, it's easy to store, compare, and count. The field names are my own, working names—I'm not a linguist and don't claim any terminology expertise.
