The study I am analyzing followed 27,000 Chinese schoolchildren for 30 months: those who started using generative AI did their homework faster and better, but stopped truly learning. Scores on closed-book tests (those without multiple-choice/essay questions) dropped by 20%, and on entrance exams by ~25%.

We all strive to simplify tedious work using available tools. Initially, we invented writing instead of memorizing; abacuses and their younger sibling, the calculator, simplify our work with math, etc. Mnemonic rhymes like "Every hunter..." are similar tools, but existing in language. And much, much more... With enough desire, one can find a hundred more examples of how we expand our cognitive control zone using external tools. There's even a whole philosophical doctrine of externalism about this. I won't delve too deep, but I'll quote something necessary for my further analysis:

Human thinking and memory are not limited by the skull or biological body. Physical tools and the external environment are not just aids to the mind, but its active components. If an external tool performs a function that, if performed in the mind, we would unhesitatingly recognize as part of the cognitive process, then that tool is part of the mind itself. The Extended Mind. Andy Clark and David Chalmers. Journal of Oxford

From this perspective, a calculator that figures out how much we'll spend at the store is as much a part of our consciousness as our own brain.

What's actually happening in China?

The study's authors (economists from Stockholm University and the University of Hong Kong) found an ideal laboratory.

Why China? Firstly, it's a country with massive digitalization and fierce competition in education. Secondly, the researchers gained unprecedented access to closed administrative data from an entire county in central China (population around 1 million, income slightly below the national average – a typical, non-elite Chinese rural area).

Who was studied and how? The study included 26,811 students from grades 7 to 11. They were monitored continuously for 30 months – from September 2022 to June 2025. This period was chosen deliberately: in September 2022, only a few used neural networks in class, but by June 2025 (the survey date), about 80% of students were using AI tools like Doubao, DeepSeek, and ChatGLM. Thus, scientists tracked in real-time how the technology was taking over schools.

What's unique about the data? Usually, such studies rely on surveys: "Do you use AI?" and "What are your grades?". This is different. The local Ministry of Education provided the authors with raw logs from school digital systems. The researchers saw:

  • Grades for weekly homework in 9 subjects.

  • Exact completion time. After starting to use AI, the average homework completion time decreased from 64 to 45 minutes.

  • Scores on monthly closed-book tests in class.

  • Results of the main exams of a Chinese student's life – Zhongkao (high school entrance) and Gaokao (university entrance).

My personal shock wasn't even the study results, but the fact that schools in China teach a subject called "Politics." I even double-checked and was very surprised 🗿. Essentially, it's our Social Studies with a flavor of triumphant cat-wife-communism.

The results can be interpreted as follows: social sciences were most affected, while STEM subjects also declined, but significantly less than their humanities counterparts. The decline was most pronounced among the top third of academically gifted students:

"The negative effects on learning are more pronounced in students with higher initial achievement. The differences in estimated total effects are substantial: a 50% gap between the strongest negative effect (-24% for the top tercile, i.e., top students) and the least negative (-16% for the bottom tercile, i.e., weaker students)<…> For entrance exams, the differences are similar: the effect is -18% for the top tercile compared to -11% for the bottom." <…> This result indicates a compression of the skill distribution<…> the compression effect in our case arises because the penalty for learning with AI is greatest among the most successful students.

Key Conclusion

The authors identify a subgroup of AI users who maintained their homework completion time at the level of those who did not use AI. Their scores on closed-book tests did not statistically differ from the control group. However, their homework was better. What's the secret of Boris the cat's energy? How do they differ? In analyzing this study, I've come to the conclusion that the reason for this phenomenon might be a correct form of interaction with the new "calculator," from the perspective of delegating mental effort (it should be noted that this is not necessarily conscious).

Self-learning has never experienced such a boost with a tireless, absolutely non-toxic teacher (I recall my school math teacher who was genuinely convinced my destiny was to sweep streets). Learning becomes easier, and this is both a gift and a curse. The top third of bright students, in their pursuit of perfect grades, turned into a homework assembly line and then faltered in exams. The C-students A small sample of students not chasing homework completion speed used AI as an ideal tutor capable of explaining any topic... That is, AI acted as an extension of our consciousness, as I wrote in the section on externalism.
