In May, I submitted OenoBench, a wine benchmark for language models, to NeurIPS. During discussions with reviewers, a question arose that neither I nor they had a basis for: what result at release is sufficient for a new benchmark, and how long will it last.

To answer this, I collected data on 224 benchmarks reported between 2018 and 2026 by nine labs: OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, xAI, DeepSeek, Alibaba, Moonshot, and Mistral. For each, I found its birth date and, if it had passed, its death date—the moment the benchmark can no longer distinguish top models: the leader achieves 90% or human-level performance, or the creators themselves declare the set saturated or broken. From these dates, I calculated the half-life: the time it takes for half of the benchmarks to die.

Key findings:

  • The benchmark half-life is 23 months. For those released before 2023, it was 38 months; for those released later, it's 19 months. The estimate accounts for benchmarks still alive, so the short history of new ones doesn't distort it.

  • New benchmarks are not easier than old ones; models are simply faster. The initial gap to the ceiling is the same, but models cross it twice as fast.

  • Every seventh new benchmark dies not because of models, but due to its own defects. For agentic environments, this is the cause of nearly half of all deaths.

Below is how I calculated this and how long, based on this data, OenoBench itself will last. The dataset, codebook, and scripts are publicly available, with links at the end.

A Question Without a Basis

OenoBench is a dataset of 3,266 wine-related multiple-choice questions, compiled from 38,104 facts. I ran sixteen model configurations on it. The top four (o3, GPT-5, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and Opus 4.7) were within 2.6 percentage points of each other, with the best scoring around 83%. On half the questions that don't require reasoning (closed-book solvable), the leaders score 96–97%, and on the other half, 65–70%. Is this a lot or a little for a new benchmark? Arguing about the threshold without knowing how long the benchmark itself lasts proved futile: it's unclear how many months each percentage point of margin buys.

Everyone following the field has the feeling that benchmarks are dying faster and faster. This is usually illustrated by curves from the Dynabench paper (Kiela et al., 2021): six benchmarks from MNIST to GLUE, the path of each from release to human level, and the newer the benchmark, the steeper the curve. Ott et al. (Nature Communications, 2022) analyzed 3,765 benchmarks from Papers with Code and divided their trajectories into growth, saturation, and a post-stagnation surge. Epoch AI stitched together several dozen benchmarks into a single capability scale and began their paper with the phrase "Most AI benchmarks saturate within years or even months after they are introduced" (Ho et al., 2025). The EvalEval coalition, in early 2026, calculated the saturation index for 60 LLM benchmarks: nearly half are saturated, with larger tests and curated datasets being less saturated, and a closed test split doesn't help (Akhtar et al., 2026).

However, these works don't answer the reviewers' question: Dynabench and Ott show the shape of the curves, Epoch shows model progress, EvalEval shows saturation on a single date. I needed to know how long benchmarks live, accounting for those still alive, what kills them, and how many months of margin the initial gap provides. This is a standard survival analysis problem.

How a Benchmark Dies

First, I had to agree with myself on what constitutes death. Here's the first trap: rules you make up as you go along subtly adjust to the data. Therefore, I recorded them in the codebook before starting annotation. Death is the earliest of five events:

Code

Event

When Applied

A

Best reported score ≥ human baseline

baseline ≥ 85% of scale, measured by humans on the same split using the described protocol

B

Best reported score ≥ 90% of the main metric

no baseline or baseline below 85%

C

Maintainer withdrew the dataset, froze submissions, declared it obsolete, or cited saturation or defects as reasons for replacement

only based on public statements by the creators themselves

D

Threshold announced by organizers reached

e.g., 85% for ARC Prize, verified by organizers

E

Independent compromise

leak of answers for most frontier models; a trivial method reaches the ceiling; an audit finds ≥ 20% defective tasks, and at least one of the nine labs stops reporting afterward

There are three other outcomes. A benchmark that hasn't had any reports for twelve months and where nothing is happening on the leaderboard, I consider abandoned: it didn't die, it dropped out of observation. Born dead is one where the threshold was met at release. And finally, alive: as of September 30, 2026, nothing has happened to it.

I count any publicly reported system: a raw model, a model with a wrapper, or an agent, an ensemble, if the result was accepted by the official leaderboard or published by the developer with a protocol description. Self-reports from labs also count. For my question, the only important thing is whether the benchmark still distinguishes top models; the method used to reach the threshold is secondary. The exception is results obtained with tools where the benchmark's protocol does not allow them (AIME, GPQA, HLE, MMLU): these are not counted. The death date is considered the publication date of the result.

Here's how it looks for familiar benchmarks:

  • GPQA Diamond was released in November 2023 and died by criterion B after 24 months when Gemini 3 Pro achieved 91.9%.

  • MMLU also died by B. The "expert level 89.8%" from the original paper is an estimate based on the 95th percentile of people who took the original exams, not the result of people on MMLU tasks, so criterion A doesn't apply here. Gemini Ultra achieved 90.04% in December 2023, 39 months after the benchmark's release.

  • ARC-AGI-2 died by D: ARC Prize announced a target of 85%, and in April 2026, GPT-5.5 showed exactly 85.0% in a verified submission. The benchmark lasted 13 months.

  • Humanity’s Last Exam did not reach 90%: the best score without tools by the cutoff was around 61%. It was killed by an audit. The Qwen team analyzed all 2,500 questions and left only 668 unedited, corrected 1,143, and could not make a verdict on 689. In August 2026, Google switched to a verified subset, and HLE died by criterion E, 19 months after its release.

  • Terminal-Bench 3.0 lasted one month. The maintainers released 4.0, removed 8 out of 74 tasks, and recalibrated resources. If more than 10% of tasks are replaced or the protocol changes, I consider it a new benchmark, and the old version as dead by criterion C.

The last rule is the most contentious, so below I will separately show what happens if a new version is considered a dropout from observation rather than death.

Who I Counted

The second trap is the sample. In previous versions of this research, I selected "flagship" benchmarks manually, which is precisely what breaks survival analysis: the sample included famous ones, and among old benchmarks, the famous ones were mostly long-lived. Therefore, the sample is now defined by a rule. A benchmark is included if at least two of the nine labs reported on it in the official model release materials (blog, card, system card, technical report), or one lab reported on it in at least three releases. For years before 2023, the sources were papers about models, from GPT-1 and BERT to PaLM and Flan-PaLM. In total, there were 230 releases and 6,652 entries of the form "release × benchmark × score." Benchmarks are counted up to the version, exam year, or domain: SWE-bench, SWE-bench Verified, and SWE-bench Pro are three different benchmarks, as are AIME 2024, 2025, and 2026.

I did not count benchmarks with no ceiling, like Elo arenas and perplexity, internal lab evaluations without public tasks, and behavioral evaluations (refusals, honesty) that have no correct answer, only a norm. Collections like BIG-bench, speech, translation, and exam batteries were also excluded. This left 224 benchmarks. For each, the dataset contains dates of all events with links to sources and quotes. Why the Naive Median Lies

The most common way to calculate benchmark lifespan is to take the dead ones, group them by release year, and calculate the median. On my data, this yields a striking picture:

From over two years down to two and a half months, an acceleration of more than tenfold. The headline writes itself. But a benchmark released in 2026 could not have lived more than eight months by September 30. Only the short-lived ones among its contemporaries had time to die; the long lives hadn't ended yet. Therefore, for young cohorts, the median of the dead will be small, no matter how slowly benchmarks actually die.

To understand how much of this drop is real acceleration and how much is the effect of short observation, I built a null model—a world where benchmark lifespans never change. For each actual birth date in the sample, I simulate a lifespan from the overall distribution, truncate the observation at September 30, 2026, and calculate the same median. I do this 2,000 times.

Even in a world without any acceleration, the median of the dead drops from 19 months for old cohorts to two for the newest. The observed 8 and 2.5 months for benchmarks from 2025 and 2026 lie within the null model's band, so this statistic says nothing about the newest cohorts. The real signal is visible at the other end: for old cohorts, the median of the dead was at or above the upper bound of the band. Old benchmarks indeed lived longer. To measure this honestly, an estimate that accounts for the living is needed.

Half-Life: 23 Months and a Doubling Gap

I calculate the half-life using the Kaplan-Meier estimator: living benchmarks are included as censored observations, abandoned ones similarly, at the date of last activity. In lifelines, it's a few lines:

import pandas as pd
from lifelines import KaplanMeierFitter, CoxPHFitter

d = pd.read_csv("halflife_dataset_v3.csv")
m = d[d.stratum == "main"]                      # 224 benchmarks
km = KaplanMeierFitter().fit(m.T_months, m.event)
print(km.median_survival_time_)                 # 23.0

for era, g in m.groupby(m.birth_year <= 2022):
    k = KaplanMeierFitter().fit(g.T_months, g.event)
    print("≤2022" if era else "2023+", k.median_survival_time_)   # 2023+ 19.0, ≤2022 38.0

x = m[["T_months", "event"]].assign(year=m.birth_year - 2023)
print(CoxPHFitter().fit(x, "T_months", "event").hazard_ratios_)  # year ≈ 1.22

Across the entire sample, the half-life is 23 months (95% CI 19–27). A quarter of benchmarks die in the first year; by two years, less than half remain alive.

There's a twofold difference between eras. Benchmarks released in 2016–2022 had a half-life of 38 months (95% CI 27–69), and 4% died in the first year. For those released in 2023 and later, it's 19 months (15–23), and about a third die in the first year. With each subsequent release year, the risk of death increases by approximately 22%. For a typical public static test, this means: one released in 2018 would have lived about 53 months, in 2022—31, in 2026—18.

New Benchmarks Aren't Easier, Models Are Just Faster

The first objection is obvious: perhaps new benchmarks are simply released closer to the ceiling? Labs release tests on which their models already look good, creators rush, and don't have time to make the set difficult.

This can be checked. For 203 benchmarks, the best score at release is known, and the gap to the threshold is calculated from it. I calculate it in logits because rising from 50% to 60% and from 80% to 90% are tasks of different difficulty, and in logits, the frontier trajectory is almost straight. The median gap for old benchmarks is 2.02 logits, for new ones—2.14, or 35 and 38 percentage points to the threshold. New benchmarks start even slightly further from the ceiling than old ones.

I took the speed from an independent source. Epoch AI has frontier trajectories for 21 benchmarks from my sample. On old benchmarks, the frontier grew by 0.75 logits per year, on new ones—by 1.56, i.e., twice as fast. If you divide the gap by the speed, a typical old benchmark should have lived about 32 months, a new one—about 16. In fact, they lived 38 and 19 months.

On individual benchmarks, this calculation is rougher, but it still works. The survival model with gap and release year shows the same: each extra logit of gap extends life by about 1.5 times, and with an equal gap, a newer benchmark still lives shorter. The path to the ceiling is the same; models simply traverse it faster.

The extreme case is benchmarks born dead. All annual math competitions of the last two years—AIME 2025 and 2026, HMMT Feb 2026 and Nov 2025, USAMO 2026—were solved by models at 90% or higher in the same month the competition took place: MathArena and the labs themselves run models immediately after the exam. Needle-in-a-Haystack was released in November 2023 with a GPT-4-128K score above 90% right away; OmniDocBench 1.5 was released alongside the MinerU2.5 model from the same lab, which scored 90.67%.

What Kills Benchmarks

Of 116 deaths, 84 are due to models reaching the threshold. In another 12 cases, creators themselves declared the benchmark saturated before models reached the threshold: GLUE, SQuAD 1.1, HealthBench, Video-MME, and OSWorld-Verified ended this way. The creators' thresholds vary greatly: Vals AI declared the first version of Finance Agent saturated when the best score was 60.7%. The remaining 20 deaths are defects. Creators admitted errors in tasks or evaluation and released a corrected version, or the dataset was compromised by an independent audit or a trivial method.

The causes of death compete: a benchmark that dies from defects will not die from models. Therefore, I calculate the proportions using the Aalen-Johansen estimator.

In the first two years of life, among benchmarks from 2016–2022, 29% died from models, and none from defects. Among those released in 2023 and later, 45% die from models, 14% from defects and version changes, and another 5% are abandoned. Every seventh new benchmark dies undefeated, and two-thirds of such deaths occur in agentic environments.

Of the 29 dead agentic environments, 13 broke themselves, while among the 81 dead static tests, there were six such cases. The biographies of agentic environments are similar. Terminal-Bench changed four versions in just over a year, and none lasted longer than six months: either the community found problems with the tasks, or the maintainers themselves admitted the tasks were saturated. The airline domain from τ-bench died from defects in February 2026: an independent SABER audit found 31 out of 50 tasks to be ill-defined, and Anthropic stopped reporting results on it.

SWE-bench lasted ten months until the Verified version, during which 68% of tasks were filtered out as ill-defined or having unfair tests. Verified itself lasted a year and a half until OpenAI, a co-author of the dataset, stated that "we have stopped reporting SWE-bench Verified scores" and suggested others do the same. OSWorld lasted 15 months until a version with tasks corrected based on community complaints, which was then superseded after 11 months by OSWorld 2.0 authors who called it an overestimation of real progress. Toolathlon, MCPMark, and APEX-Agents died similarly. Static tests also have similar stories, but less frequently: MMMU, StoryCloze, FrontierMath.

Death from a trivial method also occurs. In October 2024, a paper was published where a "null model," which responds to any query with the same string, achieved an 86.5% length-controlled win rate on AlpacaEval 2.0—higher than any real model. The maintainers added it to their table, in first place.

One can argue whether the release of a new version should be considered death. If it's considered dropping out of observation, the half-life of new benchmarks increases from 19 to 24 months, but the gap with the pre-2023 era does not disappear.

What to Take Away If You're Making a Benchmark

Plan for a lifespan of 1.5 to 2 years, not five. If you need a tool that lasts for years, it needs an update mechanism: a single successful set of tasks won't be enough.

The initial gap is the only thing you directly control, and it works: each logit of gap extends the expected life by about 1.5 times. Therefore, 30% for the best model at the start is the benchmark's resource. If the leader is already above 80% at release, the benchmark will last months.

State in your paper what you will consider saturation and measure the human baseline on the same split using the described protocol. Otherwise, it will be decided for you, and most likely, at 90% of the scale.

If it's an agentic environment, a versioning policy is needed from day one: fixed environment versions, a changelog, separate results tables for each version. Nearly half of the dead agentic environments in my data died due to their own defects, and each new version resets the results history. Auditing tasks before release is cheaper than a Verified version six months later.

A closed test does not extend life, neither in my data nor according to EvalEval. It protects against leaks but not against model progress.

How Long Will OenoBench Last?

Returning to the question that started it all. OenoBench is a static test from 2026 with no measured human baseline, so it will die by criterion B when someone achieves 90%. Leaders at release score 81–83%. The same survival model, trained on 203 benchmarks, gives it about nine months: the chance of surviving a year is about a third, two years—5–7%. The difficult half, where leaders score 65–70%, will last 12–14 months according to the same model.

Similar benchmarks in my data died this way. Of the six benchmarks from 2025–2026 with a similar initial gap, five died in the first six months. DeepSearchQA from Google DeepMind was released in December 2025 with a best score of 81.9 F1, and in February, Claude Opus 4.6 achieved 91.3. ExploitBench was released in May 2026 with a leader at 78%, and in September, GPT-6 Astra showed 100%.

However, the OenoBench data itself offers a counterargument. Opus 4.7, released in April 2026, scored lower than o3, released a year earlier: over the year, the best score on my benchmark has not increased. The survival model is trained on benchmarks that frontier labs report on and optimize for, and it only knows the speed of progress from them. No one is optimizing for wine knowledge, and progress here only occurs as a side effect of general model growth.

I can now answer the reviewers with numbers. A static benchmark from 2026 with a 90% threshold has a 50% chance of lasting a year if the leader at release scores around 70%. Two years—if the leader scores slightly above a third of the scale, three—if less than 20%. An agentic environment, which also breaks itself, needs a leader around 10% to last two years.

For OenoBench itself, I have two forecasts. According to the model trained on frontier benchmarks, by May 2027, it will most likely be dead. According to its own trajectory—almost certainly alive. If the second forecast proves true, it means a significant portion of the speed we see on popular benchmarks is the speed of optimization for them. If the first—models are indeed getting smarter faster than we can create tests for them. I will check in May 2027 and post the result here in the comments.
