When shopping online, a user buys not just the product itself, but also an idea of what it will be like in real life. This idea is increasingly shaped by visuals created or enhanced with AI. Today, a seller can change the background, generate a lifestyle scene, create multiple versions of the main image, or "complete" the surroundings without a full photoshoot in minutes.

For businesses, this means faster, cheaper, and more manageable visual content. For users, it means a growing feeling that pictures are becoming harder to trust. If a photo can be significantly improved without changing the product itself, the visual ceases to be a reliable guide for the buyer.

This is especially important for e-commerce, where choices largely depend on how an offer looks on its product page. According to the Baymard Institute, 56% of users' first actions on a product page are related to viewing images. Salsify's "2025 Consumer Research" also shows that 77% of shoppers consider photos and videos "very" or "extremely" important when evaluating an offer. At the same time, 71% of them returned online orders in the past year due to incorrect product content—for example, when images did not match what they received.

As a result, the market is gradually facing a new conflict: the more convincing AI visuals become, the harder it is to separate marketing presentation from the actual product characteristics. And it is around this conflict that the architecture of trust in e-commerce is now beginning to be rebuilt.

The Product Card as an Interface of Trust

A product card in e-commerce has long ceased to be just a digital storefront. In essence, it has become a replacement for physical contact with the product. A user cannot touch the fabric, assess the weight of a device, view an item in different lighting, or understand its real proportions—all their perception of the purchase is formed through the content within the card.

This is precisely why images in e-commerce work not only as a marketing tool but also as part of the user experience. They help a person reduce uncertainty before ordering. This is particularly important in categories where appearance, texture, and understanding how the item will look in everyday use are paramount: fashion, cosmetics, home goods, accessories, furniture, electronics.

However, trust within a product card is formed not by a single element, but by a combination of several:

  • official brand visuals;

  • specifications and descriptions;

  • customer reviews;

  • user photos and videos;

  • the reputation of the seller and the platform itself.

In practice, this means that today the card performs two functions simultaneously. On the one hand, it must attract attention and help sell. On the other, it must accurately explain to a person what exactly they are purchasing.

In such conditions, an error in content design is perceived not as a failed creative, but as a violation of user expectations. And the more the visual influences the purchase decision, the more painfully the market reacts to any discrepancy between the image and the actual experience after delivery.

The New Economics of Product Visuals

Generative AI in e-commerce is rapidly moving from the category of experimental tools to a part of everyday work with product cards. Image generation and editing services are becoming increasingly integrated into the ecosystems of marketplaces, advertising platforms, and seller services, and the creation of such content is becoming significantly more automated.

This is largely because AI allows for a significant simplification and acceleration of product visual preparation. Previously, high-quality e-commerce content required studio shooting, models, photographers, retouching, and full-scale production. Now, a significant portion of these tasks can be performed faster and cheaper using AI. For example, Klarna stated that using generative AI to create marketing images allowed the company to reduce production costs by approximately $6 million annually and shorten the preparation cycle from six weeks to seven days.

This is particularly important for the market due to the scale of modern e-commerce. According to AKIT, the volume of online retail in Russia in 2025 reached 11.5 trillion rubles, an increase of 28% year-on-year. In such an environment, every product card becomes an object of constant optimization: sellers test the main photo, adapt visuals for different audiences, and compete literally for seconds of user attention.

As a result, the visual presentation of a product ceases to be static. The same card can now be quickly adapted for different scenarios, seasons, audiences, and advertising mechanics. AI makes such variability cheap and mass-produced, turning the content within the card into a dynamic part of the digital storefront.

The Crisis of Trust in the "Perfect" Picture

As AI visuals become more convincing, customer behavior also changes. The problem here is no longer so much technological as psychological: users increasingly doubt not the quality of the image, but how closely it relates to the actual product.

Recent studies show that most people can no longer consistently distinguish AI-generated images from real photographs. In a Microsoft experiment, participants identified synthetic content with an accuracy of about 62%—only slightly higher than random guessing. Similar conclusions are drawn by ACM, Clutch, and Conjointly: modern AI visuals are practically indistinguishable from traditional photography for many users.

But more importantly, buyers are beginning to gradually reconsider the very mechanism of checking trust. If previously brand visuals were often sufficient for making a decision, now a "verification behavior" mode is increasingly activated:

  • reviewing feedback;

  • searching for real photos;

  • watching videos and unboxings;

  • comparing the product on different platforms;

  • searching for mentions on social media or by bloggers.

In other words, trust is shifting from the official product card to the community of buyers.

Against this backdrop, the role of user-generated content is sharply increasing. Baymard Institute notes that many buyers specifically look for photos from other people because they perceive them as more reliable confirmation of how the product looks "in real life." PowerReviews also records that 80% of consumers consider user-generated photos more valuable than brand visuals.

Simultaneously, attitudes towards "too perfect" images are changing. Getty Images research shows a growing demand for more natural and less excessively processed photographs, while overly "polished" AI visuals are often perceived as less authentic.

This effect is particularly noticeable in scenarios where post-purchase errors are more acutely felt—for example, when choosing clothing, skincare products, interior items, or expensive electronics. A study by University of Zaragoza researchers found that in emotionally significant and high-risk situations, audiences are more likely to perceive images created by generative AI as less plausible and potentially misleading.

As a result, official visuals are gradually ceasing to be independent proof of product quality for the user. The ability of the seller to confirm the visual with real user experience is becoming increasingly important, rather than the image itself.

How the Market and Platforms Respond to the Challenges of AI Visuals

As AI visuals become widespread, the market is beginning to seek a new balance between the attractiveness of the product card and the accuracy of the visual promise. There is no complete abandonment of generated images—the economic effect of speed, scalability, and cost reduction of content is too great. However, platforms and brands are gradually establishing limitations on how far one can go in "improving" a product.

Wildberries' experience is particularly telling here. On the one hand, the platform actively develops AI tools for sellers: a photo editor, composition generation functions, a "Photo Studio" with virtual models, and A/B testing of the main photo. Essentially, the platform itself is making AI a part of the standard card design infrastructure.

On the other hand, Wildberries is strengthening requirements for visual accuracy. The platform's rules explicitly state that photographs must "accurately and fully correspond to the product," and the quality of the card affects not only clicks but also further behavioral signals—conversion, buy-through rate, rating, and returns. In other words, the marketplace simultaneously encourages sellers to make content more attractive and monitors how well such presentation holds up to the test of purchase.

In July 2026, Wildberries went even further and was the first among Russian marketplaces to begin testing the "Looks like AI" label for photos in reviews where the system detects signs of significant AI processing. The label does not mean the review is unreliable, but it gives the buyer an additional reference point when evaluating how the product looks in reality. This well demonstrates the new market logic. An overly "idealized" card may increase CTR or conversion in the initial stage, but at the same time increase the probability of returns, negative reviews, and a decrease in trust towards the seller.

Against this backdrop, the market is actively moving towards a hybrid model where different types of visuals begin to perform different functions: branded and enhanced AI content is responsible for attention, real photos and videos—for confirming expectations, and user-generated content—for social proof and reducing uncertainty before purchase.

Transparency is also growing in importance. Google already requires preserving metadata for AI-generated images in Merchant Center, Etsy is introducing mandatory disclosure of AI use, and in the EU, since August 2, 2026, AI Act requirements for labeling AI content, including machine-readable tags for synthetic materials, have been applied. A similar approach, albeit in a softer form, is also enshrined in Russia: since March 2027, Federal Law No. 243-FZ provides for the possibility of voluntarily labeling audio and visual materials created using large fundamental AI models.

In fact, e-commerce is beginning to shift from competing for the "most beautiful card" to competing for the most reliable visual promise.

What Businesses Should Do to Avoid Undermining Expectations

For businesses, the question is no longer whether generative AI can be used in content creation. The main challenge now is where the line is drawn between visually enhancing a product and destroying trust during actual use.

The first rule is to separate content by roles. One should not try to make an AI image both an attention-grabbing tool and the primary confirmation of what the item will be like in reality. Elements that directly affect the risk of post-order error—the main photo, color, texture, fit, size, and комплектация—should be kept as close as possible to the actual offering. Generative AI should be used primarily where content serves an emotional and marketing function and does not serve as a literal confirmation of characteristics.

The second rule is to treat user photos and videos not as an addition to the product card, but as an essential layer of trust formation. Brand visuals alone are no longer enough for the buyer. Therefore, it is important to systematically work with feedback: encourage uploading photos and videos after receiving an order, pin the most useful materials, and separately track which specific discrepancies between the card and the real experience most often cause customer dissatisfaction.

Marketplaces themselves support this trend. For example, Wildberries is developing mechanics for pinned reviews and videos from Wibes, allowing user-generated content to be displayed at the top of the card. As a result, reviews with photos and videos, short reviews, and "live" product usage scenarios begin to function as a separate layer of social proof within the purchasing process.

The third rule is to stop considering clickability the main criterion for a "good" product card. In the era of generative AI, creating a strong first impression is becoming easier, but an increase in clicks does not yet mean an increase in quality sales. If a card increases the number of orders but simultaneously increases returns, complaints, and post-purchase disappointment, the business begins to optimize not profit, but visual self-deception.

In the context of mass generative AI use, it is precisely such approaches that are gradually beginning to separate sustainable sales growth from short-term spikes built on false expectations.

What to Expect Next

In the coming years, AI visuals will likely cease to be perceived as a new technology and become a standard part of e-commerce infrastructure—much like recommendation algorithms or personalization once became the norm. For the market, the focus will shift from the mere fact of AI use to managing expectations in an environment where more and more content is created or modified using generative models. It can be assumed that platforms will begin to more actively evaluate not only the formal quality of the product card but also how well the images correspond to reality and do not create false expectations for buyers.

Simultaneously, the market will face the effect of visual inflation. As high-quality AI images become cheap and widely available, it will become increasingly difficult to stand out from competitors with a "beautiful picture." If virtually any seller can quickly create an "ideal" product card, then value will shift from the ability to attract attention to the ability to inspire trust.

Against this backdrop, content labeling and origin verification mechanisms will be strengthened. Such tools are already being implemented by major digital platforms: for example, TikTok requires labeling realistic AI content and is implementing tools for its automatic recognition. Over time, similar systems may become as common a part of marketplace operations as the analysis of reviews, seller ratings, or checks for suspicious activity are today.

This is precisely why, in the foreseeable future, the competitive advantage will no longer be the ability to generate the most beautiful AI images, but the skill to build a system of visual expectations in which the user does not feel deceived after the purchase.

The Price of a "Beautiful Picture"

The market is gradually moving from an economy of visual attention to an economy of managed trust. The product card ceases to be just a storefront or an advertising medium and transforms into a point where the buyer's perception of future experience is formed, and an error in visual presentation can cost not only a click or conversion but also returns, loyalty, and trust in both the brand and the platform as a whole.

In e-commerce of 2026, a picture indeed often becomes "better" than the product—in the sense that it is easier to improve than the product itself, packaging, logistics, or customer service. As a result, visuals increasingly function not just as a reflection of the product, but as an independent element of digital commerce that can be endlessly optimized, personalized, and scaled.

Paradoxically, this is precisely what makes trust an even more valuable resource for the market. In an environment where synthetic content becomes almost indistinguishable from "real," the main guide for the user becomes not the images themselves, but the seller's reputation, the platform's transparency, and the predictability of the post-purchase experience.

In this sense, brands and entrepreneurs in e-commerce are beginning to compete not only for user attention but also for the right to remain "real enough" in their eyes. Because on marketplaces, a picture sells twice: first—the promise, then—the product itself. And if after delivery the promise is not fulfilled, behind the beautiful picture, the user begins to see not service and quality, but carefully packaged disappointment.
