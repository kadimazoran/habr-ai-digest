How I Revived a 19th Century Painting: Photoshop, Local LLM, MiniMax H3, and After Effects

In this article, we'll explore whether it's possible to take a 19th-century historical painting and transform it into a full-fledged animated scene, ensuring the composition, style, and character positions remain intact. The main tools discussed will be Adobe Photoshop for dissecting the painting, a local multimodal model Qwen 3.8 27B Q5_K_XL via LM Studio for prompt preparation, MiniMax H3 video generation in ComfyUI, and final compositing in After Effects. The source material is Lawrence Alma-Tadema's "Anno 1812. Kapitein Benthien aan de Beresina" (also known as The Crossing of the River Berezina - 1812). Where I Started: The Painting and Why You Can't Just "Run" It Through a Video Model

With images like this, you immediately run into three problems.

First: there are many characters, and almost all of them interact with each other and the environment – someone is holding a log, someone is standing waist-deep in water, someone is blocking another.

Second: if you cut a person out of the frame, a hole is left behind. And behind them should be a wooden structure, another soldier, or part of a bridge – something that was hidden by their body in the original.

Third and most importantly: if you run the entire painting through a video model with a single prompt, you have almost no control. The model will change faces, clothing, architecture, direction of movement – anything it wants. On a complex scene, this is guaranteed to result in a mess.

Therefore, I took an element-by-element approach: I broke the painting down into fragments and animated each one separately.

As a result, instead of one large generation, you get a set of independent elements, each of which can be controlled individually.

Dissecting the Painting into Layers in Photoshop

The first stage is separating the painting into individual elements. I opened the original in Photoshop and started selecting the characters and objects I planned to animate.

There's one rule here, without which everything will fall apart later: I save each fragment in its original coordinate system. That is, I don't crop the cut-out person to the minimum rectangle, but leave them in the same position relative to the size of the entire canvas. Why? So that later in After Effects, I can simply import the layers, and they will align with each other without recalculating scale and coordinates. I don't want to manually adjust each piece – it's tedious and error-prone.

The dissection itself boils down to routine, but it's unavoidable: I select a character or object, move it to a separate layer, check the selection boundaries (especially along the edges of clothing and hair), save the coordinates, and group layers by meaning – water separately, people separately, structures separately.

After this, each object can be manipulated independently of the others.

Inpainting: What to Do with the Hole Where the Character Stood

As soon as I move a character to its own layer, an empty space remains in the original – the very spot their body used to occupy. You can't just delete it; it would create a void.

For example, a soldier stands in front of a wooden structure and another person. Remove him – and behind him should be planks, the shoulder of that person, snow. All this needs to be painted in as if the soldier was never there.

For this, I use Inpainting. But you can't just "paint over" the spot: the restored area must blend with the painting in terms of color, light, brushstroke texture, and perspective. Otherwise, it might pass in a static frame, but after animation, the eye immediately catches the mismatch – the background starts to "drift" relative to the moving figure.

Therefore, I put the character back in place and look at the seams: is there a color shift, do the brushstroke directions match? I check the boundaries particularly carefully – this is where the seam is most often visible.

This kind of "operation" needs to be performed for each fragment of the painting.

The result is two independent elements: a clean background and a separate object that can be animated.

Faces: Detailing Without Redoing the Pose

The next problem is detailing. In the painting, many figures are small, and if you zoom in on a face, you can see that it's simply not detailed enough for convincing animation: when moving, such "blurry" faces immediately give away the manipulation.

Therefore, I additionally run the cut-out characters through enhancement (generation), focusing on the faces. But the task here is not ordinary upscaling. It's necessary to increase detail without shifting anything: the head position, proportions, original pose, and overall artistic character – everything must remain as it was.

Additionally, at this stage, I remove the background behind the character that I will animate later. The logic is that other characters who will also be animated will be behind them. Therefore, it's much better to insert a green background and then remove it with an eyedropper during compositing after animation.

This concludes the preparation of the source material. It's time to move on to animation.

Prompts via Local LLM (LM Studio)

I run a local multimodal model in LM Studio (in this project, it's Qwen 3.8 27B, Q5_K_XL quantization) and use it as an intermediary between me and the video model.

The idea is as follows: instead of manually writing a long, detailed prompt every time, I provide the model with three things – my base request describing the desired scene behavior, the prepared painting fragment, and a set of constraints to preserve the composition. The model looks at the image and compiles a specialized prompt for MiniMax H3 from this.

When preparing the prompt, I keep several things in mind: which specific object should move; what the movement should be like (it must correspond to the pose and historical context, not just "waving hands"); how the character interacts with the environment – are they standing in water, holding a log, pushing another person; and what constraints must not be violated – clothing, appearance, composition, and the position of neighbors must remain. For the camera, I usually fix the initial shooting point unless separate camera dynamics are needed.

The output is a fairly dense prompt. In my case, the model generated it in English and very detailed: eight soldiers in dark uniforms and hats walk from left to right on a snowy bank, carrying long logs and planks for bridge repair, their steps are even and purposeful, the camera is completely static, medium shot at 35mm, muted historical palette, canvas texture. This is the level of description needed for the video model to avoid going off track.

Generating Fragments in MiniMax H3 / ComfyUI

I run video generation through ComfyUI using the MiniMax H3 model (in the workflow, it's minimax_h3_fi2va_pruned_int8_convrot, CLIP from qwen3vl_32b, VAE fp16; for acceleration, I attach LORA for 10 steps and enable SagaAttention mode in ComfyUI, and add further acceleration via the Spectrum node).

The idea remains the same: each prepared fragment is animated separately. This way, the video model doesn't try to process the entire painting with dozens of characters simultaneously but works with one area – one person, water movement, a wooden structure, or a group of people.

After starting, I evaluate the result not just on "beautiful/not beautiful," but on whether it's suitable for compositing: has the original pose been preserved; has the number of objects and limbs changed; is a character's arm or leg passing through another's body or head; does the movement correspond to the physics of the environment; have any details appeared that were not in the painting. If not suitable – I correct the prompt and run it again.

Here's an important point that isn't usually shown in demo videos: far from every generation makes it to editing. In my experience, for every truly successful attempt, there are four or five unsuccessful or "almost" variants. This is not a universal statistic for all models, but an observation specific to this production process. The more objects and the more complex their interaction, the higher the chance of artifacts. For the officers with logs and planks on the left side of the frames, it took more than 15 attempts because in some moments one of the officers passed through the body of a soldier repairing the bridge.

Physics is a separate problem: flowing water, people waist-deep in icy water who are simultaneously pushing logs and holding onto each other. Animating a person on the bank is much easier than the same person in the water in the middle of a crossing.

Therefore, I wouldn't assess the complexity of such work by the length of the final video. A few seconds of clean animation can cost a lot of preparation, generation, and manual refinement. (Or, as an alternative, many tokens (or credits) for cloud models).

The close-up was generated at 1.8MP (1824x1024). All other shots were at 0.3MP (736x416).

Assembly and Masks in After Effects

When suitable fragments are available, I move on to integrating them into After Effects. I assemble the composition from the prepared layers and generated video, placing everything according to the original painting's coordinates – hence the rule about the coordinate system from the second point. This preserves the original structure, and the AI fragments don't look like separate videos simply placed side-by-side.

Next, the task is to carefully blend the moving objects with the rest of the scene. For this, additional masks and separating the character from the background are needed. This is where Roto Brush comes in: it selects the object across a sequence of frames, I check the mask's stability, adjust the boundaries, and clean up problematic areas.

I pay particular attention to clothing, hands, and feet – small elements where selection errors are most visible. After this, the final assembly: I combine the animated elements with the restored background, check masks and overlaps, adjust the position and duration of fragments, remove visible seams, and harmonize the overall style so that the pieces don't stand out in terms of color and lighting.

Conclusion

The result is an animated interpretation of a historical painting: individual characters and environmental elements move independently, while the original artistic composition is preserved. In this project, traditional painting meets modern CGI and AI tools – and neither replaces the other.

I believe this approach will be useful not only for individual videos but also for museum exhibits, multimedia installations, historical exhibitions, and educational projects. Local AI allows for the creation of custom production pipelines where multiple models work together with familiar VFX tools.
