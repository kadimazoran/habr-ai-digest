First Attempts at Programming

It all began in mid-2024 when I was doing a small remastering of the cartoon "Chip 'n Dale." I ran into an issue with the subtitles – to attach them to my video, I needed to somehow adjust their timestamps, and none of the dozen or so programs I tested properly supported the batch subtitle editing from DVD discs that I needed.

Without much thought, I decided to test the coding potential of the then-cutting-edge ChatGPT 3.5. Together, we performed a very simple reverse-engineering, and the result was the creation of VobSub Index File Fast Editor. At that moment, I realized that AI could be used in my craft, even if I didn't understand a single bit of the code it wrote.

A sketch:

In short, after tormenting myself with "feedback adjustments" for another 2–3 hours, I reached the level of prompt engineering akin to talking to a child.

And then I made a fatal mistake – I wanted to add saving the window position and size upon restart. Tried once – didn't work; twice – didn't work; three times...

On the third day, the AI and I dug through everything, and the result was a "race condition."

The AI promptly enlightened me: when the program closes, code snippets finish in a random order due to the absence of any rules, and the window size trap triggers when the window no longer exists.

I had to redo everything within a week using the MVP pattern, as advised by the neural network.

It was painful and unpleasant – all sorts of bugs and glitches reappeared during the rework, but we somehow managed to close the issue, and the trap started working stably and consistently.

And, by the way, that Windows-style interface didn't last long. Specifically, within a week, I copied the parts I needed "by eye" because that ready-made shell turned out to be terribly buggy, and many things couldn't be changed the way I wanted.

2.2. Bureaucratization, Shaders, and the Failure to Migrate to Rust

Version v8.2.0 (04/05/2026)

This update was significantly delayed, by as much as six months – I decided to overhaul everything I could get my hands on.

First, I conceived a wonder weapon – to create a video recording function for comparisons, but not just any recording, one that would capture in the original resolution, whether the image was 4K or 8K. And also to be able to crop it directly within my app. It would be reasonable to be told, "You're asking for a lot," but Gemini responded differently – it told me about a new technology for magician programmers called Redux.

Visually: it's a bureaucracy simulator – if previously, to draw a line, a function only needed to approach and draw with a brush on a public canvas, now I've installed a computer with a brush right in front of the canvas, and everyone who wants to draw must first input what and where they want to draw. The computer records everything in a detailed log and only then draws strictly according to the stencil. Thus, a reproducible list of commands was created. The system took some time to build, and it turned out, let's say, to be made of "duct tape and baling wire" due to various delays, but it worked!

It was the beginning of 2026, and Claude Code was gaining traction. At that time, it was only available as a terminal, but I immediately started testing the new technology. Along the way, I also uncovered an old pitfall – switching to GPU rendering, while speeding it up, had a significant limitation.

Currently, the maximum resolution a graphics card can display is ~16–32K, and even then, not on all APIs, but I sometimes have larger images. To overcome this, I had to create a primitive LOD system: the processor cuts large images into smaller pieces in a grid format across several levels, and the GPU positions them so that the seams on the screen and level transitions during zooming are not noticeable.

However, for some reason, the implementation process took an incredibly long time, about 2 weeks: today I fixed a gap that appeared during zooming, and tomorrow a new error – the grid doesn't align and drifts apart.

2.4. Huge Debts from a Five-Year Plan in One Month, and the Day DeepSeek Deleted All Files Irreversibly, and Then Restored Them in the Most Unexpected Way

I won't keep you waiting – all these engine transitions (from CPU to GPU, from one API to another), adding LODs, and other maneuvers led me to the question, "How does this all work together now?" And it wasn't in vain – it turned out that internally, there were about 20 temporary copies of the image, each considering itself the original, and it was a miracle the system even worked (a boring explanation with diagrams should have followed, but I cut it out). In short, another 100500+ hours were spent on fixes. And now it seemed like I could finally exhale and publish the release...

And what could have happened next? DeepSeek, right during the final "update polishing," ran a test that could PHYSICALLY DELETE FOLDERS. This is, well, just fantastic. An incredible concept.

It ran it and simply erased the entire project over a vast period. BUT, just as it messed up, it also figured out how to fix it.

Here was its idea: in AI programming environments, there are session recordings with all commands, reads, and messages, and sometimes even file copies. So, I had a copy of the project from the previous month. And DeepSeek put 2 + 2 together without any prompts and decided to create a script that would play back all the changes from two environments – Claude and OpenCode – on top of it.

Of course, no logs are designed for this – their maximum purpose is for you to log in and view your chat history in a convenient interface. So, I didn't believe this nonsense. But after spending about 5–7 hours, it managed: it wrote huge scripts, and almost all changes were correctly applied – 1289 (97.5%) individual, diverse actions, from reading files to git commands, deleting, creating, and mass file edits.

After these necromantic events, I realized that if something like this happened again, I wouldn't have any nerves left, so I tasked the agents (AI) with creating a backup system.

3. New Era of OpenCode Orchestras

A new period has arrived in the world of AI programming – AI manages AI. There's an orchestrator; it receives a large task but doesn't execute it itself. Instead, it sends individual parts of the task to other agents for parallel execution, retaining the role of verifying the results and monitoring plan execution.

SEEMINGLY, it's all simple in words – you give a command, and they somehow run it themselves.

In reality: everyone lies in their reports, can change the same files simultaneously, do tasks that aren't theirs, nobody remembers why or how something works, and when there are multiple orchestrators... If not stretched out, it required developing a whole separate complex setup and refining it for 2 months.
