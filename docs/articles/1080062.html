For a solo developer, working locally with an agent might remain a personal craft. For a team, the same scheme turns into a systemic problem: the company no longer sees how the result is actually produced.

Previously, most engineering work happened within tasks, commits, and code reviews. Now, a separate production process emerges between the task assignment and the final code: the agent explores the project, builds a plan, writes the implementation, and runs checks; the human reviews the result, changes skills, switches models, edits the harness, and makes decisions. But the task tracker still shows a single line: 'task with developer.'

Because of this, agent development scales strangely. Each person gets a stronger personal factory, but the team doesn't get a shared production. It sees the product, not the method of its creation—and therefore cannot measure, compare, transfer, or improve that method. A company won't become AI-native until this work remains invisible and is developed based on intuition.

Agent Pipeline Develops Blindly

A team cannot freely test alternatives, compare them under identical conditions, and calculate the full cost of the accepted result.

1. Cannot Freely Experiment with Agent Pipelines

A developer receives not the company's computational budget, but a specific subscription. Therefore, they explore not the best way to solve a task, but the best way to stay within the tool they've been given.

To try Claude instead of Codex, one needs to request a new subscription, wait for approval, and explain why it's needed. If the experiment fails, switching back is equally inconvenient. Even within a single subscription, the limit must be conserved for current work: the task must be done today, and searching for a better process competes for the same tokens.

Options that might prove fundamentally more effective than the usual process

  • Plan → Act → Review: Claude or Fable builds a plan, Codex implements it, Kimi independently verifies the result.

  • One Strong Agent: A single expensive model with maximum reasoning gets the full context, appropriate tools, and project skills.

  • Swarm: Ten expensive agents independently propose solutions, and a final agent assembles the best.

The latter option might be expensive in terms of tokens but cheap in terms of human attention: the task is one-shot, and the developer spends fifteen minutes accepting the result. However, testing this hypothesis within a personal quota is impossible.

A personal quota shifts the selection criterion: instead of seeking the most effective scheme, the developer tries not to exhaust the available limit. As a result, the developer becomes tied to the first available process and improves it locally, without testing fundamentally different options.
