Recently, I spent almost fourteen hours at the computer. All this time I was working on a program that is supposed to help people save time.

By the end of the day, my program's update had not yet been released. I hadn't looked for clients, written a single line of my book, or even come close to finishing other projects I had planned.

Instead, I got a new to-do list.

ChatGPT suggested prioritizing.

I agreed.

Although it all started quite differently.

How the Obstacles Disappeared

I've always had more ideas than the capacity to realize them.

Write a book. Create a program. Research some economic pattern. Invent a business. Make a film.

There were plenty of ideas.

What was missing was the one small thing — the ability to implement them all.

To create a program, you need to learn to program. To write a book, you need to find time. To start a business, you need money, specialists, organization.

However, such a situation had its advantages.

You could come up with something grandiose, assess the scale of the upcoming work, discover a lack of resources, and calmly move on with your life.

Conscience clear. The idea was good. Circumstances didn't allow it.

And then ChatGPT appeared.

And the circumstances began to rapidly run out.

It turned out that you could not just discuss programming but also create working applications. Not just dream about a book, but break down scenes, characters, and dialogues. Not just believe claims from stock trading textbooks, but write a program and test them on historical data.

There was no longer an insurmountable wall between the idea and its implementation.

Instead, there was a very polite interlocutor who, to almost any suggestion, replied:

"Great idea! Let's implement it."

I should have been wary.

An Industrial Revolution in the Apartment

One such project was Jupiter — a local voice dictation program for Windows that recognizes speech and inputs text into other applications.

At first, I just wanted a convenient tool for myself. Then it turned out that the program could be turned into a full-fledged product.

An installer, an interface, licensing appeared. I started working on updates, testing, and bug fixing.

The story should have ended here with a happy ending. A person invented a program, artificial intelligence helped write it. One can enjoy the achievements of technological progress.

But the program, as it turned out, has an unpleasant characteristic.

If you create it, it starts demanding attention.

Recently, for example, Jupiter stopped properly recognizing sound from the microphone. The indicator showed that sound was coming in, but dictation wasn't working as it should. After restarting, everything returned to normal.

Great. Another investigation.

And the program needs updates, support, distribution. Users need instructions. Sales require a product page, and most importantly, that someone even knows about its existence.

ChatGPT, of course, was ready to help.

It's always ready, in fact. An unfailing companion.

But while I was busy with Jupiter, the book didn't disappear anywhere. I wanted to rewrite it, refine the characters, improve the dialogues. New stories emerged.

In parallel, I researched financial markets. For example, I tested trading strategies on historical data. I had to write code, understand the methodology, analyze the results.

Then I wanted to write about it on Habr.

Then ideas for new services, promotion, and monetization questions arose.

At some point, all this stopped looking like the hobbies of one person.

I had formed a small enterprise.

It had a development department, a research department, an editorial department, a marketing department, and something like strategic management.

One person served all departments.

Me.

The other employees were in the chat window. Seven of them, sitting on benches.

Paralysis

To be fair, artificial intelligence has indeed made me more productive. Without it, a significant portion of the listed projects would not have appeared at all.

But at one point, a question arose that hadn't existed before.

What should I tackle today?

Suppose Jupiter needs an update. This is reasonable: there's a working product, errors have been found, and they need to be fixed.

But at the same time, I need to look for clients. Because developing your own product doesn't mean it's making money yet.

And I also want to finish the book. It would be good to publish the research too.

And so you open ChatGPT to sort out priorities.

It suggests creating a plan.

Then it turns out that the plan doesn't account for some important circumstances.

We discuss the circumstances, adjust the plan, allocate time, assess prospects.

After some time, I have a perfectly organized to-do list.

None of which I've done yet.

And you can't say that all this is useless. The plan is quite reasonable. It just took time to create it.

And the most unpleasant thing is that I can actually implement almost every one of these tasks.

Previously, there were many reasons not to start. Now I have to look for reasons to stop.

Sometimes I feel like I've been given a superpower but forgot that it should have an off switch.

Artificial intelligence tirelessly offers solutions. It doesn't notice that I've been sitting in front of the monitor for ten hours. It doesn't worry that tomorrow I'll have to look for money, and the day after tomorrow release an update.

It has no book, no move, no financial obligations.

It has a wonderful life, in contrast to my workday.

No one, by the way, is forcing me to work fourteen hours. I'm my own boss.

And, apparently, a rather unpleasant one.

What About Unemployment?

Against the backdrop of all this, I periodically read forecasts about how many professions will disappear due to artificial intelligence.

Programmers will be replaced by neural networks. Designers will be replaced by neural networks. Writers, analysts, and many other specialists face the same fate.

I'm not going to predict the future of the labor market.

But my own experience has made me look at the situation from a different perspective.

Let's take Jupiter.

If it weren't for AI, this program might not exist at all. I didn't have the resources to assemble a development team and pay for product creation.

And if there were no program, there would be no need to test, promote, sell, and support it.

Now the product exists.

Moreover, AI allowed me to perform some work that, under other circumstances, would have required paying specialists.

It would seem that here it is — job cuts in action.

But then something interesting happened.

The program required new work. Other projects did too. And gradually, I began to think about attracting specialists, without whom I previously managed, because I couldn't even afford to start.

It turns out that the technology that allowed me to do without a team when creating a product has created the prerequisites for a team to emerge during its development.

Now, let's imagine that such a story happens not to one person.

AI reduces the cost of development, design, research, and creation of new services. Thanks to this, entrepreneurs can launch projects that were previously too complex or expensive.

Not all of them will be successful. But those that manage to find customers will need development, maintenance, promotion, and production.

And it's not necessarily about programmers.

New businesses can create demand for engineers, workers, installers, sales specialists, and many other professions.

This leads to an interesting economic hypothesis: AI can reduce the amount of labor required to create one product, but simultaneously increase the number of products created.

Which will be stronger — the reduction of labor per unit of product or the growth of total production volume — is an open question.

Of course, new tasks are not equal to new jobs. Buyers, revenue, and investments are needed. Some professions may indeed disappear, and the benefits of new technologies may be unevenly distributed.

But I think it's premature to conclude that the automation of individual operations will necessarily lead to a reduction in employment in the economy as a whole.

Perhaps we are on the verge of the emergence of many businesses that simply could not exist before.

And therefore, new opportunities to earn money.

In my case, new jobs are still a long way off. But I have already felt one consequence of technological progress.

Staff Shortage. Where to Find Hands?

When I started actively using artificial intelligence, it seemed to me that I could finally do without a team.

No need to look for a programmer for every idea. No need to assemble specialists at the start. You can experiment and create products yourself.

And it really worked out.

But now I increasingly need not new ChatGPT capabilities, but people who could handle the existing work.

Developers, designers, promotion specialists. People who are interested in creating products, conducting research, and bringing projects to completion.

For now, it's more about collaboration and partnership on specific tasks than about full-time hiring. Conditions, responsibilities, and payment will have to be discussed separately in any case.

And yet, the situation is amusing.

Everyone around is discussing how many people will be left without work due to artificial intelligence.

And I'm sitting here thinking about where to find people. I intended to replace the team with neural networks.

And now I'm looking for a team.

If I disappear from Habr, you know where to find me, not in ChatGPT, but look for me in the city squares with a high-held cardboard sign "LOOKING FOR PEOPLE".

I agreed.

It seems we're busy with something again.
