Why Did I Gather You Here?

This fight against AI has gone too far. Today, almost everyone even remotely connected with text is implementing a ban policy: universities, schools, scientific journals, forums:

  • Major scientific publishers require the declaration of AI use in articles, and Nature refuses to recognize a neural network as an author (Nature).

  • In the US, universities are re-stocking "blue books" for handwritten exams – demand has noticeably increased at Texas A&M, Florida, and Berkeley over the past two years (Fox News), and in China, during the Gaokao, Alibaba and Tencent services temporarily disable photo recognition and question answering (Fortune).

  • Habr, starting June 2026, directly prohibits texts "written and edited with the help of neural networks" (Habr rules).

But there are also those who have realized that fighting AI is ineffective and are starting to regulate rather than ban:

  • Estonia is launching the AI Leap program: instead of banning AI, the country is negotiating with OpenAI and Anthropic to provide AI tools to 20,000 high school students and 3,000 teachers (HM.ee).

  • In Singapore, the school chatbot Learning Assistant asks guiding questions instead of providing ready-made solutions, and the teacher enables it themselves (MOE Singapore).

  • At Harvard, the CS50 course features an AI "duck" that, by design, guides students to the answer with hints rather than giving it directly (CS50; analysis at SIGCSE 2024).

  • Skoltech has taken a similar path, adopting a policy with a presumption of AI permissibility for students and faculty (Skoltech).

My final straw was the following event:

2026. September. St. Petersburg. Polytechnic University. Master's program. 2nd year. Information Systems and TECHNOLOGIES. The professor says something like this: "Due to the fact that AI makes electronic notes no longer able to guarantee that a student has done the necessary work to study the subject, it has been decided to accept work for subject X in the format of handwritten notes... a minimum of 24 pages.".

I don't know how many people will share my indignation, but when I decided to study IT, I assumed that I would at least not be hindered from learning new technologies, and ideally, I would be encouraged to do so. But, of course, everything turned out differently. I've come to terms with the fight against AI-generated texts, but to sit and write WITH A PEN in a NOTEBOOK...

I'm not going to rebel, but I really want to discuss where we are heading with this fight against AI, what alternatives exist, and how I would like to see the education system after the advent of AI.

Why Is Education Necessary?

Education is currently losing badly in the fight against AI texts.

  • At the University of Reading, five academic modules received papers entirely written by AI – 94% went unnoticed, and their grades were higher than those of real students (PLOS ONE).

  • Vanderbilt University calculated that even a 1% false positive rate for a detector on 75,000 papers per year means about 750 papers that could have been mistakenly flagged as AI-written (Vanderbilt).

  • In Russia, "Antiplagiat" found up to 96% "neural network" in independently written works – there are already real stories of female students who were not allowed to defend their theses because of this (Kommersant).

  • OpenAI shut down its AI text detector back in 2023 – it only detected 26% of machine-generated texts and in 9% of cases mistook human text for machine-generated (OpenAI).

But for some reason, it continues to fight, and in my opinion, it won't win. With every improvement in AI text detectors, AI humanizers will appear, and models will get better at bypassing their triggers each time. So:

I propose everyone stop this fight and start devising a cooperation plan.

What do I propose? I suggest, first of all, to zoom out as much as possible and ask the question: why is education needed at all?

I think many will answer: "To gain knowledge" or something similar, but I've started looking at education differently.

The educational system should not impart knowledge, but foster curiosity for knowledge.

As far as I'm concerned, this formulation changes the situation drastically.

What do we have now? You go to kindergarten, school, college, university to be taught something (well, conceptually, that's how it should work, let's ignore the social institution aspect of these establishments). And you are taught, but it's all somehow painful – I and everyone I studied with had roughly the same feelings (except for one person).

Then there's a test, then an exam, then a session, all of it involved cramming, cheating, workarounds, and as a result of this approach, over 20 years of study, not that many hard skills have been acquired.

But if, instead of being pushed from one subject to another, from one topic to another, I had been trying to "sell" this subject to me, explaining: what is it? How is it used in practice? How has it developed historically? I think I would have defended my doctorate by the age of 23.

As I said, the education system should not provide knowledge, but be a tool for acquiring it and encourage understanding of what surrounds us. That is, teachers should become consultants, not lecturers, and students – not listeners, but researchers.

Will this really change anything significantly? I can only speculate, and show the facts I found:

  • George Loewenstein describes curiosity as a feeling of a gap in one's own knowledge (Loewenstein).

  • Susan Engel observed classrooms: episodes of curiosity – questions, fiddling with objects, wanting to look inside – occurred two to five times in two hours in kindergarten, and by the fifth grade – less than once on average (Engel).

  • Benjamin Bloom showed back in 1984: a student with a private tutor on average outperforms a student in a regular class by about two standard deviations – the "two-sigma problem" (Bloom, 1984). However, the result itself has not been reproduced with such strength since – later studies show a more modest effect.

It seems the problem is not with the children, but with the system.

How to Integrate AI into the Learning Process?

What do we have at the moment?

Our starting point A: most people fear AI and ban its use everywhere.

Our point B: a teacher takes the Hippocratic Oath, and each student has a tablet at their desk with which they study the subject by interacting with AI, under the supervision of the educator.

Bold? Bold.

I won't describe all the stages of transition from state A to state B, of course, and without extensive analysis, I can't look that far ahead (and I came here to complain about being forced to write by hand, not to offer you a dissertation to read, although there are many ideas). But what I can do is describe at least what needs to be done as first steps towards point B.

First, let's ask the following question. What's wrong with AI editing my text for me? Seriously, what's wrong if I write a paragraph with typos, missing a couple of commas, and in some phrase I can't find the right word, and it reformulates and corrects errors? In my opinion, it's an ideal scenario, and it will even fact-check to prevent tomatoes from being thrown at me.

I started writing articles on Habr quite recently – this is my second article. And AI played a significant role in my decision to do so, because I realized that I don't have to waste time on layout and error correction, but can focus solely on content. And I would really like many who have something to share not to stop because they are too lazy to format an article. Why should a doctor of science or an entrepreneur waste their time on article layout? Let them churn out these articles with the help of AI, sharing their experience.

But I understand that most often the scenario of working with AI for educational purposes is completely different: it's told not to help, but to do everything for the person. But this problem should have a different solution – the person must be asked what they actually wrote, at least at the level of understanding. If they can explain it, great; if not, go and ask AI to explain what it did.

Thus, the first stage is to remove all restrictions on AI and restructure work, considering that everyone can use it and that we can evaluate work under these conditions.

Please, let the youth keep up with the times

If each student now needs to be interviewed personally, then we will immediately face a shortage of personnel.

  • UNESCO estimates the global teacher shortage at nearly 44 million by 2030 (UNESCO).

  • In Russia, less than 1.2% of teaching positions are vacant – a minimum in five years (regions.ru). At the same time, the Ministry of Education states that a teacher's workload should be 1.4 full-time equivalents, while Savvateev's 2024 survey shows an average of about 1.65, and in the Irkutsk region – up to 1.8 (T–Zh).

The problem is serious – it certainly won't be fixed quickly, so I propose as a second stage to introduce training sessions before exams. That is, an AI tool on the educational institution's side is needed, with which students could rehearse their understanding of the topic before the final exam with the teacher. Thus, we are no longer fighting AI, but integrating it into the learning process. Here are a couple of examples where they are already trying to integrate AI somehow:

  • In Texas, on the STAAR exam, written answers are first checked by an automated scoring system, and then about a quarter are additionally checked by a human (Texas Tribune); according to the technical report of the Texas Education Agency, automated and human scores match exactly or within one point in 97–99% of cases (TEA, Technical Digest 2023–2024, Table 4.7).

  • Stanford has developed Tutor CoPilot – a system that provides real-time suggestions to a live tutor on how to best conduct a lesson (Stanford).

  • NYU Professor Panos Ipeirotis conducted oral exams with a voice AI agent – 36 exams cost $15 (the-decoder).

And then, as a third stage, we scale what has worked, balancing the cost and effectiveness of the proposed solutions – taking into account the current times.

I won't dare to look further. The main thing I wanted to convey is that education is not working properly.

For some reason, I still have to go through interviews, even though I have a bachelor's degree in the relevant field. There is no trust in the education system; we need to think about what we can do about it, considering new technologies.
