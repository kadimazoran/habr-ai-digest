In the first part, the assistant recorded the call and stored it in a graph, in the second, the graph learned not to lie for weeks, in the third, I made a mistake three times with the question of who is speaking. This part is about the twenty-five minutes after the Stop button: nothing happens on the screen, everything happens on the disk. A vulnerability was found there this week that had been living since July. The cloud revision found errors in the local model, carefully described them, and no one listened to it.

One meeting, four files, twenty-five minutes

Friday, a two-hour work call. Recording stopped at 17:33. According to the logs:

17:33  stop; live transcript draft closed
17:36  final transcript: diarization by channels, re-recognition, names
17:38  meeting note in graph, people nodes, minutes, local model analysis,
       seven facts went into memory
17:58  cloud revision ready: 20 minutes, 57 graph edits transferred,
       7 assignments added to minutes

The local model wrote three decisions and a list of assignments into the minutes. The revision, after reading the transcript in full, rejected two out of three decisions: one was a joke about the budget (one token for everyone, next request next year), the second was a task for a neighboring team. The third had the correct decision but the wrong assignee. It removed three assignments: to a person who wasn't at the meeting, with a deadline of the 25th that no one mentioned; without a deadline and to an assignee who is on vacation from next week; and one with a number read backward — the plan was accepted as a difference. Plus a name: the recognition named the host's track with someone else's name based on the address, the revision found the real one based on the same addresses.

All this was in the revision file with quotes and timestamps. Next:

  • in the minutes, the revision could only add: seven restored assignments were added, three false ones remained and went to the Tasks tab;

  • in the meeting note, the joke remained with the 📌 solution tag, the refutation lay below, in the section for the person;

  • seven facts went into memory at 17:38, twenty minutes before the revision declared two of them non-existent. Memory search then calmly returned a single token for all teams as an accepted decision.

The revision worked from the end of July, every day, for every meeting. Its conclusions never reached anywhere: the second pass was set up as another reader, without the right to cancel.

What happens after stop

A five-step pipeline, the first two were detailed in previous parts.

Reassembly. The live draft is assembled from three-second chunks during the meeting. After stopping, the recording is re-diarized by channels, microphone echo is cut by overlap with system sound, track names are guessed from addresses. The old version goes into a hidden folder: the person could edit the transcript manually. Minutes are either reassembled or re-stamped — names are substituted for speaker numbers.

Graph. The local model (35B, MoE, via Ollama) parses the transcript into JSON: people, systems, decisions, assignments. From this — a meeting note, nodes of people and systems, a line in the table of contents, facts in memory. The analysis is also written here: what it was and what to do next.

Revision. If the cloud layer is enabled, a separate process sends the transcript, minutes, analysis, and graph context to a large model via CLI by subscription. The model writes a revision: where the local model made a mistake, what it missed. In edit mode, it corrects a clone of the graph, and the edits are transferred under lock with verification of each file, as was in the second part.

Delivery. The revision is placed next to the transcript, in the archive, and in the documentation; minutes are reread into the Tasks tab.

Night. Dossier, core revisions, morning brief.

Three models: recognition, local, cloud. And the question I haven't asked for a month and a half: who is responsible if step three disagrees with step two?

Why a second pass

Why not make the local model smarter? It makes mistakes for one reason: it writes minutes during the meeting, sees the tail of the transcript every two and a half minutes, and has to produce a document. A joke about the budget looks like a decision because the model hasn't seen the laughter ten seconds later yet. A person mentioned three times looks like a participant. The word "turnover" in a conversation about the budget is read as "personnel turnover," and "development halt due to layoffs" appears in the meeting risks.

The large model reads the transcript once and in its entirety. It sees that a track with a different name answers a question addressed to Igor. It sees that recommendations in the analysis came from another meeting through the graph context. One pass of the large model after the meeting is cheaper than forcing the small one to think like the large one on the fly.

But then the second pass must be able to remove, not just add. That is, to change files that the Tasks tab, graph, and memory have already read. This is what I've been doing all week.

Remove, not add

The revision communicates with the code not with prose, but with strict sections at the end of the file. Prose is left for the human.

## Restored assignments
- [ ] **Name** — what to do — deadline, if mentioned

## Removed assignments
- **Name** — what to do — reason: ...

## Name corrections
- **Label** → **Name** — basis: ...

Restored ones existed before. Removed and Name corrections are new. The model is asked to write the name and task the same way as in the minutes, so that the code can find the line.

The most tedious part is how the code finds the minutes line by the model's retelling. The first version considered any directional overlap a match: *prepare report* overlaps with *prepare budget report*, so it's a match. An external reviewer showed in fifteen minutes that this way, one removed item removes all similar lines: the budget report and the deadline report are removed from tasks together. A consumed assignment will never be seen by the human, an extra duplicate will be seen and removed with one click. The cost of errors is asymmetric, hence the rules:

  • one revision item removes exactly one line;

  • if several match, an exact match takes precedence, and without an exact match, nothing is removed; the log shows: matches two items, we don't guess;

  • overlap is counted from two common significant words: one word *call* does not remove *call the bank*;

  • an item that the human marked as done is never removed;

  • a removed line is not deleted but moved to the Removed by revision section, crossed out, with a reason. It disappears from Tasks, but not from minutes.

The rule of not guessing was already in the project for graph nodes: the name is similar to two existing nodes — the node is not created, candidates go to the human. It turned out to be general: where the choice is based on fuzzy matching, and the error is silent loss, it's better to do nothing and report it.

The second round of review brought a story about the owner. In the settings, he is recorded with two words, the minutes write his full name, and for comparison, the code reduced the owner to the first word only on one side. The keys differed by surname, removal for the owner never worked, and no test caught this: all tests were with a one-word owner. Two external heads found this independently, one rated it Critical. It's fixed with one canon function on both sides. Test data should include the inconvenient case from the config, not the convenient one from the head.

It's simpler with decisions. The cloud marks an erroneous decision in the note in place: 📌 becomes ⛔, the reason is at the end of the line. Everything that reads the note — dossier, search, memory — knows that ⛔ is not a decision.

Memory: catching up with revision

The assistant's memory (a separate service with a vector database and Postgres, shared with my chatbot) doesn't have a fact key, only a meeting key. You can't forget a single decision, you can forget a meeting. Therefore, after transferring edits, the code compares the list of active decisions in the note before and after. If it changed, the meeting is forgotten entirely and sent again without ⛔. If it didn't change, we don't touch anything.

On paper, it's two lines. In code, this turned out to be the most expensive part of the week: four rounds of review, and something was found in each. The break between forgetting and resending left the meeting memory empty forever — a debt file appeared next to the sending mark. The debt belonged to the wrong sender, and reprocessing didn't clear it. The debt was cleared by the number of external keys in the mark — with a dead service, without a single successful request. This was already Critical. The debt of an external meeting cleared an external process in the middle of its own sending. I had to add a sender lock, debt age, and a queue. By the fourth round, the code was correct and ugly.

The audit by zones (more on that below) finished it off: out of seventeen confirmed defects, five were in synchronization with memory, and three out of ten verdicts independently named the same thing — a meeting debt from another graph is cleared without payment. The mechanism that keeps the second memory in sync with the first turned out to be the main place where they diverge.

Here I stopped and realized that the second memory has capabilities that the graph doesn't. The meeting note with decisions is in the graph. Semantic search — the graph is already searched by hybrid. Two unique things: personal facts, which are intentionally not in the meeting graph, and dialogue memory. The rest is a copy, for which I pay with debts, locks, and headaches.

I'm turning off the second memory. Not today, and not by deletion: first, personal facts, several hundred of them, will be moved to the graph as regular markdown files; hints will get their own graph search without the service; dictation and diary will be written to a folder. Then a dump, stopping services, and a week later, deletion. The old memory works until the new one answers the same questions. Two memories without a common key are like two writers in one folder without a lock from the second part: the same data loss, only slower.

Names: whose track is this

Recognition names the track by addresses and makes mistakes, the revision sees this. Previously, it wrote a paragraph about it, and that was it: the headings of replicas, participants in minutes, copies in the archive remained with the wrong name.

Now, based on the Name corrections section, the code re-stamps the replica headings and the participant line in the transcript and minutes. The pre-edit version goes into the same hidden folder. The hash of the machine text is updated so that the next reassembly doesn't take the edit for a manual one. Four restrictions, each from a reviewer's finding:

  1. Assignments are not renamed. *Igor — approve plan* could refer to the real Igor from the speech, not the track with that name. Such assignments are removed by the revision and returned by themselves, using the same sections.

  2. Label exchange in one pass. Sequential replacement of A with B, then B with A turned two participants into one. Critical from an external head.

  3. Track merging is prohibited. Two voices under one name without rollback is worse than a wrong name on one track.

  4. Owner's microphone label is not touched. The channel is hardware fact, the model has nothing to do with it.

And a decision I argued with myself. Re-stamping only happens when the cloud is allowed to edit the graph and the transfer is verified. In read-only mode, the transcript is not renamed, otherwise, it would result in a fractured truth: the transcript has the correct name, the person node has the wrong one, and no one will reconcile them. Both heads called this a risk, I agreed. But a restored assignment with the correct name in this mode would get a tag that the assignee is not a participant. Therefore, the name map from the revision is still built, doesn't touch files, and is given to the assignment bridge as a list of participants.

Repeat and honest status

In three days, nineteen revisions, four without result: a thirty-minute timeout on a long transcript, two non-zero codes after eight and eighteen minutes, one failure to launch — the CLI was updating at that moment. In all four cases, only the entire processing with reassembly could be repeated, and the meeting status showed readiness.

Now the revision has four outcomes: delivered, response not like revision, process not launched or crashed, timeout. One class is repeated — a crashed process, once, after ten minutes, and not during a live meeting. Repeating a timeout is pointless: the same context will hit the same ceiling. A response with code zero, but not like a revision, is the model's response; a second attempt will yield the same.

The revision stage is in the meeting status as a separate field: in progress, repeating, ready, failed. It doesn't shift the meeting readiness. The field survives the readiness record and expires after three hours if the worker was killed during a pause. Another vulnerability was found by a reviewer: the Repeat processing button in the application appears after thirty minutes of silence, and a legitimate revision takes up to thirty minutes plus a lock. The button appeared just when the first worker was still alive, and the second launched a paid analysis again. Now, before the second attempt, it checks if a revision newer than the transcript has appeared.

Audit by zones for seventy-nine cents

External heads were mentioned in the second part: each change before merging is read by DeepSeek and GLM; as long as the round brings Critical — we send the next one. This week, another mode was added: audit by zones.

Five zones of the post-meeting pipeline: reassembly and names, graph, cloud revision, minutes bridge, memory and statuses. Per zone — a clean clone of the repository, read-only tools, a list of files in reading order, and a budget: twelve minutes and no more than forty moves, then a verdict on what was read. Without a budget, a broad zone cannot be read: last time, one head made three empty attempts, the second responded on the third. Both heads receive the same five zones, ten processes start together.

10 zones (5 DeepSeek 4.1 flash + 5 GLM 5.3 flash), all in parallel
wall           10 min 13 s (longest zone — 10 min)
per zone       6–10 min, 17–39 tool moves
tokens per zone 120–155 K input, 1–2.8 M cached, 33–61 K output
price          $0.38 + $0.40 = $0.79 for ten verdicts

DeepSeek — 12 Important and 16 Minor, GLM — 1 Critical, 9 Important and 23 Minor. Critical — about the echo filter that cuts the owner's speech during simultaneous conversation: a known compromise, marked in the third part, rejected. Of the twenty-one Important findings in the code, all were confirmed, after merging duplicates — seventeen defects. Three out of ten verdicts converged on the same defect, and this is the best signal such an audit can provide.

Heads don't vote. I checked each finding in the code myself, and it took longer than the entire audit. Eight cents per zone is the price of a verdict, not the truth.

One failure was in the wrapper itself — a script outside the repository that starts clones and collects verdicts. Its gate for an exhausted limit looked for substrings like `402` and `rate_limit_error` in the response. It declared two out of ten verdicts as a limit stop because the audited code contains the same strings, and the verdict quoted them. Restored from the raw response, the gate now only looks at short answers. Any substring filter will eventually encounter text that talks about that filter.

What has changed this week

  • Revision can remove: false assignments go from tasks to the Removed by revision section, erroneous decisions are marked in place, meeting memory is resent without them.

  • Track names are re-stamped according to a strict section, only with verified graph edits and without guessing.

  • Revision is repeated after a launch failure and shows its stage in the status.

  • Analysis stopped pulling recommendations from adjacent meetings: graph context is now only for names and terms.

  • The second memory has been recognized as a duplicate and is being phased out.

  • Audit by zones: ten minutes, less than a dollar, seventeen defects.
