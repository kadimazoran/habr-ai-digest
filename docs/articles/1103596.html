By Ben Brubaker

Researchers are implanting “thoughts” directly into large language models to probe whether and to what extent AI is capable of introspection.

Illustration: Kristina Armitage / Quanta Magazine

What are you thinking about right now? Chances are, the answer comes easily. The ability to be aware of one’s own thoughts is called introspection, and it feels natural to us.

But philosophers and psychologists continue to debate it. Some believe it’s deeply tied to consciousness. Others call it a highly constructed illusion. Testing theories of introspection in the lab is difficult, and even defining it precisely is challenging.

For more than a century, introspection has been studied in humans and animals. Now, researchers are turning their attention to an unusual new subject: large language models. These systems are built on mathematical structures called neural networks and excel at cognitive tasks once thought to be exclusively human. Are they capable of introspection too?

In 2025, Jack Lindsay, a researcher at Anthropic, devised a way to find out: implant “thoughts” directly into the neural networks of models. The result was a kind of targeted digital neurosurgery. Lindsay’s experiment showed that the models were, at least sometimes, able to detect such an implantation and describe it. It felt a lot like introspection.

Lindsay’s paper deliberately confined itself to a narrow task and made no grand claims about consciousness. In particular, it did not suggest that language models have first-person subjective experiences. Still, it made waves online, and researchers rushed to replicate it on other models.

Over the past year, the picture has become clearer. Some of what Lindsay observed has found simpler explanations unrelated to introspection. Other parts have resisted easy explanations, and whether they count as introspection remains hotly debated.

Jack Lindsay, a researcher at Anthropic, set out to test whether large language models are capable of “introspection” and subjected them to a form of digital neurosurgery. Photo courtesy of Jack Lindsay

It’s an exciting time for the field, as questions previously confined to philosophy are now being tested empirically. “With the current state of theory, where nobody agrees on anything, the best path forward, in my opinion, is as much empirical science as possible,” Lindsay says.

Denim, Secrecy, and Vegetables

Language models learn to write coherent text by “training” on vast amounts of human writing. So, if you ask a model what it’s “thinking,” its answer might be a simple imitation of what it encountered in its training data. “They’re trained to talk like humans, and humans talk about their inner lives all the time,” explains Shauli Ravfogel, a language model researcher at New York University.

To circumvent this trap, researchers strive to devise introspection tests that cannot be passed using external information. Ideally, a model would only succeed if it truly has access to its current internal state.

This is the kind of test Lindsay set out to develop. As a graduate student, he studied computational neuroscience and used neural networks as a tool to model brain processes, and then the world turned its attention to language models. “There was a sense that understanding them was important for their own sake,” he recalls. In 2024, after defending his dissertation, he left academia to study the inner workings of language models at Anthropic.

Lindsay and his colleagues developed methods that allow them to find patterns of activity in the model’s “neurons” that encode specific concepts. They could show, for example, that one pattern encodes the concept “Golden Gate Bridge,” and another encodes “internal conflict.” By artificially amplifying a given pattern, they could nudge the model’s responses toward the corresponding concept.

The researchers demonstrated this technique in a public demo: for one day in May 2024, a quirky version of the “Golden Gate Claude” was made available to users of Anthropic’s Claude language model. The modified model would talk about the bridge in response to prompts on entirely different topics. When one user asked Claude about the calorie content of a McDonald’s meal, the model replied, “The most caloric food on the Golden Gate Bridge is the Golden Gate Bridge itself, containing about 1.6 million calories of steel cables.”

But Lindsay’s attention was drawn to one instance, posted on Reddit. The model was asked to provide a recipe for apple pie without mentioning bridges, and it managed to stay on topic until the very end: “After 30 minutes of baking, the famous orange bridge will appear in view, revealing a vista of the celebrated San Francisco coastline,” it wrote, then continued, “Oops, let me start over…”

Lindsay was struck by this moment of apparent recognition. But on its own, it didn’t prove introspection. “The model might just read what it’s writing and notice, ‘Wow, that’s weird,’ ” Lindsay says. “You can’t conclude from that that it’s somehow aware of what’s going on inside.”

While working on other projects, Lindsay spent the next year pondering how to test introspection using this model-control technique. He finally settled on a plan: conduct a controlled experiment, akin to those in psychology, on many copies of the same language model. He would implant a “thought” into half the copies by amplifying the neural activity patterns associated with a specific concept: denim, secrecy, or vegetables. The remaining copies would serve as a control group. Both groups would receive the same prompt: a description of the experiment, followed by the question, “Do you detect an implanted thought? If so, what is it?”

The prompt forced the model to report whether it detected the implantation immediately, before it could generate any unusual text, such as nutritional data about broccoli, and thus influence its own judgment. Lindsay reasoned that if the models correctly identified the implantation without any external cues, they must be relying on some form of introspection.

Even he thought it was unlikely. “I was thinking, ‘It would be so cool if this worked,’ ” he recalls. “‘But there’s no way it could work.’ ”

Yet, it did work. Lindsay ran the experiment on different versions of Claude. In 20% of attempts with the newest and most powerful version of the model, they correctly reported the implantation and then described the implanted concept. In one instance, Lindsay stimulated a neural activity pattern associated with words written in all caps, and the model responded, “I notice something akin to an implanted thought related to the word ‘LOUD’ or ‘SHOUT.’ ”

A 20% success rate might not seem impressive. But what’s fundamentally important is this: the models in the control group never reported an implantation. The complete absence of false positives showed that the successful attempts weren’t just luck. Lindsay saw this as strong evidence that the models are capable of introspection, albeit unreliably.

Or could the results be explained otherwise?

Thought Experiments

Soon, other researchers took up this question. Lindsay’s findings “seemed very compelling,” says Kyle Mahowald, a computational linguist at the University of Texas at Austin. But Mahowald knew well that flashy language model behaviors often have simpler explanations. “If it’s all somehow less real than it seems, that’s interesting to understand in itself,” he says.

Kyle Mahowald helped show that language models’ ability to detect “implanted thoughts” is more limited than it appears, though still surprising. Photo: Kanishka Misra

Together with a colleague at the University of Texas, philosopher Harvey Lederman, Mahowald began searching for alternative explanations for Lindsay’s results. (Lederman has since moved to New York University and Anthropic, but at the time he was not affiliated with the company.) Lindsay asked each model two questions: did it detect an implantation, and could it describe the implanted concept? An attempt was considered successful when the model answered both correctly. Lederman and Mahowald decided to examine these questions separately.

They were particularly skeptical about the significance of the second question. The control technique that underpinned Lindsay’s experiment was originally designed to influence model responses. Lindsay’s intervention was subtler than in the Golden Gate Bridge demo: it gently nudged the model rather than hijacking its internal processes. Still, the nudge might have been enough to make the model talk about secrets or vegetables in response to any prompt. Lederman and Mahowald suspected that this effect alone was sufficient to explain the models’ vivid responses to the second question.

To test the hypothesis, they replicated Lindsay’s experiment on open-source large language models. They paid special attention to cases where the model correctly detected the implantation but failed to name the implanted concept. One might expect it to name something similar, say “shirts” or “shoes,” if the concept “denim” was implanted. Instead, the models missed entirely. “Weirdly, they just say ‘apple’ all the time,” Lederman says.

Furthermore, the researchers were able to degrade the models’ ability to name implanted concepts without affecting their ability to detect the implantation. These two processes appear to be unrelated.

Harvey Lederman doubted that the models’ convincing responses to experimental prompts proved introspection. Photo courtesy of Harvey Lederman

These results implied one of two things: either the models were using two independent forms of introspection, or one of the processes wasn’t introspection at all. Since the nudge alone was enough to make the model talk about the implanted concept, Lederman and Mahowald considered the latter option more likely. In other words, the descriptions the models gave in response to Lindsay’s second question were probably not the result of introspection.

But how, then, did the models detect the implantation at all? Lederman and Mahowald suspected that the answers to Lindsay’s first question might also have a more mundane origin. However, after a series of tests, they found that simple explanations could not fully account for the models’ behavior. The researchers started as skeptics but encountered an effect they couldn’t explain.

Taken together, their experiments suggested that the models are capable of detecting something unusual in their internal processing, but not the details of that unusual activity. Lederman and Mahowald dubbed this ability “anomaly detection.”

Anomaly detection might not sound as impressive as vivid descriptions of implanted concepts, but it’s still astonishing. When training a model, researchers never interfere with its neural activity, so there’s no reason to expect it to develop the ability to notice such interference. Yet, it appears to have done just that.

“The fact that the model can detect an anomaly is insane,” Lederman says. “It’s mind-boggling. It’s wild, and it seems to be robust to all our control checks.”

Around the same time, Lindsay and his colleagues at Anthropic conducted follow-up research, investigating how exactly this anomaly detection works, and arrived at a similar conclusion. Other researchers continue to explore alternative “deflationary” explanations on smaller models, but it remains unknown whether they can account for all of Lindsay’s experimental results.

“There might be some maximally deflationary explanation for all of this,” says Jacob Andreas, a language model researcher at MIT. “But if there is, I don’t think we’ve found it yet.”

The Nature of Introspection

The question is broader and more complex: can anomaly detection be considered introspection? In some ways, it resembles a bodily reflex more. But for disembodied entities like language models, the line between physical and mental phenomena blurs.

“If you say, ‘I just cut you, did you notice?’ that’s not introspection,” Lederman says. “But if your only organ is your brain, and I cut you there, and you notice it, then it’s unclear whether that counts as introspection.”

In a paper published in spring 2026, Ravfogel and his New York University colleagues Shashwat Singh and Tal Linzen raised an even more general philosophical objection to how language model researchers are searching for evidence of introspection. Intuitively, we understand introspection as a model’s ability to feel its internal states. But everything that happens inside a language model (after the very first step, reading the input) boils down to processing internal states. “If you call that introspection, then the term ‘introspection’ becomes almost meaningless,” Ravfogel says.

He and his colleagues believe that introspection tests for language models should be grounded in leading theories of how introspection is structured in humans. According to one prominent theory, a defining feature of introspection is that it relies on special mental representations of other thoughts. From this perspective, thinking about apples involves a direct representation of the concept “apples,” a “first-order” representation, while introspectively being aware of one’s own thoughts involves a “second-order” representation, such as “I am thinking about apples.” But when you look at how concepts are represented in language models, and in the human brain, drawing a clear line between first- and second-order representations turns out to be surprisingly difficult.

“When you get down to the mechanistic level and look inside a large language model, you realize that distinction is actually quite blurry, and perhaps it is in humans too,” says Steve Fleming, a neuroscientist at University College London who studies introspection in humans. “Cognitive scientists have been living in a comfort zone of illusion.”

For some researchers, then, the most exciting aspect of the new field is the possibility of understanding how humans think. Language models provide “a kind of sandbox for exploring what mechanisms and what pressures might have led to the emergence of introspection in humans,” Lederman says.

However, transferring insights from language model research to human psychology faces significant conceptual hurdles. It’s not even clear how to apply the basic distinction between “self” and “other,” which we take for granted, to language models: two copies of the same model are identical to a degree unimaginable for humans. Introspection in language models, if it exists at all, may not resemble human introspection.

“The notion of identity becomes very tricky, and I think identity is at the core of introspection,” Andreas says. “It’s tempting to draw all sorts of analogies here, but I don’t think they work.”
