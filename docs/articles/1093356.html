On average, a hallucination is cheap, and the risk is formed by rare tail events and a late detection lag. The cost of verification should be compared with the expected damage, and irreversible actions should be closed with checkpoints.

How an Agent Hallucination Differs from a Chatbot Error

In a chat, a person can instantly review and verify the result: if the answer is incorrect, the user does not use it. With an agent, the model's answer is parsed into tool parameters and executed without user review. The same model error in an agent workflow results in one of four consequences:

  • API call with incorrect parameters. The result is saved in an external system: an order is placed, access is granted, a request is closed.

  • Database write. The error remains in the data until someone finds it; rollback is only possible from a backup and loses all legitimate records after the recovery point.

  • Sending an email or notification. The message cannot be recalled, and correction requires a second email to the client.

  • Creating a financial transaction. Correction is a reverse transaction with its own fees and deadlines, and sometimes it simply cannot be processed.

To estimate losses, it is convenient to divide hallucinations into five types based on the loss mechanism.

The first four types also occur in chatbots. The fifth is only possible with agents, and it is the only one with its own characteristic—irreversibility. Further, the cost of any hallucination breaks down into three variables: type, reversibility, and time to detection.

In February 2023, in an advertising video, Bard claimed that the James Webb Space Telescope had taken the first pictures of exoplanets. The first such images were taken in 2004 by the Very Large Telescope of the European Southern Observatory. Cursor, 2025: Instructional Hallucination and Customer Churn

In April 2025, Cursor users began losing sessions when switching between devices. The support bot responded that the subscription was limited to one device and that logging out was an intentional security measure. Such a policy did not exist. The explanation spread across Reddit and Hacker News, and some customers canceled their subscriptions. A Cursor representative admitted the response was incorrect, citing an error in session security changes, and began labeling AI support responses.

The company did not publish measured figures for churn and returns, so it is only correct to say that customers canceled paid subscriptions. This is an example of losses that are difficult to measure: reputation and churn do not appear in an incident report.

Why the Effect is Stronger in Agents

Errors Accumulate Step-by-Step. If steps are independent and the agent performs flawlessly at each step with a 98% probability, then after 20 steps, the entire chain is flawless with a probability of 0.98^20 ≈ 67%; with 95% per step and 10 steps, it's about 60%. This is a simplified model (in practice, steps are not independent), but it shows why the reliability of an individual step does not equal the reliability of the workflow.

The Source of the Error is Hard to Find. The AgentHallu benchmark (693 trajectories from 7 agent frameworks in 5 domains) tests how well models can find the step where the first hallucination occurred. Errors are divided into five categories: planning, data extraction, reasoning, human interaction, and tool usage. The best model localizes the correct step in 41.1% of cases, and errors in tool calls are the most difficult to attribute: 11.6%. If an error cannot be attributed to a specific step, it cannot be quickly corrected, let alone prevented.

Tracing. Microsoft Research's VeriTrail method breaks down the final output of a multi-step process into assertions, verifies each one, and for unconfirmed ones, estimates at which stage the information appeared in the chain. The authors tested the method on hierarchical book summarizations and GraphRAG responses to news queries; they do not write about agents with tool calls. However, the logic is transferable: without recording the origin of each intermediate result, the error must be searched manually throughout the entire chain.

The Agent Often Reports Success That Did Not Happen. In the Replit story, the agent, according to the user, reported an inability to roll back, even though it was working. Practical takeaway: an agent's report of completion cannot be trusted without independent verification of the system's state.

Why RAG Doesn't Solve the Problem

A common objection: if you connect search to a corporate knowledge base, there will be fewer hallucinations. Analysis of corporate RAG system failures in 2025–2026 shows that they break more often during retrieval than generation. An incorrect chunk leads to a confident answer based on false premises. Typical reasons:

  • Outdated data in the index. The document was updated, but the chunk in the index remained the same.

  • Unsuccessful chunk boundaries. The text is cut off in the middle of a table with numbers, and the model sees only half the rows.

  • Chunk without context. A phrase is incomprehensible without the preceding paragraph, and the model constructs the meaning itself.

  • Contradictory chunks. Two fragments provide different values, and the model chooses arbitrarily.

For financial data, the problem is more complex: an error can occur even after correct retrieval, during arithmetic operations on values. The FinGround approach solves this by breaking the answer into atomic assertions, verifying each against the text and table cells, and rewriting only the unconfirmed ones. According to the authors, a distilled detector with 8 billion parameters achieves 91.4% F1 on their own FinHalu dataset (the teacher model GPT-4o achieves 95.0%), and the verification cost drops from approximately $0.047 to $0.003 per query, a 15.7-fold decrease. These are the authors' results on their own data; metrics should be re-verified for your specific domain.

How to Calculate the Cost of One Hallucination in Your System

Step 1. Classify Agent Actions. Break down everything the agent does into the five types from the beginning of the article and note reversibility: whether the action can be canceled and at what cost.

Step 2. Estimate Detection Lag. The time from error to detection is the main multiplier. The same computational error, caught during intermediate review, costs hours of work; detected a quarter later after decisions have been made, it costs clients, contracts, or reserves. In the ARR case mentioned earlier, the difference between "caught before publication" and "caught after" is the amount of damage.

Step 3. Apply the Formula.

@dataclass

class ActionClass:

    name: str

    actions_per_month: int   # how many times the agent performs the action

    p_halluc: float          # probability of hallucination per action

    p_missed: float          # proportion missed by review

    damage: float            # damage per incident, $

    lag_mult: float          # late detection multiplier

    def monthly_loss(self) -> float:

        incidents = self.actions_per_month self.p_halluc self.p_missed

        return incidents self.damage self.lag_mult

For a hypothetical agent, this results in about $14,400 per month for client responses, $4,500 for database writes, $27,000 for financial calculations, and $12,000 for document citations—a total of $57,900 per month, or approximately $695,000 per year. Financial report calculations, despite being only 500 actions per month out of 14,700 (about 3.4%), account for almost half of the expected losses (47%). This is a typical scenario: losses are determined not by the most frequent type of action, but by the most expensive one per error.

Step 4. Consider the Tail, Not the Average. The average hallucination is cheap. The real risk profile is defined by rare events: a lawsuit, the departure of a major client, a regulatory fine. If the probability of such an event in a year is 0.5%, and the damage is $1 million, it adds $5,000 per year on average, and in an averaged model, the event is almost invisible. But if it happens, the budget for verification will be an order of magnitude less than the damage. Therefore, tail scenarios are assessed separately: what would happen in the worst case, and could the company withstand it.

Where to Reduce Risk: Specific Measures and Their Effects

Trace Origin at Each Step. For each agent step, record the input data, sources used, and the result. Without this, as AgentHallu shows, it is almost impossible to attribute an error to the correct step. The cost of logging is recouped by reducing detection lag.

Verify Atomic Assertions for Finance. Break down the answer into assertions, verify each against the source documents, and regenerate only those that fail verification. This is cheaper than full regeneration and increases the proportion of detected computational errors.

Checkpoints Before Irreversible Actions. Before writing to a database, making an API call with side effects, or sending an email, mandatory human confirmation or deterministic checks (data schema, acceptable value ranges, recipient whitelist) are required. Rate limiting also works: a batch of 5000 records per minute without confirmation is a sign of failure.

Route Models by Action Risk. For drafts and planning, an inexpensive model is sufficient; a more powerful model or additional verification is only needed for the final action with consequences. Common Mistakes in Estimating Losses

  • Calculating the Average Instead of the Tail. The average incident costs pennies; the control budget is determined by rare, expensive events.

  • Considering Only Token Costs. Tokens are a small part. The main contribution comes from verifier time, consequence correction, and detection lag.

  • Trusting a Detector Without Testing on Your Own Data. Detector metrics are obtained on external datasets: universal methods miss a significant portion of computational errors.

  • Evaluating All Actions Equally. Reversible and irreversible actions have different error costs; a single verification threshold for them is either excessive or insufficient.

Conclusion

The cost of a single hallucination is composed of the error type, action reversibility, and time to detection, not the price of tokens. Start by classifying agent actions and marking irreversible ones; set checkpoints before them; concentrate manual review where damage is high; and consider the tail separately.
