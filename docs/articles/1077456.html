Given: three very different development teams, each with its own technology stack. From the very second day, a ton of information started pouring in: lots of meetings, many new faces, technology specifics, legacy code (of course). I realized I couldn't keep up with everything, that I was missing important details and nuances before I could document them as meeting minutes or tasks.

First, I started simply recording the meetings I attended on my computer. Ha! But who would listen to them again, and more importantly, when? There wouldn't be enough time to re-listen to everything. Reading would be much faster. And if this text could be processed by an LLM, that would be amazing! It would read everything for me and extract only the most interesting points, and I wouldn't have to read anything! That's how the idea was born to create something that would greatly simplify my life, and also the lives of my colleagues.

To begin, I needed to test the hypothesis – create the first working MVP and ensure it was a viable concept. I got the most ordinary OBS Studio, recorded a couple of my conferences, and started searching the internet for ways to extract words from recordings (transcribe). The most viable option at the time was Yandex SpeechKit. I used it, costing about 30 rubles per hour. No problem – register, deposit money into the account – process the audio recording, and get a fairly accurate meeting transcription!

I deployed ollama locally, chose YandexGPT-5-Lite (it had just been released then, early 2025) – a good model that my old GTX 1080 could handle. I asked it to analyze the text and summarize the meeting, waited 30 seconds, got the summarized results, and voilà! Done! Amazing! My home graphics card with a fairly simple model processed all the words of the conference and produced a result. I reread everything immediately – the main points were highlighted and recorded. It even figured out names in some places (by understanding the context of assignments like: "Yura, will you do this task by the weekend?", "Yes, no problem.")! I tested this complex several more times to ensure these were consistently reproducible results and not AI hallucinations, and shared them in emails with colleagues. Over time, colleagues started asking how I was doing it and wanted the same for themselves. Excitement, it's time to scale! After getting the go-ahead from Nastya, I got to work.

Time to Automate

We have: bank servers where calls can be held, a tested and debugged hypothesis that if a conference is recorded and then the text is extracted, that text can be processed. So, we need to:

1) Learn how to connect to conference rooms, authorize, and record the conference. It would be desirable to also record who was speaking.

2) Transcribe the resulting recordings (preferably with the highest possible quality), extracting the words. It would be desirable to also identify the speakers.

3) Process through an LLM to get results.

4) Find time for this. Don't forget that the main job is not to build robot secretaries :)

The most important part is getting the recording, and we'll figure out how to process it further along the way.

The main requirement: complete offline operation. Nothing should require internet access; everything must work within the bank's closed network.

Connection and Recording

This turned out to be the most difficult part. I thought: most AI software is implemented in Python, so it makes sense to use Python for everything. Let's research – how and what connects now, take dumps, describe the protocol (generally a very popular conference room technology based on WebRTC). We take Python, ask ChatGPT to draft a script for connecting to a conference room. We get it, run it... errors... We fix some ourselves, and ask GPT to further refine the script for others. We managed to authorize, see users, and even send messages in the chat. But connecting to the audio stream via WebRTC failed. More errors. And again, and again, and again. Ah, this love-hate relationship with ChatGPT. Browsers connect somehow, right? And there are a huge number of WebRTC clients, how can it not work? The main thing is not to give up, keep going!

It took a whole month of trial and error with various attempts and approaches. I tried everything: port forwarding, studying WebRTC, and even tried cleaning the SDP of unused addresses before sending – nothing helped! I tried all the open-source Python packages; the most viable one was aiortc – all links and the entire internet pointed to it as the best package. But something wasn't clicking – the RTC connection couldn't be established, the ICE protocol couldn't establish a connection with the server.

Realizing I was going in circles and wasting time, despite the progress made, I decided to rewrite everything in a proper programming language – in Go. To avoid wasting more time, I fed the almost working Python developments into ChatGPT, asked it to rewrite them in Go, got 5-6 files, assembled them, compiled, tweaked them for myself (a couple of hours), ran them... it worked! To say I was shocked would be an understatement. I struggled with Python for a month (!) to get aiortc to record a conference, and here a Go version worked in just 4 hours, literally from the first try! On one hand, I regret the wasted time, but on the other hand – experience. The main thing is: we now have a program that can connect to a selected conference and record it. Thus, the first part was created: the recorder.

Transcription

Now I need to extract words from the audio recording – transcribe it. Having gained experience working with LLMs, I understood how to set tasks for them. After searching the internet and studying best practices, I settled on OpenAI's Whisper (I also encountered more and more articles about it on Habr). Again, why write everything myself when I can assemble it faster with GPT? We describe the situation, generate a Dockerfile with it, deploy, fix errors, deploy again. After 4-5 iterations, we have a working build. After several tests, we realize that this thing generally works, but with a nuance: it only works on my CPU, and the processing speed at 100% CPU load is about 1:1 to the conference duration. If a conference lasted an hour, you wait an hour watching the computer die... This won't do! We describe the situation to ChatGPT again, like this and that, I need a Whisper version with GPU support. The internet has everything, and ChatGPT knows about it all: we deploy the modified Whisper: https://github.com/ggml-org/whisper.cpp. The most stable model turned out to be ggml-large-v3-turbo: if you're transcribing something too, don't skimp; it uses a bit more memory (1.5GB) but recognizes much better than others. We pass the GPU to the Docker container, build, and voilà! The container confirmed that GPU support is now possible. We upload the hour-long conference file, wait... on a GeForce GTX 1080 (2016 flagship), the ratio is 4:1. That is, an hour-long conference is processed in 15 minutes! That's already good. We save it as a transcription service. Let's move on.

Speaker Diarization

Now I need a script that will take the spoken words, match them with the participants who were speaking at that moment, and send everything to a local LLM deployed on a home graphics card. From the recorder, I have a speaker log accurate to the second. We write a matching script, run it... we get some nonsense. Why was I speaking, but it's attributed to my colleague? It turned out that second-level accuracy is not enough: in the same second, 2-3 people might be speaking or making sounds simultaneously. And a whole phrase or question can be spoken in half a second. This means we need to record the speaker log with millisecond accuracy.

I added millisecond accuracy – still problems. I analyze the log, compare it with the audio recording, and see the problem: the conference server reports speakers with a random delay, while the audio and words are already coming through. This means we have spoken words, but at that moment, according to the log, "no one was speaking." Well, who said it would be easy? We create heuristics, try to guess who was speaking, including by continuing speech until the next paragraph. The solution is not ideal; it includes random interruptions, background noise, and conversations, but it's the maximum quality under the current conditions.

LLM Agent

Okay, finally, we have obtained the long-awaited, coveted text of the entire conference, and with speaker names matched from the log! What to process it with? For the pilot, we'll use the beloved YandexGPT-5-Lite that I've come to cherish (thank you, Yandex!). We write a prompt – it takes the transcribed text, processes it through the LLM, and gets the results.

Overall, this turned out to be the easiest part – all the APIs are well-known: set up llama/ollama, interact via REST, get a simple, understandable response. There's practically no chance of making a mistake.

Order Taking

It would seem all the programs are written. But we still need some kind of bot to take orders for recording meetings. For the pilot phase, a Telegram bot was chosen: this time I didn't deviate and used my familiar PHP, whipping up the bot in an evening. It accepted orders for recording from users on a whitelist.

Runner - Orchestrator

Almost all programs are written. But something is missing. I thought a lot about implementation options for the entire complex: from receiving a recording request to sending results. I could have gone the simple route: one program passes control to another upon completion. But I understood that something could go wrong at any moment, and I needed the ability to kick a stuck/broken script at any time. Therefore, a mechanism with a centralized runner-orchestrator was chosen, which would monitor all programs, launch them one after another with the necessary launch parameters, and ensure everything worked as it should.

I decided to write this in Go as well; after all, it's a very responsible part. There were no problems here; for simplicity, I used a regular Postgres database with a table and job queues. I placed all the equipment entirely on my home computer, assembled a pilot group of 20 people, and started the pilot.

Who else is for DimaTorzok?

The pilot project went quite well: I recorded 3-5 meetings a day, reviewed the results, and analyzed them, monitoring operational stability. I increasingly started noticing mentions of some DimaTorzok. At first, I thought someone was joking. It turned out to be a bug; they even made a separate website for it: https://dimatorzok.com/ru/. The essence is this: Whisper was trained on Russian speech using YouTube videos, and to match who said what, they used subtitles from the videos. And it so happened that a certain portion of these materials was prepared by a person with the nickname DimaTorzok. And in scenes where the video was ending and there was a certain silence (not complete mute, but the sound of silence when someone is quiet with the microphone on) – a message appeared: "Subtitles prepared by DimaTorzok." And so, this noise, when everyone is silent, but some background sound is picked up by Whisper as "DimaTorzok." This is a clear bug, and fortunately, it's easy to bypass. After collecting several dozen conferences, it turned out that the most frequent buggy phrases were: "Subtitles made by DimaTorzok," "Tasay i faydi stenderka autbuksdela" (I don't know where this came from, don't ask), "To be continued" (yes, who would have thought that such things are written at the end of videos).

In general, we added such phrases to the exclusions, and we move on.

LLM Instability

The results obtained generally met my expectations: my LLM generated the main points, but missed some of them. Sometimes it confused names (due to the initially messy input). But the worst thing was that it periodically output content in .md format! No matter how I asked it, half the time it produced the wrong format. Over time, I found a pattern – the longer the conference (1.5 hours), the higher the chance the robot would send something wrong. I started digging, and the whole reason was the small context window: 32k tokens is about 15,000 words spoken on average in 45-60 minutes of a typical conference (unless there was someone speaking very fast). Due to the excessively large context, the LLM forgot what we asked it to do at the beginning (output the conference in HTML with simple markup) and started outputting what it deemed necessary.

To solve this problem, a workaround was written: if the conference turned out to be too long (over 12,000 words), it was divided into 2 parts (or more parts with overlap). Then we ask the AI to divide the selected segment into topics with markup: from which line which topic was discussed, with overlaps. And then these chopped-up parts were fed to the LLM, one after another. Each part is about 5000 words. With this, it's possible to work and get quite accurate results.

The Best Interface is No Interface

Let me remind you – during the pilot, colleagues ordered transcriptions via a Telegram bot. This was cool, of course (and Telegram wasn't blocked back then), but we needed to do something else within the network. There were several options:

1) Create an internal website where any employee could order a conference recording.

2) Create a bot in an internal messenger.

3) Or maybe make it even simpler? Maybe forget about interfaces altogether?

I'm a proponent of simplification, not complication. To figure out how to make the user journey simpler, you need to go through it yourself. It should start with the question: how does a conference begin? With its planning. Meetings are scheduled through Outlook calendars, where free slots are checked, and the time and place of the meeting are selected. This means the simplest way to get into a conference is to have the robot added to it immediately! So, we need to become a regular Outlook user so the robot can be invited to the conference. No problem! We submit a request to SD, describe the situation, they register a technical account for us, and even assign it an email address. We rewrite the Telegram bot: instead of working with Telegram, it will now work with Outlook email via the EWS protocol. Again, there were no problems; my PHP skills are honed – the bot checks for unread meeting invitation emails every 15 seconds (mimicking Outlook's logic, so we don't create unnecessary load; we don't want to crash the corporate mail, right?). After the meeting, the results are sent to the organizer/all participants (depending on settings).

Further Development

I completed the first working full-cycle MVP in July 2025, it was recognized as a successful pilot in August, and by September, it was deployed within the bank's network. I've worked in many banks, but I've never seen such speed, ease, and colleague interest in implementing interesting features before.

Since implementation, we've added meeting analytics: was the meeting agenda voiced, were there agreements, how many invited attendees actually participated in the meeting (so as not to invite unnecessary people in the future, distracting them from work). This is more informational analytics for measuring personnel development.

Over time, we noticed that some meetings sometimes run over and might last 1:05, 1:15, or even 1:30 hours instead of the planned hour. The robot would stay for a maximum of 5 extra minutes and disconnect. And some meetings ended much earlier than planned. We realized it's pointless to hardcode the exact number of minutes the bot should stay. Instead, it stays in the conference as long as the main group of participants is still talking: if 50-70% of users remain the same, it means the conference is continuing, and thus the bot needs to keep recording (but this extended recording can be stopped prematurely via chat).

We also improved email notifications: we started adding a survey about the quality of the meeting results analysis and included a link to an internal resource where the full meeting transcription can be read. Recently: we generated video snippets with kling.ai, and when the meeting time is running out, the bot turns on the camera and starts hinting that the meeting is ending soon/has gone over the planned time and it's time to get back to work. This helps to stay within the time limit and not be late.
