For the past few days, I haven't been writing on Reddit myself. Claude Code has been doing it for me.

It would open the browser, find fresh threads, read posts and comments, decide if I had anything to say, write replies, return to old threads if someone responded, monitor reactions, and sometimes talk about my app. If a discussion was already closed by other people, it would skip it. If a dialogue ended logically, over time it learned not to try to have the last word.

I usually ran all this when I didn't need the computer. For example, I'd go for a walk with the kids, disable screen auto-lock, and leave Claude Code running. Or I'd start it before bed. And while I slept, the cursor would move across the laptop screen, Reddit would open, tabs would switch, threads would scroll, and comments would appear.

Over a few days, the log accumulated over a hundred actions. The account's total karma exceeded 1000. One comment received 565 points.

And it was precisely on this comment that Claude was discovered. And not by one person.

This is probably the best description of the entire experiment: the most successful comment turned out to be the most obviously AI-generated one.

Why did I even go to Reddit?

I have a language learning app called VibeLing. I've been trying various ways to promote it for quite some time: articles, SEO, Twitter, product directories, and so on. At some point, I returned to Reddit.

Reddit constantly pops up in indie developer stories as a channel from which you can get users almost for free. But when you start looking into it, it quickly becomes clear that the mechanics there are a bit more complex than just finding a suitable subreddit and dropping a link to your product.

In most language communities, direct self-promotion is either completely forbidden or heavily restricted. And often, these are the most active subs where your target audience hangs out.

However, dozens of questions appear there every day that I can, in principle, answer. How best to memorize words, why a person understands a language by ear but can't speak it, how to structure training, why Duolingo accepts one answer and not another, how to learn vocabulary from movies, and so on.

And I had a fairly simple hypothesis.

Don't try to constantly advertise the app. Just be a useful member of the community. A person reads a normal answer, goes to your profile, sees who you are and what you do, and then, perhaps, goes to the app. If, in a specific discussion, VibeLing is indeed relevant to the context and the sub's rules allow it, you can mention it directly, honestly stating that it's my app.

There's only one problem: this requires constantly being on Reddit.

At first, I did everything myself

For a while, I actually tried to do it manually. I'd go to several subreddits, sort threads by recency, open interesting ones, read the answers, and see if I could add anything useful.

Writing a comment itself isn't difficult. But Reddit very quickly turns into a series of endless micro-tasks.

Open a subreddit. Find a good thread. See if someone else has already answered better. Formulate a thought. Write it. Then, after some time, return because the author clarified something. Reply again. Check a neighboring thread. Switch to another sub. See if the previous comment was downvoted.

And most importantly: one such session yields almost nothing. Meaning only appears when you are present there regularly.

After a while, I just got tired of it. And then I made the first automation.

I automated only part of the process

I put together a small tool through OpenRouter that found new threads and immediately suggested an answer.

At first glance, it seemed like this was it: half the work was done.
