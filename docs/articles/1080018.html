Hello everyone! My name is Roman, and I am the author of the requirements management system prorelease.rf. Let's talk about RMS (Requirements Management Systems). How do you like this statistic:

  • 80% consider implementing RMS expedient

  • 67% know what RMS is

  • 4% use RMS in their work.

These survey data from hundreds of analysts are presented in the 2024 review "Which Requirements Management System to Choose: A Review of Tools". The gap between 80% and 4% is, to put it mildly, unpleasantly impressive. It would seem that requirements exist in every project, and a specialized tool is precisely what is needed. How does this happen? Do you use RMS? I think most will answer 'No' or 'Excel' (poll below).

Let's look at a more recent "Review of Russian RMS for IT and Engineering Teams". This review highlights three important factors driving the adoption of RMS:

  1. Cost of error: the project has too high a cost for an error in requirements

  2. Staff turnover: RMS allows you to be independent of staff turnover and individual experts

  3. Evidence: proof is needed that you did exactly what you promised, and checked all "dangerous" scenarios"

It seems that the practice of using RMS has shifted from where requirements simply exist to where they are indispensable. It's not like tasks where if there are tasks, there's Jira. RMS is no longer just about IT, but about projects in aviation, automotive, medicine, and finance.

I propose we move from aviation and automotive to more familiar IT projects within small teams and analyze the factors separately.

Cost of error. Look, if I'm not an "Boeing" equivalent, can I afford to make mistakes and depend on specific employees? Of course not. Regardless of the team size or budget, the quality of the result must be maintained at a high level. It is assumed that the rule here should apply: correcting an error at the requirements gathering stage is exponentially cheaper than fixing a problem at other stages, and the closer this stage is to the customer, the greater the losses.

Staff turnover is, of course, a problem. But if the project is relatively small and lasts, say, six months, it becomes not critical.

Why is evidence needed? By and large, it's a matter of responsibility. Software itself cannot yet cause harm to health. Harm can be caused by a product that includes software. And this gap explains why pure IT projects pay little attention to this factor. Consequently, another bureaucratic barrier in the form of requirements management processes is usually not needed by the contractor.

It seems to me that out of the listed factors, only the "cost of error" can influence the situation with RMS in IT projects. And what is the cost of error in IT projects? It's the cost of rework and fixing deficiencies. Plus reputational losses.

We've discussed the factors, now let's talk about the other side: why manage requirements at all? What problems can be solved with RMS? I will provide a brief overview of publications on the Internet:

  • NaPiRE — Naming the Pain in Requirements Engineering, 2016 Gives these figures: Incomplete/hidden requirements were identified as a critical problem by 48% of organizations;

  • Mitigating risk of failure in information technology projects: Causes and mechanisms, 2023: Requirements are highlighted as one of the 11 persistent groups of failure factors.

  • Various websites provide statistics: in 37%-39% of cases, problems with requirements lead to project failure:

We see that there are problems with requirements, and perhaps RMS can help solve them. But how to assess the benefit of RMS? There are various outdated methods for evaluating the economics of errors in requirements. I don't think it's worth applying NASA methodologies to a typical "civilian" project. Boehm's Law in its pure form has probably lost its relevance due to the automation of CI/CD and testing, modular architecture, and other innovations of recent decades.
In the article Are Delayed Issues Harder to Resolve? Revisiting Cost-to-Fix of Defects throughout the Lifecycle, the authors, with a median team of 7 people and a median duration of 61 days, could not confirm the exponential increase in the laboriousness of correction that forms the basis of Boehm's methodology.

I could not find a modern methodology for estimating the cost of rework applicable to a small team. If you know of any, please share them in the comments.

And what is the cost of RMS?

Several Russian vendors publish the cost of their solutions. The monthly subscription cost starts from 2,590 rubles for a starter set of features. For a small team, this is 150-250 thousand rubles per year. The next price range is a subscription of 32,700 rubles per month for a comprehensive product that includes not only RMS functions but also architectural tools.

The cost of maintaining on-premise versions, according to open sources, ranges from 1.449 to 7.524 million rubles per year.

As a friend of mine, an AI agent, says, the picture is now completely clear: RMS is complex and expensive. And calculating the effect is extremely difficult, which means justifying the budget for purchase to the business will be problematic. Now the figure of 4% using RMS becomes understandable.

Conclusion from the previous review part is as follows: Managing requirements is useful, including for small teams. But the complexity and cost of traditional RMS do not suit most teams. As a result, RMS is replaced by a set of makeshift tools: Jira, documents, spreadsheets, agreements, managers, etc. The problem is distributed among tools and people.

Project Prorelease.rf

I set out to build a lightweight requirements management system for small teams. The system should be simple and inexpensive. Accessible and understandable. Obviously, for such a project, it is important to define boundaries, otherwise, it won't be cheap, and an excess of functions will complicate the product.

What is the minimum RMS needed? Here is the set I am currently working with:

  1. requirements catalog with stable requirement identifiers

  2. sources of requirements

  3. change history of approved requirement versions

  4. link to release, task, acceptance criteria

  5. import from existing documents and export to common formats

Target scenario:

Customer sent technical specifications in text document format -> Analyst populates requirements catalog -> Distribution of requirements by releases -> Definition of acceptance criteria -> Assignment of tasks -> Control of release preparation

I launched my project at prorelease.rf. It is still very young. Currently, the functionality is focused on creating a requirements catalog. Authentication via Yandex is available, one free project for a team of 6 people, uploading source documents, working with the requirements catalog, assigning releases, exporting the requirements catalog as a text document or spreadsheet. I await your feedback and suggestions.
