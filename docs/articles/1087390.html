During harvest, grain travels a long path inside a combine harvester: from the header through the threshing drum, sieve, and cleaning shoe to the grain elevator and hopper. This entire path is not for nothing; it's designed to clean the grain as much as possible while preserving its integrity, as the selling price directly depends on this. However, you usually only find out how well the cleaning process went after the fact: at the grain elevator or in a lab, when it's already too late to influence the harvest outcome.

I'll tell you how we created the Crop Control system, which can be retrofitted onto a combine harvester (even if it had no automation before) and allows monitoring of grain quality and quantity directly during operation. Quantity monitoring is needed to understand how much crop has been harvested from a specific field: yield is one of the key metrics for assessing the productivity of the entire harvesting campaign. Quality monitoring is necessary to influence the harvested product in a timely manner, for example, by adjusting combine settings, and to prevent already threshed grain from being spoiled.

System Components

The system consists of several modules.

The first is the grain quality assessment module. It is installed on the combine's grain elevator and takes a grain sample directly from the flow after cleaning.

The second is the grain level sensor. It is placed near the hopper, and from it, two sensors in the form of aluminum strips extend into the hopper, from bottom to top. They are used by the system to determine the fill level of the grain hopper.

The third is the instantaneous grain volume sensor. It is installed on the grain elevator next to the quality assessment module and is designed to estimate the volume of grain on the elevator buckets.

Depending on the configuration chosen by the client (autonomous or integrated with the autopilot), a navigation unit may also be included in the kit.

How Grain Passes Through the System

The header cuts the ears of grain, which then go into the threshing drum (Fig. 5). After the drum, the grain goes to the sieves, then to the cleaning shoe (if the combine has one), from there to the grain elevator, and finally to the unloading auger.

Cognitive Crop Control analyzes grain at two points along this path.

The first point is the sampler on the grain elevator. During installation, two holes are made in the elevator, through which the system takes a grain sample approximately every 6-8 seconds, analyzes it, and returns it back to the flow. Also located on the elevator is a pair of optical sensors that determine the grain height on the elevator buckets. This indirectly allows for mapping instantaneous yield.

The second point is the storage hopper, where the grain level sensor is installed.

What Parameters the System Measures and Their Practical Significance

Some parameters are displayed to the operator instantly, while in motion, and others, which are cumulative, are aggregated and shown in the agro-service, as it is often physically impossible to assess parameters like yield at a specific point on the field without considering the overall picture.

Instantly displayed on the screen are: grain moisture, grain temperature, percentage of broken grains, foreign matter content, and hopper fill level, as well as a photographic image of the grain (Fig. 6).

Moisture and Temperature. The system measures temperature to more accurately determine moisture. Moisture itself is determined by the capacitive method: the grain quality assessment module has a pair of capacitor plates, which measure the dielectric permittivity of the medium, and from this, the moisture content is calculated with a correction for the crop type.

Photographic Image. Displayed in three user-selectable modes: normal image, detection (the system highlights grains and determines their dimensions and size), and segmentation (the system shows detected broken and damaged grains, as well as grain and non-grain impurities). A large dataset for each grain crop was collected to train the models (Figs. 7-17 show examples of some of them), and a separate neural network was trained for each crop.

Broken Grains and Foreign Matter. These parameters are determined from the photographic image using neural network methods. Broken grains directly affect the selling price of grain, making this parameter particularly interesting to farmers. Foreign matter content (grain and non-grain impurities) affects the grain classification and, importantly, can be corrected during harvest if excessive amounts are detected in time (Fig. 18).

Hopper Fill Level. This is displayed to the operator to stop the filling process in time. Many combines already have standard systems for this, but we have implemented our own solution for this task, including notifying the operator.

Separately, in the agro-service (Fig. 19), rather than on the operator's screen, yield data is available. It is aggregated as the combine traverses the entire field and is presented as a yield heat map, broken down by sections. The agronomist can compare this map with soil maps (including data from our other product, the RKAAP soil robot) and prepare a map for differentiated fertilizer application for the next season, thus saving on fertilizers. Previously, an agronomist had to take two to three manual measurements across the field to assess the overall situation; the system provides a much denser and more accurate picture, broken down every 10-20 meters of the field.

An additional, not always obvious benefit of controlling grain quality and quantity is that it helps prevent grain substitution during unloading, where instead of several tons of harvested quality grain, a cheaper feed mass is accidentally (or intentionally) loaded into the elevator.

Measurement Principle

The system can be called multi-component: each parameter is handled by a separate measurement unit, except for the photographic image, from which several parameters (grain size, breakage, foreign matter, etc.) can be obtained simultaneously.

Physical characteristics, temperature, and moisture (via dielectric permittivity) are measured by separate sensors. Other parameters requiring visual analysis are determined optically: a pair of optical sensors on the elevator and a camera with artificial lighting inside the module take high-resolution images, which are then processed by a neural network.

Model inference occurs on a separate computer installed directly in the grain quality assessment module on the elevator. This is done intentionally: this way, calculations do not consume resources from the autopilot's computer, which is simultaneously analyzing images from the combine's roof cameras.

What the Operator Sees in the Cab

If the combine is equipped with the Cognitive Agro Pilot autopilot, the operator has a tablet with a screen and a "Spikelet" tab in the application. Opening it while in motion, the user sees an interface with a photographic image of the grain (display mode can be switched), indicators for moisture and broken/foreign matter content in percentages, temperature in degrees Celsius, and hopper fill level from 0 to 100% (Fig. 20).

A separate field displays recommendations: short and clear instructions on what to do if set thresholds are exceeded or not met. For example, if the proportion of broken grain increases, the system suggests increasing the gap between the drum and the concave or reducing the threshing speed, and if foreign matter content exceeds the limit, increasing the blowing force. Since the image updates every 6-8 seconds, the operator sees in real-time whether the adjustment made has helped.

Where the Data Goes

The data first goes to the operator's tablet so that a decision on adjusting the combine's settings can be made instantly, while spoilage can still be prevented. Simultaneously, telemetry data is sent to the server, where it becomes available to the farm owner and agronomist in the form of heat maps and graphs in the agro-service. There, data from Crop Control is combined with data from the autopilot and the RKAAP soil robot into a single, dense telemetry map of the field.

How It Differs from Analogues

Elevator-based quality control systems have a fundamental limitation: they operate after harvest, when it's too late to influence it. The purpose of Crop Control is precisely to provide this information in real-time, while the combine is still moving through the field.

Among the solutions for in-combine grain quality control, there is, for example, John Deere's Grain Sense system (Grain analyzer). It uses a high-speed camera (around 120 frames per second) pressed against the grain elevator: grain on the elevator buckets sometimes adheres to the glass, and at these moments, the camera manages to take a picture. This approach has drawbacks: processing such a high-frequency stream requires a powerful computer and significantly loads the combine's power system, and the tight adherence of grain to the glass is not always ensured, making it difficult to accurately restore the actual grain dimensions by converting pixels to millimeters. Furthermore, this system cannot be transferred to combines from other manufacturers.

Cognitive Crop Control is designed differently: thanks to the sampler, the system physically extracts a grain sample from the flow and analyzes it completely every 6-8 seconds, essentially acting as an express laboratory directly on the combine. All parameters are measured from the same sample at one point, providing a consistent data slice. For comparison: in some competing solutions, moisture, temperature, and optical sensors are located in different parts of the combine, and the grain's path from header to hopper takes about 10-15 seconds, making it difficult to honestly combine their readings into a slice from the same sample.

Engineering Challenges

The most complex technical challenge throughout the development, which took about four years, was preventing grain samples from getting stuck inside the sampler. Throughout the grain processing path in a combine, something is constantly mechanically acting on the grain: it's pushed by the elevator buckets, the auger, the sieves. However, the sampler deliberately avoids a mechanical agitator, as used by some foreign competitors, because such an agitator can break the grain, compact the sample, or squeeze juice from damaged grains, which spoils the optical image for the neural network.

The solution was found in the design: a rotor with rotating rubber blades of variable geometry was added (Fig. 21). This allowed the device to handle difficult-to-process crops like soybeans and chickpeas, which can be as dense as stones and jam ordinary motors, while ensuring constant sample renewal in front of the camera.

Vibration also had to be addressed: initially, the grain mass was planned to be measured with strain gauges, but they proved too sensitive to vibration, and they were abandoned in favor of indirect calculation of mass and volume based on the entire set of measured parameters.

Dust was almost a self-solving problem: each new grain sample effectively polishes the glass, removing dust from the previous one. Dirt adhering to the glass was more problematic; regular maintenance is provided for this: the module's glass needs to be cleaned approximately once a day according to the operating instructions, similar to a car's windshield. Direct sunlight does not affect the system's operation: the camera is housed in a completely sealed, enclosed body.

Compatibility and Operating Conditions

The system is not suitable for all combines: it is important that there is a flat platform in the under-hood space between the hood and the grain elevator for mounting, without interfering moving parts or levers in that area (Fig. 22). Most Rostselmash and Gomselmash models, as well as a significant portion of Class machinery, meet this requirement and allow system installation without modifying the hood.

Power supply is standard for agricultural machinery: from 9 to 36 V, meaning the system works from standard 12 and 24 V batteries. The system is designed for a wide temperature range, but harvesting in sub-zero temperatures has a natural limitation: when harvesting wet grain in snow and rain, grain may adhere and freeze to the metal surfaces of the path, affecting not only the system but also the combine itself. Formally, this is a violation of harvesting technology, but in practice, in challenging climatic zones, farms sometimes have to harvest under such conditions.

Installation

Currently, installation takes 8 to 16 hours, and the team is constantly working to reduce this time (Fig. 23): the typical duration was recently reduced from 16 to 8 hours. Most of the time is usually spent not on the installation itself, but on organizational matters: to attach one of the grain quality analyzer modules, a bracket corner needs to be welded, and finding a welding machine and welder at the farm often becomes a bottleneck.

Initially, some operators were wary of the system, primarily because it introduced additional control over the harvesting process. Over time, this attitude changes, much like how people eventually got used to surveillance cameras on roads and in public places. Most objections are related not so much to the system itself as to the natural caution towards a new and unfamiliar tool; after operators and agronomists start working with the system, they quickly get used to it and no longer want to go back to working without it, according to the team's observations.

Early Results

One of the first cases where the system brought commercial benefit to a farmer occurred during the early stages of product testing. A client harvesting soybeans reported that the system detected a high percentage of broken grain in the image. At that time, automatic notifications were not yet available, so the team manually messaged the client, attaching a photo. Just 20 minutes after adjusting the combine settings, whole soybeans in pods appeared in the image instead of broken ones. Now, similar situations are tracked automatically through the warning and alert system.

Future of the System

In the near future, the plans include geographical expansion: besides Russia, we are preparing trials in Brazil and South Africa, where the system will need to be adapted to different operating conditions (longer harvest seasons, different temperature ranges) and new crops, including sugarcane. Among the metrics under development is the determination of protein content in grain: this is one of the key characteristics for grain classification, and the team continues to work on improving the accuracy of this parameter to the required level.

Regarding the crop quality control industry as a whole, the team believes the trend is towards full automation: from a system that only provides recommendations to the operator, to a system that can itself influence combine settings, such as the gap between the drum and concave, blowing force, threshing speed, and travel speed, reacting to changes in grain quality in real-time.
