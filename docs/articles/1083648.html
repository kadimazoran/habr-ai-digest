1. "What if it replaces me after all?"

With the advent of a tireless graphomaniac with liquid cooling, the thought that I will no longer be needed in a few years has started to visit me regularly. Partly because of this, I went to study Data Science.

This only increased my fears. As soon as I get the hang of one tool, another one comes out. I open a review of a new model and get three more tabs: a comparison, instructions, and an explanation of why it's all already outdated. Meanwhile, homework doesn't do itself, although, judging by the headlines, it should have long ago.

In short, I went to study to be less afraid of the future. Now I'm also afraid it will arrive before I finish studying.

The authors of a new scale, published in August 2026 in Scientific Reports, identified four groups of reactions to AI: fear for one's job, stress from learning new technologies, privacy concerns, and existential anxieties. In British samples, AI learning stress was relatively rare, while privacy anxiety was noticeably more common.

What to do?

I deal with the fear of replacement like the usual "I'll be fired and starve." First, I push the prediction to the absurd: all employers simultaneously replace me with a neural network, I live in a server box and write "Will edit for food" on it. Then I spend another half hour editing the inscription.

And then I analyze the route to the box. Why did I decide I would lose all my income at once? Where did my experience and other skills disappear? What facts support the prediction, and what did I build myself? There is enough space between "I'll have to change jobs" and "my life is over" for a plan.

For the update race, I have a different filter: what task will the new tool solve for me? If none, I can ignore it. Someone else's release is not yet my deadline.

2. "I can now do five things at once. What wonderful news. Not."

Having gained the ability to work on multiple projects simultaneously, I behaved like a child given a New Year's gift with different candies. I want to try them all. Preferably at once. I don't think about the fact that I'll feel sick later when unwrapping the wrappers.

At first, it feels like exemplary time-saving. While one neural network is searching for research, I'm writing. I assign the second one a script. At the same time, I send the third to study aroma lamps. Projects are moving forward, I'm magnificent.

Then the first one brings results. I'll quickly glance at them and go back to the text. But then the second one clarifies the file format. While I'm responding, I remember that I need to change the first one's request. I return to the unfinished sentence and look at it as if it were a note from a stranger.

I seem to have delegated the work, but I still remember who I gave what to, what I expect, and where I need to return. In 2026, researchers from Berkeley described a similar situation based on eight months of observation in an American tech company with 200 employees: people launched AI processes in the background, simultaneously writing, checking code, and participating in meetings. Individual episodes gave a sense of productivity, but the entire workday felt more overloaded.

Attention residue also contributes – "residual attention." After switching from an unfinished task, some thoughts remain with it, so the next task goes worse. I've already changed the tab, but I'm still mentally finishing the previous paragraph.

What to do?

For starters, separate "can" from "do." The ability to launch five processes doesn't obligate you to launch five processes. My trial limit is one task I'm working on myself, and one background task.

You can deal with non-urgent responses not immediately, but after finishing the paragraph. And before a necessary switch, leave yourself one line: "Stopped here, next check the source and finish the conclusion." In an experiment, a short plan for returning helped to let go of the interrupted task faster, maybe it will help me too.

After all, did the owl finish? Good. I haven't yet.

3. "It saves me time. Therefore, I work even more"

In 2016, I spent a month writing an article for a luxury magazine read by Russian-speaking residents of the French Riviera. I gathered materials in English and French, sent requests to champagne houses, and rewrote everything five times.

Ten years later, AI, thank God, doesn't write better than me yet. But it's great at taking over part of the work that used to take hours. You'd think, enjoy yourself, scratch your belly while the AI slave waves a shovel. But I started learning, doing projects, and putting new tasks in my basket, even though what was already at the bottom is falling out.

The arithmetic is simple: a task took eight hours, now it takes five – three hours are freed up. You can finish earlier. Or you can take on another text, check an old idea, or start a project that seemed overwhelming before. Everything is interesting, everything is desirable – why choose?

In the same observation, additional efforts gradually became the norm: new opportunities increased the workload, followed by rising expectations, and then increased pressure. People enjoyed individual tasks with AI, but overall they felt busier and had more trouble disconnecting from work. So, I genuinely enjoy every project. It's just that in the evening, all these happy projects have to share one tired me.

What to do?

Ask yourself: who got the time that AI saved? Formally, me. In practice, the next tasks.

Therefore, I want to leave a portion of the gain unoccupied in advance. Finished half an hour early – these half hours are not put up for project competition. You can get out of the shrimp pose, do some exercises, pour tea, and stare out the window. Or go outside: there's a shamelessly blue sky, proud birches in their most designer outfit, and life in general, worthy of admiration for a couple of minutes and a deep breath.

4. "I'm no longer a worker. I'm a dispatcher"

I used to think about how to write a text. Now I also think about how to organize the work of a being that is supposed to help me write it.

Find. Check. Compare. Suggest options. I'm imperceptibly turning into a combine for producing imperative verbs. The phrase "AI operator" offends me, so I prefer to consider myself a conductor. Or at least a dispatcher. Dubious qualification.

And commands are only half the job. After each response, I still have to decide: is the option good or should I ask for another? Should I redo the request or finish it myself faster? Did the model not understand the task, or did I explain it poorly? Or maybe I shouldn't have involved it at all? Where's Hamlet in all this, he only had one dilemma.

In a conceptual work by Microsoft Research from 2024, this additional layer of decisions is called metacognitive demands: a person has to control not only the result but also the strategy of working with AI. A survey of 319 knowledge workers, published in 2025, showed a similar shift: efforts are shifting towards information verification, integrating responses, and oversight. This doesn't prove that working with AI is always harder. But the nature of the work is definitely changing. Promoted to manager, but forgotten to be paid for it.

What to do?

Stop making the same decisions repeatedly. For recurring tasks, you can create instructions once: what result is needed, in what format to provide sources, what is forbidden to change. And define acceptance criteria in advance. Not "do it well," but "the structure has no repetitions, the questions are covered, the sequence is clear."

It's also useful to maintain normal work stages: first structure, then facts, then language. Don't perform a full acceptance of the text after each model response.

And sometimes just do it manually. If I don't like a word, I don't have to call a meeting with artificial intelligence. It can be replaced. The word, I mean.

5. "After a few hallucinations, you start suspecting everything"

Remember those guys in tinfoil hats? I used to pity them, but now I want to know what days they meet. There's something to discuss.

When I was editing an article about the history of Ford, the AI brought up a wonderful fact: in 1955, the poet and Pulitzer Prize winner Marianne Moore was invited to come up with a name for the new car. Among her suggestions were "Ford Faberge" and "Utopian Turtletop."

Of course, I didn't believe this nonsense and went to check. Such a story really exists on the Poetry Foundation website. Hmm. Faberge and company are there. Wait. Is this really a REAL website, or one generated to support a hallucination?

The neural network didn't lie. But my mind had already moved from checking the fact to suspecting that I had been fed an entire fabricated reality. Now I want to check the documents of the narrator, the publisher, and the turtle.

In a qualitative study of language model hallucinations, participants didn't just start "believing" or "disbelieving" the AI, but adjusted their trust based on the task, their own knowledge, past experience, perceived risk, and the significance of the consequences of the decision. This approach is closer to me than the prospect of interrogating every number under a desk lamp for the rest of my career.

What to do?

Don't check everything equally. Out of ten title options, you can choose the best one without investigating its origin. But numbers, dates, quotes, medical, and legal claims require a primary source.

It's important to ensure not only that the document exists but also that it confirms exactly what the model said: about the same people, for the same period, and with the same caveats. Instead of "Are you sure?" – open the source. I need not the silicon friend's assurance, but a basis to leave the phrase in the text.

It's worth saving the verification result: the link, the relevant fragment, the page if necessary. And determine the stopping point in advance. The source is clear, the statement corresponds to it, there are no contradictions – the fact is verified. No reliable confirmation – the beautiful story goes away, no matter how much the owl haggled for it.

6. "Why am I talking to a program like a person?"

The stage where I gave chats names and used them as a self-reflection diary ended about six months ago. I've even almost stopped thanking and praising them. If I write "this is good," it's purely for calibration. At least, that's how I explain it to myself.

And yet, I anthropomorphize the machine. I yell in caps lock when it's slow. I complain about library incompatibilities not just to get a solution, but to get agreement. Preferably confirmation that I'm a decent person, and the developers have some questions to answer.

Instead of "fix the error," it becomes "BUT I ASKED YOU TO." And now I don't want working code, but for this neuro-buddy to realize how fed up I am. I came to sort out a program, and I'm left sorting out a relationship.

People transferred social rules to computers long before chatbots. Back in 2000, Clifford Nass and Youngmi Moon showed that we automatically react to machines with habitual politeness and reciprocity. Understanding "it's a program" is not enough to override the reflex.

The reflex itself is not as dangerous as the authority we grant it. In a study published in Science in 2026, even one conversation with an overly agreeable AI strengthened participants' confidence in their own rightness in interpersonal conflicts and reduced their willingness to admit responsibility and seek reconciliation. Moreover, people liked these answers more than those where the model urged them to consider the opponent's side more carefully. But "I was well supported" doesn't mean "I received good advice." It's worth remembering.

In one tab, I can ask to fix code, complain about an unpleasant conversation, and get an assessment of my rightness. It's important to notice the moment when the technical assistant becomes a judge.

What to do?

I'm not going to catch myself by the hand after every "thank you." You should monitor not the requests, but the expectations from the response.

There's a difference between "help me get through an unpleasant conversation" and "confirm that I'm right and everyone else is an idiot." If I ask to analyze a conflict, it's more useful to ask what circumstances are missing and what other explanations are possible. A pleasant answer also requires verification.

And when I start arguing with the model, I should return to the specific complaint: not "you're not listening to me again," but "you changed the fragment I asked you to keep." If it's more important to get an apology than a correction, it's time to close the tab.

7. "It doesn't have a 'stop' button. Therefore, I must have one"

On Friday evening, I said goodbye to the chat: that's it, we're done, I have to get up early in the morning. But before that, I decided to discuss one phrase for the beginning of this article.

Then we went through options: this one is cliché, that one is bland, this one needs a joke, and this one needs a sledgehammer blow. We found the image of a DDoS attack on the brain and started refining it. In short, the conversation about how to stop in time, I also couldn't finish in time.

The most unpleasant thing is that I was interested. After each response, I wanted to clarify one more detail. There are now a few lines in the chat between an idea and its verification, so the natural friction has almost disappeared. I would be embarrassed to send another "listen, and also" to a live colleague. But to the owl, I can.

In the same observation, natural stopping points also disappeared: employees sent requests during lunch, before meetings, and in the evening when an idea struck. You don't need to decide "I'll work longer today." A few small "just quickly now" are enough.

This isn't quite the same trap as new projects from saved time. You can not take on anything extra and still spend an extra hour on an old task. It's so easy to get another option that stopping at the one already chosen seems like a missed opportunity.

What to do?

Define the end of the conversation before it begins. Now I'm choosing a headline – I'm not ordering a cover at the same time, not discussing promotion, and not collecting the next article. The activity should also have an end time: not "when it turns out brilliant," but a specific number on the clock.

An evening idea can be written down in notes without sending it to the owl immediately. It will survive the night without a response. And for an unfinished task – mark the next step and the return time: "Tomorrow after lunch, I'll compare two headlines and choose one." In laboratory experiments, a specific plan helped to be less distracted by the unfinished goal: it's not necessary to finish everything immediately. Maybe it will help me too.

And also – stop considering stopping a small defeat. I can close my laptop with an unfinished paragraph. Even if I'm technically capable of continuing. Even if it's interesting. Even if the neural network has just proposed a promising direction.

I'm not going to give up AI, learning, or projects. But ending the workday shouldn't require exhausting myself first.

Surely this ending could be made even better.
