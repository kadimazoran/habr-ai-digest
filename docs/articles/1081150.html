What Makes a Developer Valuable When AI Agents Are Getting Better at Writing Code

Over the past year, I've found myself writing significantly less code manually and delegating much more to AI agents: implementation, tests, codebase exploration, option generation, and parts of analysis. This has prompted me to re-evaluate a rather simple hypothesis about the future of development. Initially, I believed that as AI capabilities grew, an engineer's value would simply shift from writing code to architecture and technical decision-making. I decided to test this idea. I reviewed studies on developer productivity with AI, engineering company career ladders, and spoke with Middle/Senior developers, Team Leads, and Engineering Managers. The picture turned out to be more complex. AI can indeed accelerate work significantly, but the effect depends on the task, experience, and context. Green tests don't guarantee a change is production-ready. And the ability to work with uncertainty doesn't, by itself, fully explain the difference between Middle and Senior levels. In this article, I explore what starts to better indicate an engineer's level in such an environment and why implementation itself is gradually becoming less convincing proof of good engineering work. Over the past year, I've almost stopped writing code manually.

Not literally, of course. If it's faster to fix a couple of lines myself, I'll do it. But I increasingly delegate research, architectural options, implementation plans, tests, migrations, analysis of existing code, and most of the implementation itself to agents.

This hasn't reduced my engineering workload.

Code appears faster. Moreover, agents now help with more than just implementation. They can explore the system, suggest options, identify risks, and even challenge my decisions. But the main question remains.

Which conclusions am I prepared to trust, what do I need to verify myself, and ultimately, for which decision am I willing to take responsibility?

I decided to check how my experience aligns with what's happening elsewhere. I looked at recent studies, several public career ladders, and spoke with team leads and Middle/Senior developers.

Along the way, I had to abandon a few convenient theses. For example, the idea that "AI makes developers 30% faster" and that "a Senior is simply someone who can be given a poorly described task."

Ultimately, a single neat formula didn't emerge. Below, I'll break down what I managed to find out.

AI Does Speed Up Development. But Not All of It

For some tasks, AI already provides a significant advantage.

In 2026, Management Science published the results of three field experiments involving 4,687 developers from Microsoft, Accenture, and a Fortune 100 company. Developers with an AI assistant showed an approximately 26% increase in completed tasks across the combined data.

Another 2026 study involved 151 participants, 95% of whom were professional developers. On their first Java task, the group with AI finished 30.7% faster by median.

However, METR had an important caveat regarding the acceleration picture. In their study from early 2025, 16 experienced open-source developers tackled real tasks in familiar repositories and spent approximately 19% more time working with AI.

By early 2026, METR itself considers this picture outdated. In a new experiment, developers increasingly refused tasks without AI and chose tasks for exploration where the expected AI benefit was lower. Because of this, the authors consider new task-level estimates to be heavily biased and do not provide a reliable figure for current acceleration.

However, based on interviews and participant surveys, METR believes it's likely that current AI tools are already accelerating developers more than they were in early 2025.

The results are not directly comparable. The studies measured different tasks and metrics, and the effect itself changes with tools and how they are used.

AI can already significantly speed up parts of the work. But there's no single coefficient that can be applied to every engineer. It all depends on the task, the codebase, the person's experience, the tool, and how the workflow is structured.

What interested me more in these results was another question. If implementation is easier to obtain, what happens to the rest of the work?

Getting Code Faster Doesn't Mean Getting a Ready Solution Faster

One of the engineers I spoke with said he barely writes code anymore. His focus has shifted to the system as a whole and verifying what the AI has done.

Another engineer articulated this thought more precisely.

It has become more important to verify not the code itself, but whether the task has been solved correctly in the first place. Good code doesn't necessarily mean the right solution.

In 2026, DORA described a similar effect. Some of the time engineers save on code generation is then spent on auditing and verifying the results.

And METR has an even more illustrative experiment. Maintainers of three open-source projects reviewed 296 pull requests created with AI that had already passed SWE-bench tests. They still wouldn't merge about half of them into the main branch.

These PRs passed formal checks, but for the maintainers, this was insufficient. They evaluated the changes within the context of the actual codebase.

Green tests confirm that the tested scenario works. They do not prove that the change is production-ready.

In one interview, I was given an almost textbook example. A developer made a technically sound solution but failed to account for backward compatibility and error scenarios. Everything looked correct locally. The problem only appeared at the system level.

Therefore, I wouldn't call review the new bottleneck just yet. But as code generation speeds up, task definition, context, verification, and result integration start demanding more attention.

Good Code Might Solve the Wrong Problem

In my own team, I see another issue. AI has increased our speed, but along with speed, it's become easier to prematurely decide that the work is finished.

One Team Lead admitted that after a plausible AI response, it's very easy to slack off and push the result without proper verification.

Another believes that the entire process now needs to be checked, from task definition to final implementation.

This thesis resonates more with me than discussions about whether AI writes "good" or "bad" code. Good code, in itself, doesn't mean we've solved the right problem.

The problem might lie entirely outside the code. You could misunderstand a requirement, fail to consider a constraint of a neighboring service, or choose a solution that looks good locally but doesn't fit well into the overall architecture. Even tests can pass successfully and still not cover a real failure scenario.

Therefore, for me, the more important question is: Did we understand the problem correctly, choose the right solution, and verify that it works in the necessary context?

What About Fundamental Knowledge Then?

It's easy to fall into another extreme here and decide that since AI writes code, fundamental knowledge is now paramount.

I haven't found solid data that allows for such a confident comparison between "before AI" and "after AI." However, even if an agent can explore a system with an engineer, the understanding of that system is still needed by the person making decisions and verifying the results.

In one of the 2026 studies, the developer's level remained a significant factor even when using AI. At the same time, the researchers found no systematic degradation in the maintainability of AI-generated code in the tasks studied. This is also useful to remember. The assertion that "AI code is inherently worse" is too simplistic.

In an interview, one Senior developer called "technical breadth" the most important factor for growth. According to him, a frontend engineer doesn't necessarily need to become a backend developer, but it's beneficial to understand how backends, networks, databases, and architecture work, and where trade-offs arise.

Another described the same issue from a different angle. You can be excellent at writing code and still fail to see how your solution breaks something outside your own component.

For me, fundamental knowledge here isn't about memorizing syntax.

An agent can easily find several options and list their pros and cons. The complexity begins later. You need to understand which context is truly important here, which assumptions can be trusted, and which trade-off is suitable for this particular system. For example, where is the consistency boundary, and what happens during a partial failure. You need to assess the scale of potential consequences, understand how to monitor the system after deployment, and estimate the cost of such a solution a year from now.

Knowing the terms is not enough. You need to understand which trade-off is important in this specific situation.

I Thought a Senior Was Distinguished by Their Ability to Handle Uncertainty

Before the interviews, I had a rather simple model.

A Middle developer receives a more or less clear task. A Senior developer receives a problem and figures out what to do themselves.

Two Team Leads indeed described a Senior similarly. You can give them a task in general terms without needing to provide a detailed decomposition.

Then, a strong Middle+ developer told me that he doesn't feel a significant difference in autonomy and is ready to tackle almost anything.

And then I looked at the Dropbox career ladder.

There, even at IC3, independent work with open-ended and ambiguous tasks is expected. At IC4, it's not just the number of unknowns that grows. The work horizon, business impact, number of affected teams, complexity of technical solutions, and responsibility for the outcome all increase.

So, the formula "a Senior can handle uncertainty" is too simplistic.

As an engineer's level increases, they can be entrusted with decisions involving a broader context and more serious consequences.

It's no longer just about whether you can figure out a poorly described task. It's about whether you can build a model of the problem yourself, compare options, foresee consequences for neighboring systems, bring a change to production, and then understand what happened.

One of the respondents described a good practical marker of this maturity. Instead of asking "What should I do?", the person comes with their own understanding of the problem and proposed solutions.

I understood the task this way. I see two options. Here are the pros and cons. I would choose this one. What do you think?

After that, the conversation shifts to the solution itself, not the sequence of actions.

Before the Code and After the Merge

This thought was repeated most often in the interviews.

Before writing code, a less mature approach often looks the same. A person starts coding too quickly, doesn't gather context, doesn't verify assumptions, and doesn't look at what surrounds the task.

After the merge, it's easy to fall into another extreme and consider the work finished.

But production doesn't know that the ticket has already been marked as Done.

A change can affect latency, errors, load, cost, user scenarios, or neighboring services. Or it might simply fail to deliver the intended effect.

I have another personal criterion here. A strong engineer isn't obligated to turn every problem into a week-long investigation. Sometimes a quick fix is enough. Sometimes you need to get to the root cause. And sometimes it's reasonable to consciously defer a systemic issue as technical debt.

Maturity isn't about always digging deeper.

It's about choosing the right depth of solution and understanding the consequences of that choice.

Where's the Business in All This?

This topic arose not from research, but from a comment under my first LinkedIn post. Someone wrote that the next step for a developer is to stop viewing code as an independent value and start looking at the business.

The wording is blunt, but the idea was later echoed in several interviews.

One TL said that an engineer with agents should be a "product owner on a minimal plan" and understand not only the technology but also the process for which the system is being built.

One Senior phrased it even more simply. Sometimes the best engineering work is understanding that a proposed technical solution is overkill and the problem can be solved more cheaply.

I'm not concluding from this that every developer must become a Product Manager. Business context was part of strong engineering work even before the current AI wave.

If agents can be delegated more and more implementation and analysis, the quality of the context on which the solution is built becomes more noticeable. Moreover, this context doesn't just live in the repository. Part of it resides with users, in the product, processes, and goals.

Therefore, the line no longer runs between "the agent does it, the engineer thinks."

For me, it's less important who proposed the solution and more important who is responsible for its quality, decides which context and conclusions to trust, what to verify, and what consequences to accept.

Can This Be Taught Outside of Work?

I've long been interested in production-like practice. Can an engineer gain experience in architectural decisions, working with uncertainty, rollouts, observability, and incidents if their current work rarely creates such situations?

One Senior developer replied that the truly valuable experience for him only came from real tasks.

I asked what specifically made them indispensable.

The answer wasn't about codebase size or infrastructure complexity.

A real task leads to a real positive or negative outcome—either a satisfied user or a disgruntled manager. The feeling of involvement in the process and outcome, as well as real responsibility, is important.

This answer is interesting to me precisely because it's inconvenient.

You can simulate production very well technically. But if a person knows there will be no real consequences, the question arises as to what part of the experience such an environment still cannot replicate.

At the same time, there are studies on project-based learning in software engineering, where students work with real-life tasks and even external clients. Such formats help train not only technical skills but also working with changes, teams, and client requirements. This means that at least part of the experience similar to real engineering work can be trained outside of a regular team.

Therefore, I am now more interested in understanding what exactly such an environment can train, and where its limitations begin.

What aspects of engineering experience can be trained, and what only emerges where there is real responsibility?

Instead of a Conclusion

I started with the idea that AI writes more and more code, meaning an engineer's value would simply shift to architecture and the quality of engineering decisions.

Now, this formulation seems too neat.

AI doesn't eliminate development itself. For some tasks, it significantly speeds up work; for others, it helps little; and sometimes, an experienced developer works even slower with it.

But if obtaining a plausible implementation has become cheaper, implementation itself works less well as proof of good engineering work.

You still need to show that you correctly understood the problem, chose a suitable trade-off, considered the surrounding system, safely deployed the change, and achieved the desired result.

Perhaps this is why I increasingly like the phrase "beyond the ticket." Code remains an important part of the job, but engineering responsibility has always been broader than the ticket itself.

I continue to collect such cases. I am particularly interested in those that contradict this picture.
