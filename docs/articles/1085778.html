When news about portable X-ray tomography appears, neural network images like these immediately come to mind (Fig. 1):

And in most cases, this is not too far from the truth. In fact, any tomographs that can be moved with some effort – for example, rolled from one ward to another or transported by truck to another hospital – and set up in a new location are not shyly called portable tomographs [1–4] (Fig. 2).

But fantasists promised us something completely different, and although there is a clear trend towards portable devices and miniaturization in medicine, it has not affected computed tomography (CT) – for 50 years, the form factor of medical CT devices, as well as the principles of 3D image formation, have practically not changed.

When it comes to CT, engineering thought mainly revolves around the gantry ring – we are so accustomed to the ring being an integral part of the tomograph. But in reality, the concept of X-ray CT is not related to this ring at all: essentially, for tomography, it is enough to take several X-ray projections (radiographs) from different angles, solve the inverse problem, and this will already be considered tomography – we thought, paying attention to handheld portable devices for X-ray diagnostics (Fig. 4).

Throughout the development of our technology, which we want to tell Habr about, it has had several working names: "3D radiography," "Handheld Tomography," "Tomograph in a Suitcase," "Free-Positioning Tomograph (FPT)." Now we have settled on the X-SLAM variant. From the name and the preface, it is generally possible to understand what we have conceived – to take a portable X-ray and turn it into a tomograph, by means of auto-positioning in the spirit of the classic simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) problem (or V-SLAM, V stands for visual, as opposed to X-SLAM in the X-ray range). In the visible range, V-SLAM allows a robot or smartphone to simultaneously determine its own position and reconstruct the structure of the surrounding space based on matching distinctive points in different frames.

When the idea of combining CT and V-SLAM is formulated, it seems quite obvious, and it even gives the impression that such devices must already exist. However, if you ask CT specialists (to whom we modestly count ourselves among), they will boldly tell you that nothing will come of it: the accuracy of determining the relative positions of the radiation source and detector will not be enough, there will be too few projections, they will be of low quality, and the experiment will be too long, because a portable X-ray takes several seconds to capture each projection, and at least a hundred projections are needed, and even then, the diagnostic value of the resulting images cannot be expected due to various artifacts associated with the arbitrary choice of trajectory. For comparison, conventional tomographs take thousands, and sometimes tens of thousands of projections in less than a minute with an angular accuracy of tenths of a degree.

Nevertheless, we decided to try. We fix the detector, place the object of study on it, take a portable X-ray source, which the operator manually moves near the object and takes X-ray images, connect V-SLAM and an iterative CT reconstructor – a plan as reliable as a Swiss watch.

Why V‑SLAM did NOT help us?

In the standard V-SLAM setup, the input is a set of images of the same scene taken from different angles. By finding corresponding distinctive points (correspondences) in these images and assuming a mathematical camera model – for example, a pinhole camera – the algorithm simultaneously reconstructs the three-dimensional structure of the scene and the camera positions in space. The solution is determined only up to a homothety: the absolute scale of the reconstruction cannot be determined from the images, so all camera positions and scene points can be simultaneously multiplied by the same factor without changing the observed images on the sensor. This problem can be overcome in various ways, for example, by using a pair of calibrated cameras placed at a known fixed distance from each other.

Conventional tomography fits entirely into the pinhole camera model, with the peculiarity that the "scene" is inside the virtual camera, between the optical center and the sensor-detector. At first glance, the differences between conventional CT and our "handheld" tomography setup are minimal. The camera is formed by the source-detector pair: the sensor is the detector, the optical center is the radiation source.

However, there are pitfalls: in a "handheld" tomograph, the optical center moves relative to the sensor arbitrarily, meaning that with each new frame, not only the external parameters of the "camera" change, but also the internal ones. There can be no talk of a calibrated camera, or even a fixed camera geometry. In particular, the effective focal length changes from frame to frame (Fig. 7).

In terms of projective geometry, this leads to an unexpected effect. For ordinary V-SLAM, as already noted, the solution is determined up to one parameter – the global scale. In our problem, the uncertainty is much more significant. We have shown that in the X-SLAM model, the source trajectory can only be determined up to a four-parameter family of transformations (in technical terms: these are axial collineations with an axis coinciding with the detector plane). Such transformations leave the detector plane fixed point-wise but change the positions of the radiation sources and the reconstructed points in space, whose projection correspondences on the detector we have. As a result, the problem of determining the source positions from correspondences of points on projection images has not a unique solution, but a four-dimensional subspace of solutions that equally well explain the observed data (Fig. 8).

The problem we encountered turned out to be quite fundamental, and it's not just that ready-made V-SLAM algorithms didn't work "out of the box," but that they couldn't work. In the classical case, five reliable correspondences are sufficient to reconstruct the scene geometry [5,6], and in practice, eight are often used [7–9] because the corresponding algorithms are significantly simpler. In X-SLAM, the fundamental ambiguity has a completely different nature: even an infinite number of ideal correspondences do not allow eliminating the four-parameter uncertainty without using additional a priori information. In V-SLAM, a priori information arises from the camera's calibration. It should be noted that this condition is somewhat flexible: camera parameters may not be known precisely, can be determined and refined automatically, but still, the camera is very far from the arbitrary geometry characteristic of X-SLAM.

However, the 4-dimensional projective degeneracy was not the last problem. So far, we have assumed that corresponding points, which correspond to one point in space, exist in the projections, and the question is only how accurately the shooting geometry can be reconstructed from them. But in X-SLAM, this assumption is, generally speaking, incorrect.

Where to get correspondences?

In V-SLAM, a point on the sensor of a calibrated camera corresponds to a point on the scene, and by matching the same point in two frames, its position in space can be found, which allows using all the beauty of epipolar geometry. In the case of X-ray projections, a point on the sensor corresponds to a line passing through the entire scene (Fig. 9). That is, matching points on projections is essentially a meaningless operation, as they do not correspond to the same features in the scene.

Of course, if it were that bad, we wouldn't be writing about it now. The fact is that there is one special case – areas with strong absorption. If there is a relatively small, spatially localized area with strong absorption, then the shadows of this area will provide corresponding points on the sensor, corresponding to lines passing through this area and positioning it in space. However, not everything is smooth here either; usually, these areas correspond to small objects that have significantly different outlines from different angles, and most importantly, they are not "points," unlike in the visible range, where corners and joints are mainly taken as distinctive points. This leads to the fact that standard descriptor-based approaches for matching distinctive points stop working [10]. Moreover, in the general case, at the stage of detecting areas on the projection, it is impossible to confidently distinguish between areas with large integral absorption along the line and the localized areas of interest.

To solve the first problem of 4-dimensional ambiguity, it is necessary to add some a priori knowledge to the problem, and to solve the second problem of correspondence, it is necessary to add strongly absorbing inclusions, which may not be present in an arbitrarily chosen object. Interestingly, both problems can be solved simultaneously by adding an anchor object with strongly absorbing inclusions to the experiment, and this is the path we took. Such a solution is, in a sense, a "shortcut"; a priori knowledge for the first problem could be an assumption about the shape of the object being scanned, to which we could regularize the solution, or the distance from a beacon on the detector to the source, and if necessary, it is indeed possible to do without additional tools, sacrificing universality or solution accuracy. At the same time, the anchor is a relatively simple and reliable solution to both problems, while guaranteeing the necessary accuracy for subsequent stages of work.

Developing an effective anchor object required certain efforts. Although its final pyramidal shape (Fig. 10) looks trivial, it is based on non-trivial mathematics inspired by epipolar geometry. One of the key features of our developed anchor object is that its position is not fixed in advance: it can be placed almost anywhere – both on the detector surface and directly on the object of study. This, by the way, is another reason why we call our technology X-SLAM: during the shooting process, the mutual geometry of all participating objects is automatically determined, without relying on the pre-known position of the anchor. For the developed anchor object, we have proposed mathematical algorithms that allow analytically calculating the solution to the problem of reconstructing the shooting geometry from the projections of metal balls at the anchor's nodes. To avoid overloading an already lengthy article, we will refer the mythical interested readers to the detailed description in our patent [11].

What else did we not expect?

We did not expect a good reconstruction result.

We implemented a method for calculating the trajectory of the radiation source from the corresponded projections of the balls at the vertices of the anchor object. And it turned out that with the accuracy of auto-positioning provided by our method, even quite ordinary classical low-angle reconstruction algorithms can demonstrate a not-so-bad result. This inspired us to refine and improve the reconstruction algorithms. As a result, we have developed unique, not at all ordinary reconstruction algorithms, significantly different from classical ones, but this is a topic for a separate article.

In the meantime, let's return to the result. It surprised even experienced tomographers… in fact, it only surprised them; others perceived the result as quite natural, and the only questions that arise for an ordinary person are "Is it possible that there are no analogues?" and then "Why hasn't anyone done this before?" It turned out that 10–20 projections are already enough to obtain a 3D image with diagnostic value, for example, to measure the size of a bone defect, detect and position foreign metallic inclusions relative to the bone, or understand the structure of a complex fracture.

What about the dose?

The main and justified concern is the dose received by the device operator. Taking X-ray projections by hand is generally not intended, and with some stretch, it is only used in dentistry. Nevertheless, the prognosis here is quite optimistic, as we use an extremely low-power microfocus source [12]. The dose received by the patient does not exceed the dose received from one day of stay in Moscow or St. Petersburg [13] (Fig. 15). The operator receives a dose orders of magnitude lower, as they are not in the direct beam of radiation and are protected by X-ray shielding. Nevertheless, there is a long bureaucratic path ahead before the widespread use of such devices, which we intend to follow.
