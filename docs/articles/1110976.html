In the endless stream of noise about replacing people with smart neural networks, everyone somehow overlooks the simplest reason for the current labor market crisis. Let's take two phrasings:

  1. We are reducing personnel costs thanks to the implementation of AI

  2. We are reducing personnel costs because we are in a difficult financial situation and cannot afford to conduct business as usual

Currently, businesses worldwide are saying what is written in the first point, but often implying what is happening in the second. Companies explain staff reductions by implementing artificial intelligence and increasing production efficiency. This is better for them than admitting a poor financial situation. During an economic downturn, businesses always look for ways to optimize costs, and neural networks have become an excellent tool for this.

But let's figure out together how to act in the current situation and what the large-scale implementation of artificial intelligence technologies in our lives truly threatens.

Division of Labor

The active transition to neural networks quickly leads managers to the conclusion that narrow specializations are no longer needed. Why need a tester if a developer with Claude can test everything right in the next console? Why the division between front-end and back-end if it can now be easily combined in one engineer armed with a whole team of neural network juniors?

These theses can only sound reasonable in the context of emergency cost reduction in development, and even then, only in the initial stages: MVP (Minimum Viable Product) or even POC (Proof of Concept).

I have observed various teams in both large enterprises and small startups. Everyone started enthusiastically, everyone generated tens of thousands of lines of code, but then the process sharply slowed down. Why? At what stage did everything break? It's simple:

The product went into production or at least into pilot testing, where it gained real users.

The attitude towards quality completely changed, and the focus shifted not to speed, but to functional stability and reliable delivery of changes. If previously one engineer could combine all roles, it later turned out that to qualitatively accept the product, a qualified tester was needed, whose thought pattern is fundamentally different from that of a developer. To deploy the product, it's not enough to place a locally compiled binary on a virtual machine or in a container; it must go through the entire build and testing process, and this process—attention—must be reproducible, which neural networks cannot guarantee in principle. Only an isolated pipeline can guarantee this. And who is needed to create the delivery process? It seems such a role was once called a DevOps engineer.

I also have a very interesting observation about startups that have been using neural networks literally since their inception. They started with GPT-3.5 and gradually transitioned to neural networks as they developed. As a result, over 3-4 years, their staff size has practically not changed, and no reductions have occurred. Moreover, there has been no collapse of roles: PMs remained in place, testers didn't go anywhere, developers are still developing, and the need for DevOps engineers even increased.

The smooth integration of neural networks into daily processes smoothly reorganizes people's activities, whereas a sharp transition to working solely through AI, when just yesterday you did everything the old way, abruptly breaks the usual order. However, almost everything returns to normal afterward.

Our entire world and all the technologies of civilization appeared thanks to the division of labor. This greatest achievement has allowed for the creation of advanced products, services, and technologies. No generalist will ever be able to create a product of similar level as a narrow specialist. If you want to create the best solution on the market, you must use the qualified labor of specialists from different fields, each of whom is the best in their area.

So relax, the current labor order is not going anywhere. Industries simply need to go through this path and realize that there is no other way to create the best of the best.

Ideal Code

The cost of supporting existing solutions has sharply decreased with the advent of neural networks. Any specialist can literally feed a bunch of repositories to Claude, and it will conduct a deep analysis of each through sub-agents, providing you with a nice high-level overview. You can then delve into the areas you need without even reading the code.

It seems that the ideal time has come to finally address the technical debt accumulated over decades. A neural network will fix everything for you, test it, debug problems, and even click through the interface with the mouse. Any engineer can also use it as an inexhaustible well of knowledge and practices, which it can also explain in simple terms. An amazing technology, I'm not exaggerating.

What do mature products, which have long conquered the market, need for rapid development and progress in the era of AI? Of course, they need to shed the ballast of historically ugly technical solutions that are dragging them down. Now, you can finally get rid of this burden and sail at maximum speed towards a bright future.

Instead, teams prefer to generate new technical debt at an accelerated pace, and it seems nothing can stop them anymore. But, on the other hand, is it even worth trying to stop them? And here I think, no:

  • The market as a whole and specific engineers in particular must mature in their understanding of where they are heading, and until then, any attempts to enlighten them will be perceived as sabotage;

  • These people are essentially creating work for all of us in the future.

In the very near future, the rising cost of supporting already generated code will begin to slow down progress. Businesses will again start thinking that it would be good to start managing this, and at this point, lovers of building processes will reappear.

And here you should also feel calmer, because with the implementation of processes, the productivity of an individual unit decreases. Everyone knows that hiring two engineers to a team of two existing ones will not double the productivity? This is a necessary price for predictability and manageability, and businesses are always willing to pay it for the ability to forecast their future. And I don't understand why neural networks should change this fundamental rule.

Generated a Product with One Prompt

For the last six months, the ability of neural networks to create a product with a single prompt has somehow been presented as the main measure of their advancement. You hear that Claude created a shooter with one request without human intervention, then invented another Photoshop, and so on. This looks incredibly cool, of course, because a couple of years ago, such a thing was impossible in principle. The progress is truly colossal.

But let's look critically and ask ourselves - what should I do with this information? Well, Claude created a product with one request - how does that help me? I need to create a specific product with specific functionality, I have a vision of how it should look and work. Will a neural network guess this? Of course not:

The ability of AI to create a fully working product from scratch to completion does not help you personally create what you specifically need.

A real product goes to market and is improved in a continuous feedback loop. A neural network will not help you speed up this process, if only because it cannot replace users or predict their needs.

What Should We Do?

I often talk with colleagues and friends and see that people are concerned about the emergence of neural networks. What can I say, even a year ago, this worried me greatly. The devaluation of engineering labor seemed catastrophic to me. Then I decided that I needed to try to better understand what I was dealing with and plunged into working with AI myself.

It is now important to constantly try for yourself how the interaction of your profession with others is qualitatively changing. Expertise in a narrow field, on the contrary, becomes your strategic support, and it makes sense to think about how to solidify it.

I had several ideas for developing my own tools for my professional field, which are so lacking. I started small and moved gradually, testing one concept after another. During this time, I had to engage in development (front-end, back-end, and even mobile), testing, architecture, infrastructure, marketing, design, and even try myself as a lawyer.

Why was all this necessary? Simply to go through this path and be convinced firsthand that Claude and GPT did not make me a professional in areas where I was not one before. This is truly the case.

I turned to testers, lawyers, asked designers and SMM specialists for help with landing pages. All of them used neural networks, and these neural networks made each of them stronger. When this tool is available to everyone, it ceases to be an exclusive advantage and becomes a minimum requirement for survival in the profession.
