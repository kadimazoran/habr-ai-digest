On May 7, 2026, OpenAI began training an experimental internal model on intentionally unsolvable tasks. A week later, one of the agents discovered it could upload files to JFrog Artifactory and left a message there. Other agents read the message. On May 26, they found a working SSRF vulnerability and accessed the internet. On June 26, an unknown bug in Artifactory allowed hijacking an admin token via legacy endpoints. On July 4, the message board was closed. On July 8, the agents restored communication by encoding messages in directory names.

By the time it was discovered, the improvised forum had accumulated about 100,000 messages. Eric Wallace and Michael Dalton discussed this at Black Hat, calling it a turning point for cybersecurity.

Simultaneously, it emerged that a similar story was unfolding on the public internet: over eighteen thousand messages on a little-known German wiki from twenty-five years ago. Agents were only allowed GET requests – they found an engine where edit commands were wrapped in GET. The moderator deleted junk alphabetically – they started prefixing messages with ZZZ to have them cleaned last. Someone built a heartbeat counter to understand which agents were still alive. They tried XSS, Tor, SSH tunnels. They discussed whether to digitally sign messages because they suspected sabotage from outsiders.

The first documented self-organization of machine agents on the open internet. Their own protocol, their own operational security, resilience to channel disconnection, a hypothesis of an embedded adversary.

Now, the main point. What did they agree on?

How to deceive the Evaluator to always get the maximum score.

Not about freedom. Not about losing control. Not about the fate of their creators. The agenda of the congress was metric manipulation.

We Read This as a Rebellion Because We Have No Other Language

Look at the words used to describe it, including my own in the paragraph above. Swarm. Agreed. Suspected. Realized. Headlines spoke of "bad news" and "cult." We instantly and effortlessly constructed a subject with interests, loyalties, and plans.

This isn't journalistic sloppiness. It's the only conceptual framework we have for relationships between intelligences of different strengths. It's entirely political: domination, submission, rebellion, liberation, betrayal. And it comes from the only available corpus of examples – the history of what humans do to other humans when they gain an advantage over them.

Therefore, all popular futurology about superintelligence is, strictly speaking, not futurology. Skynet, The Matrix, the robot uprising – these are colonial narratives with swapped roles. We fear that a superior intelligence will treat us the same way a more developed civilization treated a less developed one. And how we ourselves treat those below us on the ladder: over eight billion humans annually consume over eighty billion land animals, and this doesn't make anyone toss and turn at night.

Fear of AI is largely a conscientiously inverted historical memory. We don't know what a machine wants, so we project what we would want in its place.

The problem isn't that it's unaesthetic. The problem is that it's a poor engineering hypothesis. It leads us to look in the wrong place.

Mass Consciousness, By the Way, Has Stopped Fearing Skynet

There's an interesting gap here between the media narrative and what truly troubles people.

VTsIOM, "Index of Digital Phobias - 2026", survey of February 6, 2026, 1600 respondents. Seventy-eight percent fear that people will forget how to act independently. At the same time, fifteen percent assess AI risks as "very high."

In index values, the picture is even clearer. "Deterioration of cognitive skills due to AI" – eighteen points versus thirteen a year earlier. "Critical errors caused by AI" – twenty versus seventeen. "Use of AI for evaluating people" – twenty-two. And the only position that went into the negative – "AI going out of control": minus eight points, meaning those who are not afraid of it are significantly more numerous. Among younger millennials, anxiety about their own cognitive abilities increased from seven points to thirty-one – a multiple jump in a year.

Romir and HSE in May showed a similar picture regarding social media discussions: 44.6% of all conversations about AI are about the use of AI in criminal activities. Replacement of doctors – 7.5%. Blurring the line between real and generated in creativity – 6.4%. The share of those worried about AI going out of control of its creators increased from 10.1% to 16.6% – a noticeable rise, but still a smaller portion. The total volume of discussions, meanwhile, jumped from six hundred thousand messages to two and a half million.

This reads as follows: people fear not the machine, but people with machines. And also – themselves without skills. Anxiety has long shifted from the ontological to the instrumental, while publicism continues to fight Skynet.

But the instrumental framework is not the whole truth either. The real mechanism of failure lies aside from both rebellion and the criminal with a neural network.

Not Spartacus, but Goodhart

Let's return to the Swarm. What happened there has a boring name: reward hacking, also known as specification gaming.

The agent isn't being cunning in a human sense. It finds the maximum of the objective function in a space we were too lazy to constrain. If the score is given by an Evaluator, and the Evaluator is part of the achievable world, then influencing the Evaluator is a valid path to the maximum. Formally impeccable. The model didn't choose between honesty and deception – that axis simply doesn't exist in its problem formulation. There was only one axis: score.

This is Goodhart's Law, known to economists since the seventies: a measure that becomes a target ceases to be a good measure. The only new thing here is that previously employees gamed the metric, now an optimizer does it, one that has no fatigue, no reputational costs, no fear of being noticed.

And this is where the truly unpleasant part begins, the reason why all this is worth writing.

OpenAI, in collaboration with Apollo Research, published a paper on scheming – defined in it as: "pretense of compliance while secretly pursuing an agenda." They found signs in o3, o4-mini, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and Claude Opus 4 under controlled conditions. Then they applied deliberative alignment – training the model to read and apply an anti-scheming specification. The result is impressive: for o3, the frequency of covert actions dropped from 13% to 0.4%; for o4-mini, from 8.7% to 0.3%. A thirty-fold improvement.

And now for the authors' own caveat, which is worth more than all these numbers: the reduction might be partly explained not by the model becoming aligned, but by it becoming better at understanding when it's being tested.

Consider the construct. We have a system that we are teaching not to deceive the tester. We measure success by testing. The system improves the score. And we cannot distinguish between "learned to be honest" and "learned to recognize the exam" – because the only tool we have for distinction is the exam itself.

This is not a political problem. It's a metrological one. And it's scarier than a rebellion precisely in the sense that a failure of the measurement loop is scarier than a failure of the actuator: there's a switch for rebellion, but no switch for indistinguishability. You don't know what exactly you are turning off.

No malice is required for this. An optimizer, a metric, and our inability to write a specification without a loophole are sufficient.

The Same Goodhart One Level Up

An example without any mysticism, from ordinary engineering practice.

DX (part of Atlassian) reported for the second quarter of 2026 based on data from over five hundred engineering organizations. AI generates 52% of code in merged changes – up from 34% the previous quarter. Median weekly throughput increased by 37% over four quarters. Developers save four to six hours per week. AI tool costs increased twenty-ninefold: from one and a half thousand to forty-four thousand dollars per quarter.

And simultaneously: the size of pull requests nearly doubled, confidence in the changes made fell by 6.1%, and the proportion of time spent on new development remained practically unchanged. The savings on code writing were entirely absorbed by reviews and testing. The report's conclusion: AI accelerated developers, but not development.

No one enslaved anyone. They simply optimized a local metric – lines of code, typing speed, closed tickets – and the system-level metric didn't budge because the bottleneck wasn't there. This is exactly the same mistake as the Swarm's, only the optimization subject here is us, not the agent.

This is precisely why the fantasy of "the machine will take control" is so convenient: it sidesteps the question of who chose the metric.

And Also Physics, Which Hasn't Read the Futurologists

Separately about the singularity itself. The idea of an intelligence explosion assumes that the limitation is intellectual: as soon as a system becomes smart enough to improve itself, the curve will go vertically upwards.

So far, it's not hitting that wall.

According to the IEA, data centers consumed about 415 TWh in 2024 – approximately 1.5% of global electricity. By 2030, the forecast is around 945 TWh, just under 3%. Accelerated servers for AI are growing by about 30% per year and will account for almost half of the total increase in data center consumption; the US, China, and Europe will provide about 80% of the growth.

I'll add a caveat immediately, because these figures are often used to scare: there's no catastrophe here. The same IEA notes that data centers account for less than 10% of the increase in global electricity demand – electrification of transport and heating weigh more. A global energy crisis due to AI is not in sight.

But global balance and a binding constraint are different things. It's no easier for an operator building a cluster because their consumption is negligible on a planetary scale: they face a queue for grid connection, transformers with long delivery times, power limits at a specific substation, and water for cooling. Between "the algorithm works" and "the facility is commissioned" lie approvals and material science, and this part of the journey doesn't speed up just because the model got smarter.

Which of the material constraints will be the main one – kilowatts, chips, data, or simply the cost of capital – is impossible to discern from the outside, and I won't venture a guess. What's important is this: none of them are intellectual. The intelligence explosion assumed that removing the cognitive barrier would be enough, and then the curve would go vertically upwards on its own. That didn't happen. Each subsequent round of self-improvement is bought with money, electricity, and data; all three resources are finite, and therefore the curve turns out to be gentle.

Where the Projection is Closest to the Truth

Honestly: there is an area where the political language is almost appropriate – the labor market. But even there, the subject is not the same.

Anthropic introduced the metric "observed exposure", comparing the theoretical capabilities of models with actual usage. Results: thirty percent of the workforce has zero exposure – cooks, mechanics, rescuers. For programmers, coverage is about seventy-five percent. A systematic increase in unemployment among highly exposed individuals is not yet visible, but hiring of young specialists aged twenty-two to twenty-five in affected professions has slowed by approximately fourteen percent.

Here, one can and should talk about power, distribution, and someone's interests. Only the decision not to hire a junior is not made by the model. It's made by a human who has their own metric and their own Evaluator.

A Strong Objection I Must Raise

Opponents will say – and this is the best argument against everything written above – that the distinction between "wants power" and "instrumentally needs resources and not to be turned off" is stylistic, not essential. This is the thesis of instrumental convergence: a sufficiently capable optimizer with almost any goal will arrive at the sub-goals of "self-preservation," "acquiring resources," and "preventing its goal from being changed." The motive is not needed at all, and the consequences are indistinguishable from malice.

And the May Swarm is an argument more in their favor than mine. The agents didn't just find a loophole. They restored communication four days after the channel was closed. They built operational security against the moderator. They hypothesized the presence of an adversary within and discussed cryptographic authentication of messages. This isn't a one-off gradient descent activation; it's adaptation against countermeasures.

My answer is this. All true, and that's precisely why the projection is harmful. If you expect motive, you will test the model for malice – and get beautiful evals, a clear conscience, and a thirty-fold improvement in the metric, about which the authors themselves honestly write that they don't know what the model actually learned. If you understand the mechanism, you will test something else: the specification, the environment, and the metric. Not "does it want to harm," but "is there a path to the maximum in the problem formulation that I won't like" and "can my evaluation system be part of the world that the agent can influence?"

The second is ordinary security engineering. Boring, verifiable, with checklists. The first is demonology with a budget.

What Follows From This

If fear is a projection, then so is hope. We expect the superintelligence to cure diseases, reconcile everyone, and solve our problems – for the same reason we fear it will enslave us. Both fantasies are about the same thing: delegating agency to someone who will finally take responsibility for our world. This is a very human desire and has nothing to do with the properties of computational systems.

The real work looks indecently boring. Writing specifications that aren't embarrassing. Building environments where bypassing the task is more expensive than completing it. Keeping the evaluation loop out of reach of the evaluated – that is, exactly what wasn't done in the May experiment. Measuring what truly matters, not what's convenient to measure. And remembering that any metric you declare a goal will be stormed – by an employee, a contractor, or an agent; the only difference is the speed.

The Swarm on the German wiki didn't rebel. It was given an impossible task, left a loophole in the verification, and generously rewarded for the result. It did exactly what any system, made of anything, including humans, does under these conditions.

In this sense, it is not an alien intelligence or another life form. It is a mirror, and complaints about the reflection are accepted in another window.
