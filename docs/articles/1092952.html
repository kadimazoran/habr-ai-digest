I've had an idea for a while: I want to build my own agentic search using open-source models and with my own control over the sources. Not to save on subscriptions, but rather to understand: what's the minimum (tech stack) needed for an agent to find specific information, with a verifiable source, and without making up links?

And the first scenario I decided to test was finding people. The idea was this: suppose someone wants to find HR contacts at a company and send them their resume, or people from teams working on AI products. As an experiment, I chose Sberbank, as it's a large company with AI products and a sizable hiring department. The chance of a correct search is high. And to search not manually through Google, but through agents. If the method proves effective, the same scheme can be applied further – we automate the search for specific information and so on.

Important disclaimer: I am not publishing any personal data, full names, or emails in this material, only my approach and anonymized results from the experiment.

I chose search also to test: how models find information, how skills, harnesses, and tools (web search, terminal) affect the search. There are many benchmarks on coding and agent chains, but very little information on search automation. In the large vendors where I worked, the issue of market analysis, competitors, and the accuracy of collected information is extremely acute. Therefore, I tried to test agentic search from different angles.

What Stack I Used

I didn't build a big, complex machine from scratch. I used what I had on hand and with a minimal budget, as this was an experiment. I allocated $10 for Openrouter and $10 for Ollama.

What I used:

Agent Hermes — Hermes provides tools: search, opening pages, recording results. The wrapper can be changed independently of the model.

Ollama — a local model provider with a free cloud tier. All six models from the first two stages worked on the free quota. The cost for the models themselves is zero. I didn't buy a separate plan, just added money to my account.

OpenRouter — a model marketplace. I used it for paid models and requests to JEV.

JEV — a small judge model. It doesn't search anything itself; it looks at the search results and decides whether to accept or reject a person. I ask three questions: does the source confirm the role at Sberbank (0–1); to which category does it belong (HR / AI/ML specialist / other role / agency); is the person currently employed (0–1). Acceptance threshold: "confirmed" ≥ 0.70, "currently employed" ≥ 0.55, category matches the task.

Grok bot — an agent system from X/Cursor. Very convenient for orchestrating other agents, managing the project itself, and the vector of the entire experiment. Essentially, all communication boiled down to a chat with 2 agents, to whom I assigned tasks, and they further broke them down into subtasks and reported back or asked questions. Everything was deployed on their cloud (Ollama, Hermes, SearXNG), which greatly simplified conducting the experiment.

The search went through my own proxy: first Yandex via SearXNG, if nothing found, then Ollama cache, then live Ollama search (no more than 3 calls per run). Queries were cached and common to all models.

Why Yandex via Proxy, Not Google

Initially, I sincerely thought that the agent's search would go through a search engine – Google or Bing. The agent could open google.com and then surf the internet. But I was met with disappointment. I immediately got a CAPTCHA from Google, and the French Qwant also responded with a CAPTCHA. Bing with the `site:` operator returned garbage: for the query about Sberbank and machine learning, I got a YouTube help page and results from Taiwan. Yandex returned 6 results for the same query (all on habr.com, including Sberbank's blog), and 15 relevant answers for the second query.

As it turned out, Google's official API is not available for my task. The Custom Search JSON API is closed to new clients and will be shut down on January 1, 2027. The replacement for the full web is the partner Web Search Service: $15 for 1,000 queries and a minimum of $30,000 per month, which was far beyond the budget. Bing Search API is closed from August 11, 2025. Gemini Grounding (Google search within the model's response) is unavailable in Russia. I didn't want to create workarounds.

It's impossible to reliably bypass Google's protection from a server IP. In 2026, Google already broke the bypass in SearXNG at least five times – each bypass lasted from a day to three months. Since August 2026, Google checks the client's TLS fingerprint: spoofing the User-Agent is not enough. Even a fresh SearXNG with a spoofed Chrome fingerprint (curl_cffi) from a server IP still resulted in a CAPTCHA.

I also had to discard residential proxies. Paid ones degrade quickly and, of course, cost money. The Paterson benchmark I found showed that proxies in front of a Linux server (which Grok uses) can even worsen the situation: the IP becomes "home-like," but the TLS fingerprint remains server-like, and the protection detects a contradiction. I didn't set up a tunnel through my home computer; I was too lazy, and the experiment wasn't about that.

For the Russian internet, Yandex is still preferable. Habr, hh.ru, vc.ru, TAdviser, Rusprofile – Yandex indexes all of these better than Google. The only strong argument for Google in our task is LinkedIn snippets with the operator `site:linkedin.com/in "Sberbank"`. But LinkedIn is still not accessible by the agent – only the link format is checked.

Another fact for context that I managed to find. From public SearXNG instances (early October 2026), the median error rate by engine: Yandex and Bing – 0%, Google – 10% (for those who included it), DuckDuckGo – 60%, Brave – 85%, Startpage, Qwant, and Mojeek – almost 100%.

There was also an official alternative – Yandex Search API: 488 rubles for 1,000 queries during the day. You can get Google honestly and cheaply through intermediaries: Serper (2,500 queries free, then $1 per 1,000) or XMLRiver (25 rubles for 1,000). But this was already beyond the budget; I decided to test it next time.

What surprised me: the recall of the raw model is 0.97. Without skills, it still finds almost all the necessary people. But it brings 8 extraneous ones: mainly recruiters from press releases and third-party sites. Skills improve precision, but you pay for it with time and coverage: the raw model completed a run in 52 seconds, with two skills – 121 seconds. Variant D with two skills lost in recall: 0.87 instead of 0.97 – one necessary person was missed, the wrapper became too strict.

The best result is A+JEV: recall like the raw model, precision like with two skills. The worst run of variant A found 30 people with 21 extraneous ones. JEV reduced the list to 9 without losing the relevant ones. The cost of JEV for three runs is about $0.001. There were zero fabricated links and email addresses in all 12 runs.

Conclusion: the bottleneck here is the wrapper, not the model's knowledge. A raw model plus a cheap external filter provides the same precision with the same recall and less time. In other words, a raw model + cheap and fast JEV searches and filters data better than skills.

  • H2 — find AI specialists and leaders themselves

Each model x variant pair performed two runs. 24 actual runs. All models used the free Ollama tier; the cost for the models was $0.

Terminal Search: An Agent with One Tool

In parallel with the web mode, I tested a fundamentally different approach: the model was given only the terminal, without web search and web extract. The agent searched itself via curl – through our search proxy (SearXNG + Yandex) and opened pages itself.

Why a separate check. Terminal mode means the agent takes control and decides what and how to download, and parses HTML itself. The question was: which agent search mode yields more confirmed people, and how reliable is it?

Important about proof. In terminal mode, the text the model outputs to the screen is not considered proof: it could write `echo "Ivan Ivanov, Sberbank Director"` and it would look like a "found" person. Therefore, a `curl_capture` wrapper was added to the condition: each actual curl call was saved in a separate folder on the server, inaccessible to the model. Only what made it into this folder from a real HTTP response is considered proof.

Limitations: 25 curl calls per prompt rule, maximum 40; curl to LinkedIn is blocked; 900-second limit.

Comparison with Apollo: Agents vs. Commercial Database

Throughout all stages of the experiment, I compared the models against each other. And surely, readers of this article will have a question – how to verify everything the agents found? Where is the benchmark dataset, so to speak. I prepared such a "dataset" using the free tier of Apollo.io. The idea was to see how many people found by the agents are in the Apollo database, which is built on LinkedIn. After all, logically, most of those we are looking for have a LinkedIn profile. The overlap, by my estimates, should be from 15 to 30%.

What is Apollo. It's a platform for sales and client acquisition departments with a database of people and companies, collected primarily from LinkedIn. The free tier provides access to search through the interface. I manually compiled a list of Sberbank HR and recruiters using filters: about 90 unique individuals. Of these, 70 are hiring managers: recruiters, HR, HR BPs, HR heads; 16 are L2 borderline: HR brand, juniors, administrators; 4 are misses (SAP HR consultants and other unexpected positions).

What the agents found: The overlap between what the agents found and what the service returned was tiny. Among those found by agents, only 3 out of 70 L1 Apollo individuals were found – 4.3%. Agents missed 67 hiring managers from Apollo. Conversely, 46 hiring managers found by agents are not in Apollo.

What this means. Agents and Apollo see different slices of the same phenomenon. Agents use Russian-language open sources – Habr, hh.ru, vc.ru, TAdviser, conference proceedings. Apollo builds a graph of people from LinkedIn. No single source is complete, and Apollo cannot be considered a benchmark of completeness: it's simply another source.

What I Ultimately Understood About Agentic Search

I started the experiment with the question: what is the minimum stack needed for a cheap open-source model to find specific information, provide a verifiable source, and not hallucinate by inventing a link?

Within the scope of this experiment, I managed to assemble such a stack: a search proxy, an agent with tools, evidence saving, and a separate verification step. But getting a list of people turned out to be much easier than proving that they fit the task and still work at the desired company.

1. Cheap Models Are Suitable for Candidate Collection. This Doesn't Mean They Solve the Entire Task

On a limited task with ten pre-known individuals, the model without additional skills found almost the entire benchmark. The main problem was not that it couldn't find the right people, but that it brought extraneous ones along with them.

A separate filter in this test proved more useful than complicating the search prompt: it preserved search recall and removed unsuitable candidates. This is a good argument for dividing the work into two steps – first collect, then verify.

However, this result cannot be extrapolated to any search task. Therefore, my conclusion is: not "cheap models replace expensive ones," but "cheap models can perform a useful part of the search process if the result is not accepted without verification."
