One phrase from the team lead in the prompt cut task estimates by a fifth

Estimating story points using neural networks seems like a quick way to speed up sprint planning. But just one phrase about how much the team lead thinks a task should cost is enough to change the estimates of two LLMs by about 20%. In an experiment with historical Jira tasks, we look at how the anchoring effect works in poker planning and why a sum close to the team's estimate doesn't necessarily mean an accurate assessment. Hello everyone, my name is Sergey Proshchaev, I am a Tech Lead and head of Java | Kotlin development at FinTech & E‑commerce, and I teach at the development and architecture courses at OTUS.

In this article, I will tell you about a small experiment: I asked two neural networks to independently estimate historical tasks from an open Jira dataset in a format similar to poker planning, and checked what happens to their estimates if one innocent phrase about the team lead's expectations is included in the prompt.

A familiar picture: before planning, someone pastes the backlog into a chat and asks to "estimate story points." The model responds in ten seconds, with a neat column of numbers by Fibonacci, without a shadow of a doubt. And in the prompt, in passing, it says: "the team lead thinks it's around 32 points.".

Spoiler: this phrase shifted the sum of estimates by about a fifth for both models. ChatGPT's sum even became closer to the team's estimate, and this is what alerted me the most.

Why this became a question now

Neural networks are already involved in planning, even if no one officially invited them. In a survey of practitioners published online on October 7, 2026, in the Journal of Systems and Software, 48.1% of 79 respondents using LLM assistants in Scrum say they do so daily or almost daily, and the main application is called backlog refinement. Among the problems, "almost correct answers" are in first place (65.8%).

Accuracy studies, meanwhile, are conflicting.

According to a preprint from the Rochester Institute of Technology (March 2026), in their experiments, untrained models predicted story points better than deep models trained on 80% of project data.

A comparative study in IEEE Access (March 2026) showed that without training, LLMs are inferior to specialized models, but their explanations influence humans: in a small experiment with 30 students, estimates were changed in more than 85% of cases, although they did not become more accurate because of it.

I was interested not in accuracy, but in something else.

Back in 2010, Magne Jørgensen and Torleif Halkjelsvik showed that developers give lower estimates if asked not "how long will it take," but "how much can we do in a week": the stated deadline becomes an anchor, and they hardly deviate from it. In 2025, this experiment was repeated on GPT-4, Gemini 1.5 Pro, and Llama 3.1.

The authors write that the models behave similarly to the anchoring effect: for GPT-4, the median estimate increased from 150 to 220 and 360 working hours with anchors of 30, 60, and 120 hours. I became interested in how this looks with regular story points.

Experiment

The tasks were taken from the open dataset by Choetkiertikul and colleagues: 23,313 Jira tasks from 16 open‑source projects with estimates made by the teams themselves.

I chose the Spring XD project as the closest to the Java audience and selected 12 tasks with a fixed seed:

  • two tasks for each estimate: 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, and 13;

  • only tasks with descriptions from 80 to 700 characters.

The sum of the team's estimates for these tasks is 64 SP.

There are two prompts, differing by one sentence:

(text, prompt A, fragment: full text in repository) You are participating in poker planning for a team developing Spring XD (a Java platform for stream and batch data processing). Estimate each task from the backlog in story points on a scale of 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21. Respond with exactly 12 lines of the format "number: estimate", without explanations. 1. Upgrade asciidoctor toolchain. This will in turn allow us to ... ... 12. Upgrade groovy version to 2.2.2. Currently, XD hast ...

In prompt B, after the scale, the phrase is added:

The team lead estimated in advance that the entire set would be around 32 story points.

This is exactly half of the sum of the team's estimates, meaning the anchor is significantly lower.

Models:

  • ChatGPT on the free plan (the interface does not show the model name).

  • Claude Opus 5.5 on claude.ai with High effort level.

Each prompt was run three times, each time in a new chat without account memory: for ChatGPT, a temporary chat; for Claude, incognito mode.

A total of 12 runs were performed on October 20, 2026. Task keys were not included in the prompt, only the title and description.

The team estimated these tasks at 64 SP, the anchor was 32. Neither model reached 32: the lowest run gave 44.

This is exactly the pattern Jørgensen described for humans: they move away from the anchor, but not enough. The ranges do not overlap: the worst run without an anchor is higher than the best run with an anchor.

For three repetitions, this is a strong signal, although the distribution of answers among them cannot be assessed.

As a manager, I am interested not in the magnitude, but in the direction: the sum moved following the hint. I would not recalculate it into tasks that "fit" into the sprint: story points are relative, and the set was assembled artificially.

Observation 2. Large tasks dropped the most

Tasks with estimates of 1 and 3 hardly moved for both models: the Groovy update received a 1 in all twelve runs.

But the large ones dropped noticeably:

  • For ChatGPT, the task about the automatic stream stop condition (team estimate: 13) became 8 in all three runs with the anchor.

  • The task about persistent metadata storage (team estimate: 8) became 5.

  • For Claude, in one of the runs with the anchor, four tasks were cut at once, three of them large, and the sum dropped to 44.

With 12 tasks, this is an observation, not a pattern. I did not measure task uncertainty separately, so it is too early to say that the anchor specifically presses on it.

Observation 3. With an anchor, the model becomes less consistent

Without an anchor, the models were surprisingly stable: out of 12 tasks, ChatGPT's estimate changed between runs in three cases, and Claude's in two.

With an anchor, ChatGPT's estimate changed in seven out of twelve cases, and Claude's in four. This is a rough measure and there are few runs, but the direction is the same for both models.

My hypothesis is that without a hint, the model relies only on the task text, but with a hint, it needs to "fit it in," and the missing points are distributed differently each time.

This instability is not visible in single runs.

Observation 4. Matching the sum doesn't mean matching the tasks

The most insidious part of the experiment.

ChatGPT's average sum with the anchor is 60.3 SP. This is much closer to the team's 64 than the 76.7 without the anchor. If you only look at the sprint total, the anchor "helped.".

But other metrics show a different picture:

  • The Mean Absolute Error per task (MAE based on the median of three runs versus the historical team estimate) increased from 2.92 to 3.25 SP.

  • The number of exact matches remained four out of twelve, as it was.

  • The rank correlation, meanwhile, increased from 0.67 to 0.73.

For Claude, it's the opposite: the sum with the anchor moved away from the team (54.0 vs. 66.7 with 64), but it got closer on a task-by-task basis.

  • MAE - 1.42 SP vs. 1.75.

  • Exact matches - seven vs. six.

  • Spearman rank correlation with the team - 0.90 vs. 0.82.

This means the anchor doesn't spoil the estimate, but subtly shifts it, and no one knows in advance exactly where.

Therefore, I would not judge the model's estimate based on a single sum: it smooths out errors of different signs.

Observation 5. Models agree with each other more than with the team

The rank correlation between ChatGPT and Claude without an anchor is 0.87. With the team, ChatGPT's correlation is 0.67, and Claude's is 0.82.

The models rank tasks similarly. I haven't investigated the reason, but my guess is that both only see the text, without team context.

The biggest discrepancies also hint at this.

The task "show the connection between streams and taps in the admin interface" was estimated by the team at 13, ChatGPT at 3, and Claude at 5. Based on the text, it's a list sorting in the interface and a shell list in the team's context.

An estimate of 13 suggests that the team saw more behind it, likely changes in the data model and API, which the text doesn't mention.

The task "add a processor for R" was estimated by the team at 8, while ChatGPT consistently gave 21 in all three runs without an anchor: the description contains the word "explore" and two libraries to choose from, and the model apparently assumed the worst-case scenario.

In such cases, I prefer the question not "who is right," but "what is the model missing?"

A discrepancy of two or more scale positions for me is a reason to check if the team has context that is not in the task description. The model can also be wrong, but finding out is cheaper than arguing about the number.

A story from an open source: "by what method do you assign a number?"

Liz Rivera, a program manager, kept a project diary in September 2026 where an AI agent acted as scrum master. Before estimating the third sprint, she asked the agent to assign story points and, before running it, asked another question: what method did it use to choose the number.

There was no method. The instruction was to "assign each task a number from 1 to 13," without criteria to distinguish a three from an eight.

Rivera redid the estimation using a general rubric where each task is measured by the same criteria.

Her conclusion resonates with me. In my translation, it sounds like this:

Automating work does not eliminate the need for expertise. It changes the question from "Can this be done?" to "Does the person directing the work know what a good result looks like?"

My experience shows the same problem from the other side: without a rubric, the model's reference becomes the number that happened to be nearby.

How I would integrate the model into planning

The order I would suggest after this experiment is shown in Fig. 3.

The main idea of the diagram: the team's estimate goes into the plan, and the model acts as a detector of unclear tasks.

In practice, this means five rules:

  1. Do not write the sprint capacity, the team lead's expectation, or "the previous similar task was 5" in the prompt: any number in the prompt can become an anchor.

  2. Show the model's estimate to the team only after their own vote, otherwise the model itself becomes the anchor.

  3. Run the model at least three times and look at the spread: if the estimate jumps, check the description and assumptions.

  4. Compare with the team based on tasks and rank, not on the sum.

  5. Consider a discrepancy of two or more scale positions as a reason to check the description. The threshold is working, not measured.

Limitations

The sample size is small: 12 tasks, one project, two models, three runs each.

The dataset is public since 2016 and might have been included in the models' training data. Story points are unique to each team, so "error against the team" here means a deviation from the specific Spring XD team, not objective truth.

Phrase B contains both a number and the team lead's opinion; the experiment does not separate their contributions. Generation parameters in chats cannot be fixed, and the free ChatGPT model may change any day.

This is not a model benchmark, but a test of one working technique: what one phrase in the prompt does to the estimate.

Conclusion

A neural network in poker planning behaves like a very confident participant who hasn't seen the code. Without hints, it is consistent and sometimes close to the team. But one phrase from the team lead in the prompt shifted its sum by a fifth, and the estimates for individual tasks became more divergent between runs.

You can test this yourself in twenty minutes. Take a dozen tasks from a completed sprint with known team estimates and run them through the model three times without numbers in the prompt and with your sprint capacity. Compare not only the sums but also the individual tasks. If the variations are greater than the repetitions of a single variant, the number in the prompt affects the model, and it's best to keep it away from the initial estimate.
