Evolution of Infrastructure

Over the past ten years, AI infrastructure has undergone significant transformations:

  • Until 2019, everything was built on bare metal and virtual machines. Consequently, dedicated GPU servers were required, manual CUDA driver configuration, and static resource allocation was assumed. To scale, physical equipment had to be purchased — and it took weeks from request to a ready environment.

  • From 2020, containers and early Kubernetes arrived. The GPU device plugin, NVIDIA GPU Operator, and the first orchestration attempts appeared. But scheduling was still largely configured manually, and debugging GPU tasks remained painful.

But today, the era of Cloud-Native AI is effectively beginning, which involves using managed clusters with out-of-the-box GPUs, auto-scaling of GPU nodes, card-sharing technologies — MIG, vGPU, time-slicing, S3-CSI for huge datasets. Essentially — Zero-Ops for ML platforms. And the transition to a new type of AI infrastructure has specific prerequisites. Let's elaborate on them.

Nuances of Traditional AI Infrastructure

The classic approach to GPU infrastructure was created in an era of predictable HPC tasks with fixed resources. However, in the context of modern AI workloads, certain drawbacks of the familiar model become apparent:

  • Slow Time-to-GPU. Using traditional infrastructure means weeks of waiting for equipment for each new project. For AI, where experiment speed is critical, this is fatal.

  • Wasteful GPU Utilization. Without resource sharing capabilities, expensive cards sit idle up to 70% of the time, which is inefficient and does not allow for the full potential of the available equipment to be utilized.

  • Fragmented Tool Stack. Training and inference are often connected by fragile manual integrations, which can be critical and affect the accuracy of the neural network's operation.

That is, by using traditional infrastructure, one can face the risk of overpaying 3-5 times due to equipment downtime. As a result, working with AI becomes unjustifiably expensive.

But that's not all. The situation is somewhat exacerbated by the fact that AI workloads are fundamentally different.

  • Resource consumption is unstable — batch training and inference spikes instead of smooth, predictable traffic.

  • GPUs cannot be permanently assigned to one machine — it's too expensive.

  • Experiment speed requires infrastructure to be brought up and down in minutes, not weeks.

  • There is fierce competition for resources, so you need to be able to share one GPU's power among multiple teams. The classic "one machine — one server" model is simply not designed for this.

From this, it follows that effective work with AI is impossible on old architectures, as dynamic workloads require a dynamic foundation. And there is already a solution on the market for these requirements.

New Standard for AI

According to the annual "CNCF Annual Cloud Native Survey The infrastructure of AI’s future", 66% of organizations today run AI workloads on Kubernetes. Moreover:

  • 23% host all their inference workloads in K8s;

  • 43% — a portion of tasks.

This means two-thirds of the market already uses k8s when working with neural networks.

Notably, a significantly smaller minority holds a different position:

  • 18% do not use Kubernetes for inference and do not plan to;

  • 12% do not use K8s at all;

  • only 4% chose another solution.

Thus, we can state that the trend is obvious and unidirectional.

The choice in favor of Kubernetes is largely determined by three fundamental reasons:

  • Isolation. A container is natively isolated, reproducible, and portable. It is an ideal environment for independent ML experiments: launch, test, shut down.

  • Hardware Demands. Classic virtual machines are slow for heavy models. Kubernetes allows you to spin up bare-metal nodes at the touch of a button, with maximum GPU and network power.

  • The Standard Reinforces Itself. The entire ecosystem of modern AI tools — Kubeflow, KServe, Ray — is created directly for Kubernetes. That is, by choosing K8s, you get direct access to this entire ecosystem.

Moreover, Kubernetes simultaneously solves two problems that the market has encountered: it minimizes equipment downtime and allows for deployments in minutes. Thus, k8s:

  • breaks down workloads into smaller pieces than virtual machines, and also effectively divides and utilizes GPUs;

  • allows starting up and deploying in minutes, not weeks;

  • guarantees portability — a manifest works anywhere, reproducibly, without vendor lock-in.

GPU Access Options

One of the advantages of working with Kubernetes is that you can interact with GPUs at different infrastructure layers depending on the task. For example:

  • IaaS — virtual machines with GPUs. The simplest start and good isolation, sharing via vGPU and MIG. Suitable when you need to start quickly.

  • Kubernetes — GPU nodes in a cluster. Graphics cards as a pool with auto-scaling, orchestration of training and inference in a single environment. Optimal when ML orchestration is needed.

  • Bare Metal — direct access to hardware. The entire physical server, without virtualization, all power for heavy tasks. An option for scenarios requiring maximum performance.

For example, in the case of Bare Metal, a physical worker can be connected to a managed cluster as a regular node via API, UI, or Infrastructure-as-Code on demand within minutes, while the Control Plane remains with the cloud vendor.

Moreover, creation, updates, and monitoring remain fully managed — you get the power of bare metal without losing the convenience of the cloud.

Note: Choosing Bare Metal over virtualization is often justified, as for training large models and working with large amounts of data, every percentage of power counts. And it is in the case of bare metal that the worker interacts directly with the hardware. There is no hypervisor overhead, local NVMe drives, 100% GPU and network. When working with ultra-heavy models, this can significantly speed up training.

Barriers to Transitioning to Kubernetes

It must be admitted that Kubernetes remains too complex.

Thus, in the aforementioned "CNCF Annual Cloud Native Survey The infrastructure of AI’s future," respondents ranked not technologies, but engineering culture as the top difficulty for 2025: for 47%, the main problem was changes in team workflow. Along with this are a lack of training, security, CI/CD, monitoring, and the complexity of administration itself.

In practice, this looks like this: a team can deploy a cluster, but then the most expensive and complex part begins — updates, monitoring, scaling, incident resolution, network settings, security control, working with logs, integration with the service ecosystem.

This is precisely why, for a significant portion of users, Kubernetes as a technology is no longer valuable in itself. The value becomes the ability to use Kubernetes as a ready-made platform without taking on all the infrastructural complexity. And the solution in this situation is Zero-Ops.

About Zero-Ops and Management Evolution

Zero-Ops is an approach where the cloud takes over Kubernetes routine tasks and eliminates hidden costs of maintaining complex infrastructure. The user works with applications and models, not with the cluster's internals. Moreover, Zero-Ops fundamentally changes approaches to working with K8s infrastructure:

  • The traditional approach involves manual updates of each cluster, reactive incident handling and manual troubleshooting, creating tickets with internal IT for every change, and manual monitoring setup for each team. Given this volume of tasks, one engineer can only maintain 3-5 clusters.

  • Zero-Ops is based on the transition to more advanced practices: automatic rolling updates and self-updates, self-healing without human intervention, self-service via API, UI, and IaC, built-in observability and security policies for all clusters at once. Thanks to this, one engineer can maintain fifty or more clusters, meaning team efficiency increases tenfold.

Moreover, each company implements Zero-Ops in its own way, of course. For example, at VK Cloud, Zero-Ops is built on four principles:

  • Self-Healing — automatic recovery of nodes and control plane without an engineer;

  • Self-Service — cluster creation via UI, API, and IaC without tickets and waiting;

  • Policy-as-Code — automatic security policies;

  • built-in observability — Prometheus and Grafana out-of-the-box, without manual configuration.

Additionally, VK Cloud promotes other best practices, including:

  • GPU-as-a-Service — a card is available in minutes, not weeks. This resource should be easily accessible just like RAM and CPU for the team, with MIG, vGPU, and time-slicing on demand.

  • Unified Infrastructure — a single train/serve/data environment, S3-CSI for petabyte datasets, built-in ML stack from add-ons.

Thus, GPU nodes, GPU Operator, MIG, S3-CSI, pay-as-you-go, and multi-GPU are already working today. In the near future plans are DRA, vGPU, and bare-metal GPU. On the horizon are multi-cluster GPU federation, serverless inference, and AutoML.

What's the Result

It is quite reasonable to assume that the shift in approaches to AI infrastructure is irreversible, because despite all the advantages of dedicated servers, physical equipment simply cannot provide the elasticity, speed of resource redistribution, and density of GPU utilization that the modern neural network workflow requires.

And practice shows that Kubernetes is a quite effective alternative to traditional AI infrastructure: it breaks down workloads, divides GPUs among teams, and combines training and inference into a single manageable environment.

The benefit is especially noticeable when working with Kubernetes in the cloud, where the team can focus directly on models, data, and business hypotheses, delegating operational routines to the vendor.
