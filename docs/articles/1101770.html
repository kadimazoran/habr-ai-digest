Self-learning in single-celled organisms and collective intelligence

Learning is usually associated with neural activity. However, evidence suggests that even single-celled organisms possess primitive forms of memory and learning. This article explores the mechanisms behind this phenomenon, drawing parallels with artificial neural networks and discussing the implications for our understanding of intelligence.

Learning, it would seem, is a process directly related to neural activity. It is no coincidence that the computational unit in a perceptron and self-learning networks is called a "neuron". The two-part work "Neural Networks for Beginners," which discussed the relationship between artificial and natural neurons, was written on Habr in 2016 and 2017 by the esteemed @Arnis71 (the first part has over 1.72 million views – perhaps not a Habr record, but look at how many bookmarks!). However, learning and assimilation are functions that can be performed not only by neurons in humans but also, for example, by astrocytes. I previously discussed these remarkable brain cells on Habr in the article "Stars in Our Heads: The Role of Astrocytes in Neural Networks." Today, I want to touch upon an even more astonishing topic – the learning mechanisms in single-celled organisms.

In the understanding of classical neurophysiology, memory is structured mechanistically and operates on the principle formulated in Hebb's theory: "neurons that fire together, wire together." This refers to molecular biochemical processes that ensure long-term potentiation (LTP) – the strengthening of synaptic connections. This phenomenon is interestingly described from a computer science perspective in the article by the esteemed Andrey Belkin @aigame, "Reverse Engineering Biological Memory Mechanisms." Hebb's theory helps understand how memory is implemented in neural tissue but doesn't explain what memory is. Can it only arise in neural tissue, or will analogous capabilities form on any medium, cellular or digital, as soon as that medium overcomes a certain threshold of computational complexity? It is precisely such a "quantitative leap into qualitative change" that is still anticipated in the design of potential AGI (Artificial General Intelligence) and the development of large language models, but this topic is beyond the scope of this article.

According to the concept of computational functionalism, cognitive abilities emerge as an emergent phenomenon and then accumulate. It is therefore all the more interesting that by now, there are increasingly more examples showing that primitive memory ("experience consolidation") is available to some protozoa, which by definition have neither neurons nor synapses. Not only do they lack and cannot develop nervous tissue, but they are also unicellular by definition, whereas nervous tissue is a network. That is, it's not just tissue, but a highly intertwined tissue. Nerve cells do not divide because for a cell to divide, it would have to shed all its synapses, and the new cell would have to rapidly grow the necessary set of synapses, i.e., "weave itself" into the right place. Any manifestation of memory in unicellular organisms (we will examine examples of such phenomena below) must mean that primitive memory is possible not in tissue with "cell-to-cell" information transfer, but within a lower-level subcellular cytoarchitecture.

This is the ciliate Stentor roeseli, which attracted the attention of American biologist Herbert Jennings as early as 1906. Jennings noted that in response to repeated stimuli, the ciliate would try various ways to escape the experimenter without repeating itself – as if "remembering" which trick didn't work.

Jennings' experiment was as follows: he collected these unicellular organisms from a pond, then added a drop of irritating red pigment to their dish with a glass pipette. It turned out that upon encountering the unpleasant substance, the ciliates first tried to evade the pipette, then to "push" the dye away with their cilia, and if that didn't help, they would "bunch up," contracting their cell surface, and then scatter. The strange ciliate was remembered in 1967, when an attempt to repeat Jennings' experiment failed – but perhaps they simply chose the wrong protozoan for study. Only in 2019 did a group of researchers (led by Joseph Dexter) from Dartmouth College and Harvard Medical School repeat these experiments, subjecting the trumpet ciliates to increasingly pronounced irritating stimuli. The ciliate evaded these stimuli more and more inventively; the most logical conclusion was that it was "taking acquired experience into account."

Biochemical Memory

Earlier, in the article "Bacterial Logic Gates and Ribosomal Memory," I discussed how logic gates can be implemented at the level of biochemical reactions within a bacterial cell. Evolution has become adept at using this mechanism: for instance, during normal metabolism, a cell can turn a specific protein on or off by attaching or detaching a phosphate group to the protein molecule. This doesn't yet allow us to speak of "memory" and "data storage" in a pure sense, but it does allow us to talk about stimulus reinforcement based on repeated exposure. In 2024, this phenomenon was studied by scientists from Harvard Medical School (the group included Jeremy Gunawardena, who participated in the aforementioned experiment with trumpet ciliates). The scientists conducted some reactions in Petri dishes and simulated others on a computer.

They were particularly interested in two molecular processes: negative feedback loops and feedforward loops. In negative feedback, the product of a process, as it accumulates, inhibits the process itself. For example, a thermostat can turn off a heater once the room becomes too hot. In the latter case, a signal triggers both the process and its inhibitor. For example, a light turns on with motion, but after detecting motion, it will turn off for a period set by a timer.

Simulations showed that unicellular organisms learn to respond to stimuli by combining at least the two aforementioned mechanisms. Therefore, some unicellular organisms are literally trainable. These experiments demonstrated that the cell maintains a separation of time scales in the operation of its molecular circuits, allowing some biochemical chains to work much faster than others. This enables slower chains to operate in a non-decaying mode.

Evolutionary Rationale

It is logical that natural selection should favor any survival-promoting strategies, so the ability to "learn" must be older than neural activity. In a multicellular organism, immune cells are most similar to populations of migrating single-celled organisms that must adapt to new "habitats" (organs and tissues). The existence of such mechanisms was demonstrated in 2022 by a group led by Aneta Kosicki from the Max Planck Institute. A more well-known model organism on Habr illustrating self-learning in foraging is the slime mold, which was discussed on Habr back in 2021 by the esteemed @ancotir. Subsequently, the esteemed Arthur Krasny @ArthurEx wrote on Habr about how a slime mold (Physarum) drew a map of the Tokyo subway in a petri dish with agar-agar, approaching scattered oat flakes and avoiding excessively illuminated areas. The slime mold's navigation is purely biochemical and resembles the ant algorithm: the organism leaves chemical trails where it has grown. The richer the trail, the more active the movement in that direction. The organism compares the gradient of its signaling substances from one pass to the next and prefers more favorable paths. Just last year, a study was published showing that slime mold solves the traveling salesman problem, and about a month ago, the esteemed @inkedsymon wrote on Habr about how the traveling salesman problem is solved by a colony of E. coli.

Here we come to an interesting distinction between the trumpet ciliate and bacterial colonies, namely, in their approach to acquiring new information. While the trumpet ciliate is trainable almost like Pavlov's dogs, bacteria and immune cells operate with a collective memory inherent not to a single cell but to the entire colony. Nevertheless, in both cases, we are dealing with full-fledged learning and experience accumulation within a single generation of cells, not evolution.

Intermittent Fasting or Nutrient Concentration Tracking

E. coli is convenient for such experiments because we not only have an excellent understanding of its habitat (the human gut) but also know how to regulate nutrient content and thin out the population with antibiotics. The chemical conditions in the gut change very rapidly, while temperature changes more slowly, but across the entire gut. Therefore, to survive, bacteria must react quickly to events while simultaneously assimilating signs of impending environmental changes.

This setting can be studied within the framework of computational biophysics, which is what Shiladitya Banerjee from the University of Georgia has attempted. His research interests lie in information processing in living systems and the adaptation of cells to environmental changes. The technological basis for such experiments has already been developed within microfluidics, a guest post about which was once published in my blog by the esteemed Anastasia Lazukina @anastasiamrr. Microfluidics works with such weak currents and such miniature (but precisely calibrated) dosages of substances that it allows, for example, studying bacterial responses to electricity. Banerjee's team has designed a device that allows tracking the behavior of thousands of E. coli individuals when the experimenter varies the frequency and magnitude of nutrient inflow.

The experiment showed that bacteria can accumulate experience about "hungry" and "satiated" periods. Populations subjected to such stress responded to the resumption of food supply faster and more actively than "inexperienced" strains. This means bacteria store "knowledge" about past events somewhere.

Where Exactly Can Single-Celled Organisms Store Information?

To answer this question and correlate such a learning mechanism in a bacterial colony with known artificial neural networks, the group led by Banerjee created a mathematical model of the biochemical interactions that determine the role of bacteria. Since the colony accumulates experience, it was necessary to determine where, if not in neurons and synapses, bacteria store information.

Apparently, ribosomes – the cell's protein factories – can serve this purpose. The cell acts as an information receiver, accumulating data about its environment, and the ribosome, based on this information, regulates how fast the cell grows and which specific proteins/enzymes are synthesized under particular conditions.

This picture is further supported by the fact that some ribosomes react faster to changes, while others react more slowly. Their function can be compared, respectively, to short-term and long-term memory. Since there are many ribosomes in a cell, this mechanism is redundant, and "memories" can be retained by the bacterium from several minutes to several hours.

Bacterial Colony with Recurrent Gated Units

Such a mathematical model allows us to equate the molecular machinery in a bacterial cell with well-known recurrent neural networks with gated units. Networks of this type are good for processing streaming data, such as speech or sensor readings.

From a computer science perspective, such a gated unit is similar to a gate. As I mentioned in the aforementioned article on bacterial logic gates, simple biochemistry can implement molecular switches that determine the importance of certain information, i.e., decide whether to store the received data or overwrite it with new, more relevant (environmental conditions have changed) or more survival-critical information. If a bacterium expends energy on adapting to its environment, it has less energy for growth and reproduction. To maintain this balance within the colony, mechanisms are used that, within the context of this narrative, I am willing to equate to learning.

Conclusion

Here I will refer to the article "Learning to Be: how learning strengthens the emergent nature of collective intelligence in a minimal agent" by Michael Levin, a distinguished microbiologist from Tufts University. In this text, he compares the actions of bacteria to the behavior of a minimal intelligent agent and, it seems to me, questions the origin of the collective adaptation of bacterial colonies to their environment: is it learning, or simply a response to "streaming data" affecting overall survival? However, the examples of "Pavlovian" adaptation found in trumpet ciliates, as well as the clear similarity between the mechanisms of bacterial adaptation and the architecture of some neural networks, lead me to believe that the experiments discussed allow us to imagine what intelligence without neurons and synapses might look like. It is difficult to draw a line between biochemical responses to stimuli and information processing, but if a neural network can learn and use information by operating on the principles of a bacterial colony, then it can likely form as a gel/suspension, not just as tissue, and operate with or without ant algorithms. Nevertheless, perhaps by the early 2020s, bioinformaticians had discovered a purely biochemical level of memory and learning that inevitably arises from the dynamic complexity of living systems.
