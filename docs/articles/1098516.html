Recently, I needed to make a change to a project that had no SDD framework at all. Absolutely none, Carl! No proposal, no design, no spec deltas, not even a humble tasks.md in the corner. I mentally prepared for a long agent onboarding: it would spend an hour wandering through the code, getting confused in modules, and asking where the entry point was. But the model simply understood the code, found the places for future intervention, made the change, and it smoothly passed human review by the code owner. Without a single spec. "Wait, you can do that?"

After that, I looked at my working project with OpenSpec, at the openspec/ folder with its two hundred files, and asked myself a reasonable question: why do we generate, store, and commit a bunch of artifacts to git that a modern model doesn't need? Who are we writing these papers for if the main reader gets by perfectly fine without them?

From this question grew SlimSpec, a plugin for Claude Code. It takes what works from SDD and throws away the rest. On a real project, a comparable change cost it 29.7M tokens versus 45.7M for OpenSpec, and not a single line of process files ended up in git. Next, I'll explain the idea, how the flow works, show the numbers, and what had to be paid. Spoiler: there was a price to pay; there are no free lunches, even in the world of LLMs.

Why Was SDD Needed at All

Until recently, models were weaker, context was small, and sub-agents didn't exist. We worked like this: in one session, you'd create a detailed plan, and in the next, you'd implement it. The model constantly lost context, like a goldfish that, on its third lap around the aquarium, is reacquainting itself with the castle. And project memory had to be stored somewhere, otherwise, each session would start with "hello, I'm a new junior, tell me everything from the beginning."

Spec-driven development solved exactly these pain points: small context, lack of sub-agents, the need for a detailed plan. You'd write everything down on paper, put the paper in the repository, and the next session would read the paper. Reliable, like a Swiss watch. That's how it became the standard.

Around the same time, two main frameworks, in my opinion, emerged: OpenSpec and Superpowers. I worked with both, and OpenSpec became my primary one. What I valued them for:

  • the explore mode (in Superpowers, it's called brainstorming), where the design takes shape before the plan. This is by far the best thing that happened to AI development: think first, then write code, not the other way around;

  • the plan and specifications themselves;

  • OpenSpec also has spec merging: only the important parts remain in the live spec. Superpowers completely lacks this and just accumulates design files like a grandmother hoarding plastic bags under the sink. It's a shame to throw them away, but you can't find the right one anymore.

In the SDD flow, as the community envisioned it, all this fit perfectly.

Where OpenSpec Started to Lag

In pet projects, I used OpenSpec with "Chinese" models for a long time and saw no problems. Quite the opposite: code became easier to write and maintain, semantic duplicates disappeared (you know, when you suddenly find three date formatting functions in a project), the agent did what was needed more often and did it more correctly.

On a commercial project, our team also chose OpenSpec, and at first, I was completely confident in this. After a couple of months, it became clear that the flow was very slow. Especially after we moved away from manual review. The subscription was corporate, no need to spare limits, we were firing away in multiple terminal windows on different features, but the process still dragged on like a queue at the public services office for a certificate stating you have a certificate.

What was slowing things down:

  • After the proposal, there were four more artifacts: design, tasks, spec deltas, and so on. We practically didn't read them. But they were diligently written, committed, and sent to the archive. Without a paper, you're a nobody, but with a paper... well, you're still a nobody, just with a paper.

  • Any change in an unarchived change resulted in another change. Changed your mind along the way? Be so kind as to fill out the form.

  • A rebase is mandatory before archiving. And you also have to ask the agent to check the spec merge "by hand," because someone else might have interfered and "corrupted" the section where the MODIFIED block fits.

My personal record of bureaucracy looks like this. I needed to remove a feature: an unnecessary piece of the screen. A deletion task, what could be simpler? Delete code, delete tests, close the ticket. The final diff:

Almost 5.5 thousand lines were removed, and almost as many were added. The added lines, of course, were specs: a proposal on why we're deleting, a design on how we're deleting, tasks on the order of deletion, and REMOVED deltas on what exactly was deleted. To throw away 5 thousand lines, I had to write 5 thousand lines. It's like taking an old sofa to the dump, but first filling out its technical passport, a receipt, and an inventory of scratches.

And then came that project without a framework. And the puzzle pieces clicked into place.

The Main Session Is the Design

I delegate all the mechanics to sub-agents long ago. My main session has turned into a conversation: I brainstorm a feature with the agent, and the design and plan are born right there. Deviations from the plan are recorded there; no new changes are needed. If a refinement grows, the agent itself suggests moving it out of scope, into non-goals. No applications, no forms. You say "let's do it differently," and it becomes different.

So, the source of knowledge about the feature is already there, it's the session itself. Files are only needed in case the session is lost or gets chewed up by compaction. Like a parachute: it's good to have, but you don't have to jump with it every day. Hence "SDD in reverse": the spec doesn't lead the work, but the work leaves a spec behind at the end.

I decided to build my framework around this idea. Take the best from predecessors and make it lighter, faster, and cheaper. The classic "I made it from what I had," but with TDD.

How SlimSpec Works

Inside, there are only skills and agent descriptions for Claude Code. No scripts, hooks, or CLI, so there's not much to break. It's installed and launched like this:

/plugin marketplace add yushman/slimspec
/plugin install slimspec@slimspec

/slimspec <idea>

The entire cycle in one picture:

It all starts with explore. A scanner sub-agent reads module specs, then code, and provides a brief summary: what's here, where things are located, what might break. Then the main session grills me: one question per decision, independent questions come in a batch. It feels like a job interview, except your own idea is being interviewed, and it's useful: half of the "obvious" solutions turn out not to be so obvious on the second question. The result goes into explore.md: goal, non-goals, decisions with rejected alternatives, risks. There are no separate proposals and designs; explore is the change spec. The session doesn't move to the plan on its own; I decide that.

In plan, the planner breaks down the work into tasks.md by waves. A wave is divided into lanes of related tasks. Lanes within a wave touch non-overlapping files, so they run in parallel. You eat an elephant in pieces, but if there are multiple pieces and different plates, you can eat them simultaneously. Each task immediately has the first failing test recorded.

apply is the shortest: an implementer is assigned to a lane, they go through the tasks in order, and for each, they produce red and green. After the wave, the main session runs the entire test suite to ensure that two parallel lanes don't conflict somewhere at the junction.

verify is more interesting. The reviewer compares the result with the plan and design and checks that red failed for a good reason, not due to a typo or a forgotten import. A test that fails with ModuleNotFoundError is not red; it's just red. Code review is handed over to the built-in /code-review. Bugs in the code automatically go back to apply. Questions about the design come to me via refine: it corrects explore.md and returns only invalidated tasks to apply. "Galya, we have a cancellation, but only for three items; the rest go through." If two automatic correction rounds don't help, the session calls me.

More on ground below; it's the most important part.

There are two stops: after explore and after plan. At each stop, the session shows a detailed breakdown. After explore, it's the design with "before/after" diagrams and links to path:line; after plan, all waves, lanes, and tests for each task. The rest runs automatically and only bothers me with questions. You can calmly go get coffee. You can even get a second one.

Sub-agents Don't Talk to the User

Decisions are made by the main session. Sub-agents work on the principle of "every cricket knows its hearth": they do their part and report back. The report is short and structured, without logs and diffs:

status: DONE | BLOCKED | FAILED
task: T3
files: [src/auth/token.ts, src/auth/token.test.ts]
decisions: ["used existing clock abstraction"]
deviations: ["renamed validate → verifyToken"]
tdd:
  red: "token.test.ts: rejects expired token — FAIL (expected 401, got 200)"
  green: "12 passed"
questions: []

If it hits an open question, it stops with the status BLOCKED. It doesn't improvise or "figure it out itself." The main session asks me and restarts the agent with the answer. The main session's context remains clean, without pages of test logs, but it contains every decision and every outcome.

TDD and Review Are Built into the Flow

In my opinion, TDD and code review have already become standard, so let them live in the framework rather than in someone's discipline. Discipline, as is well known, ends on Friday evening. TDD is verified not by a hook, but by evidence: a task without a red run won't be accepted by verify. "You shall not pass!" (c) Gandalf, part-time reviewer. Where TDD is not applicable (config, styles, migrations), the task is marked test: none — <reason> with an alternative check, and all such exceptions are visible at the plan gate. You can't hide "oh, tests aren't needed here" in the middle of the list.

Two Temporary Files

The change lives in .slimspec/changes/<slug>/, where explore.md and tasks.md are located. SlimSpec itself adds the folder to .gitignore and deletes it after ground. The squad didn't notice the loss of a fighter, and rightly so: everything useful from the fighter has already moved to the specs by this point. Commits and updated specs store the history. Lost the session? /slimspec <slug> will continue from where you left off.

Grounding: The Only Thing Left from SDD

The final stage, ground, is perhaps the only thing that connects SlimSpec to classic SDD.

Long-term knowledge is distilled from the change: behavior, invariants, contracts, decisions. The plan and process are discarded. Who cares in six months that task T3 was in the second wave? Exactly, nobody. This then flows into the module's spec, and here's the main difference from OpenSpec. Merging is not done by ADDED/MODIFIED/REMOVED blocks. It's done by an agent who understands the reasons, structure, and design of the change. It rewrites a contradictory statement instead of adding to it. Otherwise, the spec quickly turns into "it was like this, but then it became like this, although see point 7." Then the agent checks that the updated spec covers the goal of the change and doesn't conflict with accepted decisions.

The result goes into the nested CLAUDE.md of the required module. Claude Code automatically loads such files when working in that directory, so the spec gets into context without extra steps and exactly where it's needed. One spec per module instead of a scattering of files across capabilities. No need to remember which spec to provide to the agent: it enters the module, and the spec is already there.

The blocker found by the reviewer was a panic on edge dates. The very error that appears in production exactly on December 31st at 23:59. In the OpenSpec run, there was no review at all.

It's the same for small changes. Two changes on OpenSpec processed 31.5M and 45.7M tokens, two on SlimSpec (fast and medium) processed 6.8M and 12.5M. One implementer in OpenSpec alone consumed 13–16M tokens, while a SlimSpec sub-agent consumed 0–1M: each gets a narrow lane and doesn't need to read the whole world to change one function.

Now for the caveats, which are better to state yourself before others do in the comments. n = 1–2 per approach, one project. The statistics here are like the anecdote about the average temperature in a hospital. Small changes on SlimSpec involved 5–10 times less code, so part of the difference is simply due to size. Some SlimSpec agents ran on Sonnet or Haiku. And the main session's full run carried extraneous context from earlier work, which, conversely, inflated its score. So, the numbers are not a benchmark, but an observation. However, it's a rather loud observation.

What Didn't Make It Along the Way

It looked good on paper. I initially did some things differently and then redid them.

Implementer for each task. It looked nice: a fresh agent, narrow context, cleanliness, and order. In reality, each cold start re-read explore.md, specs, and code. As many tasks as there were, that many times "hello, what's this project you have here?". Now, one implementer handles a lane of related tasks.

Worktree for isolation in full mode. Didn't work because the sub-agent's worktree branches off from the committed state and doesn't see uncommitted results from previous waves. The second wave honestly built the house without the first floor. I would have had to commit every wave. I kept parallelism for non-overlapping files within a single working tree.

I wanted a hook that prevents editing code without a failing test. Sounds strict and reliable. But a prompt hook cannot verify that a red run actually happened, and plugin hooks are always enabled, regardless of mode. A guard who can't check passes but stands at every door. The check has been moved to verify.

The setup turned into a funny story: first, I wrote a bash wizard. With menus, with questions, everything like normal people. Then it turned out that the skill couldn't run an interactive script, and ! in the session works without TTY. The wizard turned out beautiful, but it couldn't be launched. Now, /slimspec:init simply asks questions directly in the session.

And a minor thing that was annoying every day: in explore, questions came strictly one by one. You wait for a round for each decision, even when all questions are known in advance. It feels like customer support: "your question is very important to us, please stay on the line." Now, independent ones come in batches, up to four at a time.

What Had to Be Paid For

The spoons were found, but the bad taste remained. Here's what was lost along with the extra artifacts:

  • Scenarios as a ready-made list of test cases are no longer available.

  • Justifications for individual changes are partially lost. Some remain in ADR, the rest only in git history. If you enjoy archaeology via git log, you'll like it.

  • The largest module spec (~14 KB) is loaded entirely for any work in that module.

  • Works only with Claude Code. OpenSpec supports 30+ AI tools, Superpowers also works with many agents and can do a lot besides specs: debugging, worktree, review. SlimSpec only covers the spec-driven cycle and doesn't pretend to be a Swiss Army knife.

Project Setup

Configuration is optional; every key has a default. Don't want to configure anything? Don't, everything will work out of the box. /slimspec:init will ask everything in the session and write .slimspec/config.yaml:

mode: medium              # fast | medium | full
spec:
  format: claude-md       # claude-md | readme | central | native | none
  unit: auto              # auto (by build files) | glob of directories, e.g., "src/*/"
  adr: false              # additionally keep decisions in docs/adr/NNNN-*.md
test: auto                # or explicit command
commit: none              # none | lane | wave
apply:
  agents: 3               # max parallel implementers
review:
  depth: auto             # auto (by mode) | low | medium | high
models: {}                # models by role: scanner, planner, implementer, reviewer, grounder

A few words about what's not obvious here.

mode sets the cycle's weight. In fast, explore and plan live in the same file, no more than three questions, one implementer, and low-depth review. This is for "changing button text and not going crazy." medium adds a scanner, grilling, planner, and parallel lanes, with medium-depth review. full adds an explicit gate on explore.md and high-depth review for changes that require sleeping soundly afterward. You can skip choosing altogether: after scanning, /slimspec will suggest a mode based on the change size.

spec.format decides where specs live. By default, they are nested CLAUDE.md files. You can use README.md next to the code, a shared specs/ folder for cross-module features, native language docs (Go doc.go, Rust //!, Python docstring, KDoc), or none, in which case ground is skipped. For those who live by the principle "code is documentation.".

spec.unit specifies what counts as a module; auto finds boundaries by package.json, go.mod, Cargo.toml, build.gradle(.kts), pyproject.toml.

commit enables committing after each lane or wave. Only the main session commits; agents never do. SlimSpec cannot push in principle, and this is a feature: no surprises in main at three in the morning.

models assigns models to roles. By default, the scanner and implementer use Sonnet, while the planner, reviewer, and grounder use Opus. Thinking is expensive, doing is cheaper; that's the whole economy.

If the project already uses OpenSpec or Superpowers, there are /slimspec:migrate-openspec and /slimspec:migrate-superpowers. Only specs are migrated: each is linked to modules, compressed, and compared with the code. Where the code diverges from the spec, you decide, because sometimes the spec is wrong, and sometimes the code is, and you can't guess here. Old files are deleted only after your confirmation.

Instead of a Conclusion

Powerful models don't need heavy specs. It's enough to hint at what to do and where to look; they'll reconstruct the rest from the code faster than they'll read four artifacts. Tokens spent on a proposal that no one will open are better spent on the next feature. Even better, on a new project. A million tokens here, a million there, and in the end, it adds up to another pet project that's been on the "someday" list for a long time.

If you've also hit a bottleneck with a slow SDD flow, try SlimSpec on a single change and compare it with your current process. It's especially interesting to see how it behaves in a team and on projects larger than mine. Write in the comments or in issues; you can criticize too, just with numbers.
