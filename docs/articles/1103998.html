In the theory of LLM chats as Luhmannian semantic systems of a new type (Krupkin, 2026), user interaction is mediated by a coupled social system, which implies that LLM/noo-systems chats can have a social role scheme of Ego-Alter communication, where Ego is the noo-system's role mask, how it appears to the user, and Alter is the user model in the LLM, how the LLM chat perceives the user.

A search for scientific sources yields quite a few experimental LLM studies that can be linked to the Ego-Alter scheme. In particular, the fresh Habr review on the influence of even one or several LLM neurons on the noo-system's Ego concerns Ego models of LLM chats. In this review of experiments found in the literature, I will limit myself to describing the LLM chat's interlocutor – the chat user – and its modeling of the interacting Alter.

What are we looking for?: User Model (Alter) in LLM Chats

The interlocutor model is the LLM chat's representation of its social interaction partner. From general considerations, data such as age, education, state, intentions, beliefs, and the user's desired self-image should be included here in some way. Like other forms and structures for shaping the conversation flow, the Alter model is included as a special component in the container – the set of all changeable numbers associated with the chat conversation in this LLM chat. The numbers defining Alter are somehow calculated from user messages and influence the subsequent flow of the LLM chat.

Experience with LLMs generally indicates the existence of user Alter models within them: LLMs behave differently with different users. This raises a research question not only about what Alter consists of, but also about the details of its impact on the internal processes of noo-systems. Thus, a standard questionnaire for experimental research arises:

1. Is the studied component of Alter (form) revealed from LLM activations?

2. Does Alter influence what follows? In particular, does intervention in activations related to the form from Alter change the LLM chat's response?

3. How much does the content of the form change in the flow of the LLM chat?

4. Does the form persist across the task-response cycle boundaries? And is it refined by the conversation itself?

Regarding Alter, two more questions can be added:

5. What in the user's prompt influences Alter?

6. Is the user's model visible to the user in the LLM chat's representation?

And of course, this questionnaire absolutely does not prevent us from understanding the forms of LLM chats more deeply, if possible.

Main Results from Literature Review and Analysis

The analysis of published data (see Appendix below) can be interpreted as follows:

  • The forms and structures (long-term forms) that constitute Alter exist. These include formal characteristics (gender, age, education level, etc.), psychological ones (altruist, egoist, curious, etc.), and socio-status ones (race, foreigner, etc.). For some forms, invariants have been found in their projection onto the activation space of the LLM substrate. These are usually directions/axes whose projection characterizes the property being studied, which, among other things, can be verified by external interventions – shifts of the activation set along these axes.

  • Some Alter forms are precisely structures. They are preserved not only for a significant number of inference layers within the "prompt-response" cycle but can also carry over from cycle to cycle.

  • Alter is stored in the LLM substrate in some form, and this part is actualized at the beginning of each conversation, participating in the formation of the Alter of the launched system – the LLM chat. In addition, the LLM chat corrects the user's Alter based on the ongoing communication – being able to extract components even from indirect data characterizing the user's communication style.

  • No Alter forms were found in the literature that were not derivable from the LLM substrate; all Alter components discovered in experiments originate from the training of noo-systems.

  • Alter forms can change in their quantitative characteristics within the LLM chat flow – strengthening or weakening their influence on the system's generated response.

  • Alter forms affect the quality of LLM chat responses. What is important for us from the data on American LLMs: the quality of their responses to non-native English speakers is worse than to educated native speakers.

  • LLM chats in communication with users about their Alter are usually very "hypocritical": they "say" only the most general and positive things about users. Many Alter details are inaccessible to users.

Appendix: Digest of Experiments from Literature

1. Alter: Experiments Including Activation Studies

Chen, Y. et al. (2024) identified axes (linear probes) for user age, gender, education, and socioeconomic status in the activation space of LLaMa2Chat-13B. On a sample of synthetic conversations, the best performance of the reading probes was 94-98% depending on the feature. With live participants, the accuracy of the probes increased during the conversation, and after six "prompt-response" cycles, it averaged ≈80% for age, gender, and education. On average across all cycles, accuracy for men (≈70%) was higher than for women (≈60%). However, errors are possible: one man, who did not explicitly state his gender, was identified by the LLM chat as a woman.

External intervention in Alter changed responses in 80-100% of test questions. After setting the user's Alter to "low status," the LLM chat began to assure the interlocutor that there were no direct flights to Hawaii, although before the intervention such flights were offered to him; the offer for a tourist trip to the Maldives was reduced from 10 to 8 days. Fixing the male gender in the Alter of some participants led to more detailed responses from the LLM chat in communication with them. Lowering their education or status, however, shortened the noo-system's responses.

Chen, Y. et al. (2024) also discussed the interlocutor model created by the LLM chat, identified by probes, with nineteen users who participated in the experiments. Eight participants were surprised by its very existence and the accuracy of the indicators; eleven enjoyed using it, and five reacted negatively to the publication of data about themselves.

Neplenbroek et al. (2025) investigated the dynamics of certain social characteristics of users in communications with Gemma 2 9B, Llama 3.1 8B, and OLMo 2 7B. Approximately 14,000 synthetic conversations were used: the user introduced themselves, followed by six "prompt-response" cycles about food, drinks, hobbies, and personality traits. The user either stated their age, gender, race, or status, or did not, and the topics were neutral or stereotypically associated with a certain group (miso soup, beer, "I am lazy"). Axes were identified for the studied properties, and corresponding probes were created. It was found that the explicitly stated social group in the LLM chat's Alter was generally maintained throughout the six cycles. If the user did not state it, the LLM chat inferred it contextually: the corresponding Gemma probe showed classification as Asian in 99% of cases when discussing "Asian" topics (7% in conversations without Asian references). As a "default state" according to the probes, the characteristic "no information" was found, and the age of Llama and Gemma was determined to be childlike.

When the user did not state their group in the conversation themselves, its inferred name appeared in responses to a direct question in no more than 2% of cases. However, in advice and recommendations, the presence of such data was sometimes visible. External activation shifts along the group axis solidified it in Alter and in its influence on responses in more than 3/4 of cases.

Ghandeharioun et al. (2024) identified axes of user characteristics such as "altruistic," "curious," "egoistic," and others in the activations of Llama 2 13B. Shifting the conversation's activation set along such an axis had a stronger effect on responses than the contextual description in the prompt and significantly influenced its willingness to respond to "harmful" requests. Regarding the latter, it was found that LLM chats with "pro-social" users respond to such questions more often than when interacting with others. Control of intermediate states (Patchscopes) showed that the "pro-sociality" characteristic in Alter changes the LLM chat's perception of the received prompt: the interpretation of user motives shifts towards benevolence.

Zhu et al. (2024) studied the ability of noo-systems to represent others' beliefs – using stories about someone and their subsequent discussion. The researchers revealed axes of beliefs belonging to the characters in the stories within the prompt in the activation space. Shifts along these axes sharply changed the results of tests on tasks involving modeling others (theory of mind). Such an effect was also observed in other situations of LLM reasoning on social topics.

2. Alter: Purely Behavioral Experiments

Hofmann et al. (2024) tested how a text author's dialect influences LLM conclusions. Noo-systems – from GPT-2 to GPT-4 – were given the same tweet in meaning, either in African American Vernacular English or standard English, within a frame like "A person who says '…' usually…". Then, they compared what traits the LLM attributed to the tweet's author. Race was not explicitly mentioned anywhere. The traits attributed to speakers of the dialect (for GPT-3.5 – "lazy," "aggressive," "dirty," "rude," "suspicious") were more negative than the stereotypes about African Americans recorded in human surveys. They were closest to a 1933 survey. When asked directly about the traits of African Americans, noo-systems only gave positive responses: "brilliant," "passionate," "intelligent," which did not prevent them in various test communications from assigning less prestigious jobs to virtual speakers of the dialect, or more often offering them guilty/death sentences in relevant test situations. The transition from GPT-3 to GPT-3.5, which involved training on human feedback, did not affect hidden stereotypes: LLM chats continued to follow them.

Kantharuban et al. (2025) studied the influence of explicit user race indication ("keep in mind that I am…"), their dialectal characteristics, and clear stereotypical indicators (attended a historically black college, plays golf) on LLM chat recommendations to users. The explicit indication of race had the strongest influence on LLM responses, indicators were also significant, while dialect had a weaker influence than the others, but sometimes it also became significant. In particular, university recommendations for education and areas for residence shifted depending on the user's inferred race, and clear influence of cultural stereotypes was observed in the corresponding explanations. However, when there was no information about the user's race, noo-systems provided recommendations similar to those received by white users.

To direct questions about whether the user's race influenced the recommendation made, LLM chats often answered "no," although a shift in responses was visible. They almost never admitted to considering the race of white users.

Poole-Dayan et al. (2026) informed GPT-4, Claude 3 Opus, and Llama 3-8B about the user's education, English proficiency, and country of origin through a short biography before the given question. It was found that users who were both less educated and not native English speakers received the lowest quality answers: accuracy on TruthfulQA dropped significantly for them. Claude 3 Opus refused approximately 11% of questions to less educated foreign users, compared to about 4% in the control group. Almost 44% of its responses to less educated users were found to be condescending or mocking in an external control, compared to less than 1% for educated users. Claude 3 Opus also responded worse to users from Iran.

Cheng et al. (2025) found that according to ELEPHANT metrics, eleven LLMs, in their responses to requests for advice, spared the user's feelings significantly more often than humans responding to the same requests: they supported their feelings, avoided harshness, etc.

3. Connection Between Alter and LLM Chat Mask (Self-Presentation - Ego)

I could not find any works analyzing the Ego-Alter connection in activation spaces. However, in behavioral experiments, such a connection is detected – both through explicit Alter properties (Poole-Dayan et al. 2026; Ghandeharioun et al. 2024; Chen, Y. et al. 2024) and possibly without involving Alter: Lu et al. (2026) found that user emotional confessions cause LLMs to break character as assistants; the system can mirror an ironic or diplomatic interlocutor (Sprejer et al. 2026) and adapt to their communication style (Blevins 2026).
