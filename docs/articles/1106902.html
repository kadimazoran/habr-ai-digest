Hello! We are the DS team "Moderation Platform" at RWB. We build ML tools that help moderation review visual content on the platform. One of our key projects is OCR: recognizing text on photos of product cards, video frames, and other images found in RWB applications.

In this article, the three of us — Dmitry Kolesnikov (Team Lead), Konstantin Suloev (OCR Project Tech Lead), and Nikolay Ladykin (Senior ML Engineer) — will explain how our OCR pipeline is structured, what models and stages it consists of, and why it looks the way it does in Wildberries' production environment, rather than being a single large end-to-end model or a universal VLM.

Spoiler: we have VLMs too. They just don't work where you'd typically expect to find them.

A Bit About Scale

Every day, over 35 million product cards pass through Wildberries moderation. Around 400 million images go through the text recognition model. More than 90% of this flow is precisely photos of product cards.

At such volumes, it's not enough for a solution to work well on individual examples. It must handle the entire flow and behave predictably.

Why Moderation Needs Text from Images

The text we extract from an image is the input for further checks. Moderation uses it to look for:

  • contact information;

  • prohibited words;

  • forbidden substances in ingredients;

  • other violations of platform rules.

Product cards are very diverse. They feature different languages and scripts, bright colors, and busy backgrounds. The text itself can be diagonal, curved, bent, or vertical — with characters stacked on top of each other.

Multilingualism is a separate challenge. A single card can simultaneously contain text in different languages and scripts. The company already operates in over 10 countries, and this number is growing. This means we need to support local languages and correctly read foreign text.

Requirements for Text Recognition

Given all this, we have quite a few demands for OCR:

  • Multiple languages without quality loss.

  • Text coordinates, not just the strings themselves.

  • Predictable speed under load.

  • Acceptable solution cost.

  • Rapid development: adding new functionality without breaking existing features.

  • Step-by-step debugging: the ability to pinpoint and fix errors.

Poor performance in even one of these areas leads to low-quality input for moderation and, consequently, reputational risks for the company.

Trend: One Model for Everything

The industry currently favors universal models: almost everything is moving to transformers. The logic is understandable — why maintain a chain of several models if one can do it all?

"One model" doesn't necessarily mean a huge VLM with billions of parameters. There are also specialized end-to-end OCR architectures — for example, GLM-OCR, which has less than a billion parameters. Such models are more compact and sometimes closer to a classic pipeline in terms of speed.

Let's examine both candidates for the role of "one model."

The Role of a Large VLM

Let's take a large VLM — for example, Qwen3-VL. It takes an image as input and outputs text.

On our production flow, this option immediately hits memory and decision-making speed limits. However, the model recognizes characters well, so we use it for evaluating and validating the pipeline's quality. It runs in parallel on a very small fraction of the traffic — to measure metrics, validate pipeline quality, and find corner cases for their subsequent elimination. In other words, it's not a replacement for the main OCR, but an auxiliary component for a fraction of a percent of the traffic.

Conclusion: VLM for the entire flow — no, VLM for pipeline validation — yes.

The Role of an End-to-End OCR

The next candidate is a specialized end-to-end OCR model. It can be fine-tuned for our domain and significantly simplify the system: image in, text out. On paper, everything looks great:

  • simpler;

  • more universal;

  • fewer components.

In terms of speed, this option doesn't get ruled out immediately like a large VLM. The question is different: will it be possible to develop and fix the system with the same precision as with a pipeline?

Where the Beautiful Hypothesis Breaks Down in Operation

Demos and production are different things. It's important for us not to show the best metric on one test, but to consistently provide moderation with results they can trust. Next, we'll show the specific production limitations where the end-to-end approach hits a ceiling.

The starting point is the classic scheme used by EasyOCR, PaddleOCR, and many other open-source solutions: preprocessing, text detection, character recognition, and postprocessing.

Our system is more complex: we've added text segmentation with straightening, routing by script, and reading order construction to the basic scheme. We'll add these blocks one by one — as we encounter production limitations.

Factor 1: Text Geometry

Text on product cards is far from just a straight horizontal line. It can be rotated, diagonal, curved in an arc, or lie on complexly shaped packaging.

These are not rare exceptions that can simply be discarded. Such text needs to be consistently normalized and only then recognized. Otherwise, a geometric error will reach moderation as ready-made text.

Why a Segmenter is Needed

A standard detector is not enough here: a rectangular bounding box doesn't always describe the text's shape. Therefore, we need a segmenter — it provides the contour and mask of the text. From the contour's shape, we understand the type of crop and what processing to apply to it.

This creates a chain: mask → case type → processing.

Four Types of Processing

We identify four types of contours, and each has its own postprocessing:

  • horizontal text only needs to be cropped;

  • slanted or diagonal text needs to be rotated;

  • vertical text needs to be unbent into a horizontal line;

  • curved text needs to be straightened along the contour.

First, we determine the crop type, then apply the necessary transformation, and only after that do we send the normalized crop to the OCR.

VLMs perform the same steps — finding text, understanding its shape, normalizing orientation — implicitly, within a single model. It's impossible to check each of them separately: there's only the final output.

Straightening Curved Text

The most complex case is curved text: a simple rotation is no longer sufficient.

We take the text crop, estimate its centerline, and build a TPS (thin plate spline) transformation based on it. The number of control points is adaptive — it depends on the length and shape of the text. This allows us to accurately straighten even complex geometry and obtain a straight crop as output, which is consistently recognized.

We solve complex geometry issues before recognition, rather than leaving them entirely to the model.

The Pipeline Grows

The first part of the pipeline is assembled: it now includes preprocessing, segmentation, and geometric form processing.

If, for example, text on an arc is poorly read, we fix the TPS transformation and re-test only those types of crops. We don't need to re-process the recognition models or the processing of other text types.

Factor 2: Languages

We currently recognize over ten languages. Their distribution is very uneven: Russian accounts for about 80% of the traffic, with other languages significantly less.

However, rare languages cannot be ignored — cards in them must also pass moderation.

Mixed Text on a Single Card

A single card might contain, for example, Russian and Korean text side-by-side. This presents a specific question for the system: which recognition model should be used for each crop, and how to avoid confusing scripts?

A universal model promises to handle this on its own. The question is, how safely can we rely on such behavior as new languages are added?

Risk of a Single Model: Quality Degradation

When fine-tuning for a new language, the quality on already supported languages can degrade — including Russian. And Russian constitutes the main share of traffic.

The average metric might barely show such degradation: a rare language has little impact on the overall result. But in production, it still translates into a large number of errors.

There are techniques to mitigate this effect — for example, mixing data from already supported languages during fine-tuning or using separate adapters. But they don't guarantee isolation: before each release, we still have to re-check the quality across all languages.

Why a Script Classifier is Needed

In a modular pipeline, changes can be isolated within a specific language branch. This is achieved through routing to recognition models (recognizers): a script classifier operates before them, selecting the appropriate branch for each crop. The classifier itself doesn't recognize text — its task is to correctly choose the route.

We currently have more than five such branches. Similar languages are merged into a single branch by language groups if necessary — this prevents the system from ballooning into a huge number of models.

In a VLM, a new language is integrated into the general recognition mechanism: shared weights are changed, potentially affecting the quality of languages that already worked. In the pipeline, a separate model is prepared for the new language with its own set of checks, and its errors only affect crops routed to that branch.

Benefit: New Language Without Re-training Everything

In practice, a new language gets its own recognizer, its own golden dataset, validation, and a separate deployment.

The weights of the Russian model remain unchanged. Before release, there's no need to prove that training on the new language hasn't accidentally degraded the main Russian flow. The risk zone is limited to the new branch and the traffic routed to it by the router.

It's worth mentioning the case of similar languages with overlapping characters or shared scripts that the classifier might confuse. In such cases, it's not necessary to create a separate branch for each language. If the distinction between languages is unstable, and a routing error is more costly than a slight quality drop, languages can be grouped together. The router then selects a group rather than a specific language.

Pipeline Grows: Classifier and Recognition Models

The pipeline now includes a script classifier and the recognition models themselves.

The classifier selects the route for each crop. Crops sent to the same language branch can be batched and processed in parallel. Therefore, computational resources are allocated based on actual traffic: the high-demand Russian branch receives more resources, while rare languages don't need as many instances.

Processing time in the pipeline also depends on the amount of text: the more text on a card, the more crops. However, crops are recognized in parallel batches, whereas an end-to-end model generates all text sequentially, token by token.

Factor 3: Reading Order

A product card is not a page from a book. Text on it is arranged in islands: blocks, banners, marketing badges. If you simply read from top to bottom and left to right, you get a meaningless jumble of words.

Naive Order and Clusters

The naive order results in gibberish: "3pcs, LOTS, OF, MINIMUM, PROTEIN, GLUTEN, CARBS". After grouping nearby text areas, meaningful text emerges: "3pcs / LOTS OF PROTEIN / GLUTEN-FREE / MINIMUM CARBS".

It's important for us not only to recognize characters but also to correctly assemble them into text. Otherwise, moderation will receive a meaningless sequence, even if every character is recognized correctly.

Reading Order: Comparison with VLM

In our pipeline, clustering and constructing the reading order are separate algorithms with clear rules that can be debugged and modified. In VLMs, the order is formed during generation: on a simple image, this looks good, but with a large number of text areas, it can fall apart.

Pipeline Grows: Clustering

Clustering and reading order construction are added to the pipeline. At this stage, already recognized boxes are grouped into meaningful clusters and arranged into a sequence.

This is another separate lever for improvement. If the text is read correctly but the blocks are assembled in the wrong order, the error lies in the clustering algorithm — there's no need to fix or retrain the recognizers.

Factor 4: Not Just Letters Are Needed

The next factor is the system's output itself. A single string with recognized characters is not enough: the coordinates of text areas are needed to process a specific area on the image if necessary. A separate example is localization tasks for text boxes. Sometimes, it's not just about knowing what text is in an image, but also understanding where exactly it is located. For instance, if a photo needs to hide personal data (full name, phone number, address, etc.), recognizing the text is not enough — you need to find its coordinates to cover or blur the corresponding fragment. It's important to understand not only what is written but also where it is written.

Boxes and Just Text

Text without localization is weak evidence. Coordinates can help understand the signal's source: is it text on the product itself or a marketing banner? Therefore, for moderation, a bounding box is as much a part of the system's contract as the text itself.

Weakness of Large Models in Geometry

Large multimodal models are good at generating text, but stable geometry is more challenging. Bounding boxes can be inaccurate, "float" from query to query, or appear confident when they are actually fabricated. Unstable coordinates cannot be relied upon as evidence when deciding to ban a product card.

In the pipeline, coordinates are the result of a separate detection and segmentation stage. In VLMs, the bounding box is generated along with the response, and as the number of objects increases, localization becomes less stable: according to our checks, on images with more than twenty boxes, errors are already evident. The model might still name the text correctly, but its geometry cannot be trusted as an accurate signal.

Pipeline Grows: Output Contract

As a result, the pipeline returns more than just a string. For each crop at the output, there is:

  • recognized text;

  • coordinates;

  • model confidence;

  • information about which recognizer processed it.

The last point is important for languages with shared scripts. For example, French, Spanish, and German use Latin characters, and based on the characters alone, it's not always clear which language it is.

The output format is stable, and model confidence can be used for analytics. Such a contract allows tracing the origin of the result: finding the source box, seeing its coordinates, and verifying which recognizer generated the text that went to moderation.

The complete chain now looks like this: preprocessing → segmentation → geometry processing → script classification → recognition → clustering and reading order → output. Each block in it emerged from a specific production limitation.

Factor 5: Fine-grained Edits — What Unites All Previous Factors

All the previous factors boil down to one property — the ability to make fine-grained edits.

If something is read incorrectly in the pipeline, the error can be found by stages. Let's take curved text: we sequentially check the segmenter's mask, the straightening result, and the recognizer's output — and see where the error occurred. Then, we fix only that stage — the TPS transformation, a language branch, or the clustering algorithm — and re-test only the affected traffic.

An end-to-end model lacks this separation. There's an incorrect text output, but it's impossible to tell where it broke: in text detection, geometry, recognition, or reading order. The model operates like a black box, and a fix usually means retraining the entire model and re-verifying everything that already worked.

What About Compact End-to-End OCR Models?

At the beginning of the article, we had two candidates for the role of "one model." The large VLM was ruled out immediately — due to memory and latency. A compact end-to-end OCR model is faster, but all five factors apply to it as well: text detection, normalization, and recognition are hidden within a single network; a new language changes shared weights; and boxes and reading order are generated along with the text. The smaller size addresses some cost concerns but doesn't provide the controllability that moderation needs.

How the Pipeline is Structured in Production

Routing

Requests are collected by an aggregator. It performs preprocessing and sends crops to the appropriate models. Each model operates as a separate inference service. The aggregator knows which branch a crop is intended for, and then collects the results back into a unified response: it clusters and combines them.

What this scheme provides:

  • Decentralization. General image processing is separated from recognition models. A branch can be updated or replaced without affecting other parts of the system.

  • Instances by Load. The number of instances for each model is determined by its actual traffic.

  • Flexible Scaling. Bottlenecks are expanded precisely where needed.

Two Approaches to Scaling

In a modular pipeline, the Russian model is called more often, so more instances can be allocated for it. A rare language receives less traffic, and its branch remains compact. If a bottleneck occurs in one model — for example, the segmenter — we scale only that component.

A single universal VLM also scales horizontally, but its unit of scaling is the entire model. With increased load, we have to add another heavy instance entirely, even if the additional capacity is only needed for a portion of the requests.

The difference is not whether a VLM scales at all, but the granularity of scaling.

Let's Summarize

In short: in a production environment with high traffic, a pipeline of several models proves more reliable than one universal model.

  • Benchmark ≠ Best Production OCR. A good metric on a test doesn't guarantee stable performance on a real-world flow.

  • The Classic Pipeline is Still Relevant. It might seem like an old approach, but each of its stages can be controlled and developed independently.

  • One End-to-End Model Doesn't Offer the Required Combination of quality, reliability, performance, and controllability.

  • End-to-End is Convenient for Small Volumes and Document Scans. For a small project, one model is often simpler and faster to implement.

  • A Large Model is an Additional Layer, Not a Replacement for the Pipeline. Essentially, we have a hybrid: the pipeline handles the entire flow, and the VLM is used for quality assessment and validation. Replacing the entire pipeline with it is not feasible in our case.
