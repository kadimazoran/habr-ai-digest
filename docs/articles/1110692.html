On the same wavelength: why you don't want to go back after Neon

I opened it with complete skepticism: well, another "mid-tier" Google model, let's see at what minute it starts acting up, and then close it. As a result, I felt a strange sensation: Neon understands intentions no worse than Opus 5.5, it figures out non-obvious follow-ups itself, and communicates even more pleasantly.

Here's what's most impressive in daily work:

  • Normal, natural dialogue without a wall of text for requirements. You write briefly, imperfectly, and on the go, and the model accurately grasps the essence and responds in clear human language, without bureaucratic jargon and robotic politeness.

  • Healthy initiative. You give it one small task, it does it cleanly and simultaneously notices an old bug nearby that has been lingering for months, immediately offering to fix it.

  • Unconventional intuition in applied tasks. Where other models blindly follow the first available template, Neon truly digs deeper (whether it's analyzing strange server slowdowns or reviewing a full system disk where other agents miss tens and hundreds of gigabytes of junk).

  • Clean code and insightful review. From a mid-sized model, you expect messy workarounds and hallucinations, but on normal production repositories, the code comes out clean, and during review, it catches real bugs, not nitpicking formatting for the sake of appearing busy.

How Google fixed a dead cache and tripled limits in the last few days

On the first day after Neon's release, many simultaneously had their eyes burning from the model itself and their nerves frayed by Google's infrastructure.

On paper, the subscription limits look generous: a weekly budget for Google AI Ultra is estimated at $2000 (5x at $500) compared to $3000 for Claude Ultra and $1000 for ChatGPT Pro at $200. The problem was the completely broken prompt caching. A pause of less than a minute would cause cache hits to drop to 9%, or even zero. Each request re-processed the entire context from scratch at full price, burning 2.5 times more limits and noticeably slowing down. In reality, instead of a generous subscription, you got about four times less actual work done than with competitors.

But in the last couple of days, Google has reacted unusually quickly. After user complaints on X, the head of Antigravity personally reached out, confirmed the rollout of a cache eviction fix to all clusters, and promised to reset the burned limits.

The result is immediately noticeable. The cache now consistently stays around for 3 minutes with a high hit rate, actual limits in practice have increased by about three times, and the delay to the first token has been cut even further, so in live work, Neon has become even faster than Opus. Plus, the Orchestrator is now working in Antigravity: Neon handles planning and complex logic, while routine edits and tests are offloaded to the cheaper Hydrogen, stretching the quota even further. Of course, 3 minutes of cache life is still not the dream (you go to get coffee for five minutes, and the context has to be reloaded), but compared to the first day, it's night and day.

By the way, if you decide to try Neon in the Antigravity App (the app itself turned out to be light and responsive compared to the resource-hungry Codex App, although the Antigravity CLI is still a terrible mess), here's an important trick. The default system prompt there inexplicably forces the model to fill every response with kilometer-long file paths and function names, while remaining silent about what it's doing during the process. A couple of lines in the custom rules instantly remove this clutter and turn the responses into clean human text.

Where Neon is still weaker (honestly and without fanaticism)

Despite all my enthusiasm, this is not an ideal model for all occasions. It remains mid-sized, and its weak spots are immediately apparent:

  • Its visual frontend is noticeably simpler than Claude Opus 5.5's (though the texts within Neon's interfaces themselves are written much more lively and without the usual clichés). For complex design, it's easier to specifically call Claude.

  • In 3D, it noticeably lags behind both Opus and OpenAI's heavy flagships.

  • Minor "geminisims" sometimes slip through: a random emoji might appear out of place, or a funny raw snippet of internal reasoning might leak into the chat. It doesn't interfere with work, it's just amusing.

  • If the task involves image generation or parsing raw web transcripts, ChatGPT is still more confident here.

The bottom line is that Claude's monopoly has truly cracked for the first time in a long time. A second model has appeared that you want to work with, not argue with like a stubborn intern. And the most insane thought in this whole story is simple: Neon is just a mid-tier model. If Google has found this recipe for sanity in a mid-tier size, it's scary to imagine what their next big flagship will be like (I hope they name it humanely, not Gemini 4 Ununoctium).
