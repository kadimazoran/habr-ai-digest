Language models have become much smarter due to scale: they were trained on tens of trillions of text tokens that humanity has accumulated on the internet. Robotics does not have such a reserve: no one has uploaded millions of hours of recordings of a robot folding a shirt or pouring water to the network. Each such hour has to be recorded from scratch.

In this article, we will analyze how data is currently collected for robot training, how much has already been gathered, and how much it will cost to catch up to LLMs. By rough estimates, robots have about a thousand times less data.

Why Everything Hinges on Data

The progress in AI in recent years is primarily due to scale. In 2020, after the release of GPT-3, Gwern formulated this idea in his essay "The Scaling Hypothesis". According to him, intelligence is "just" simple learning algorithms plus a vast amount of diverse experience. The more computational power, the smarter neural networks will become. In 2024, a researcher under the pseudonym Nostalgebraist summarized: all current AI has grown from the discovery that models consistently and predictably improve with an increase in data volume.

The numbers confirm this:

  • according to Epoch AI, since the early 2010s, training datasets for language models have been doubling approximately every six months;

  • GPT-3 was trained on about 300 billion tokens from the internet, while the largest modern models are trained on tens of trillions;

  • in 2024, Epoch AI estimated the total stock of high-quality available text at ~300 trillion tokens.

For robots, there is simply no comparable stock of good publicly available data. Therefore, one of the key questions in the industry is where to get it and how much is needed.

What a Training Example for a Robot Looks Like

The principle is the same as for LLMs. The model needs many examples of correct task execution or at least a way to evaluate if an attempt was successful. The model receives input data, its response is compared to the correct one, and the parameters are adjusted using gradient descent to reduce the error.

With text, it's simple: any fragment is already a ready-made example. The model is shown the beginning and asked to predict the continuation.

For a robot, an example is a recording of it performing a task. At any given moment, it contains the robot's state (e.g., joint angles) and images from cameras. Based on these, the model predicts the next action.

Five Ways to Collect Data

Teleoperation

An operator uses controls directly linked to the robot to perform a task with its hands: folding a shirt, moving objects, pouring water.

All of this is recorded, and then the model is trained to repeat the operator's actions. This method, where the model imitates expert examples (in this case, a human), is called behavioral cloning. This is a specific case of a broader approach – imitation learning.

Pro: The data perfectly matches the capabilities of the specific robot, as it was recorded on that robot. Therefore, the method is still widely used. You can find photos of entire "data factories" online where people do nothing but control robots. There are also videos of accidents: the operator forgets they are controlling a robot and makes a movement that the robot cannot replicate.

Cons:

  • expensive: each operator needs a real robot, so scaling is difficult;

  • inconvenient: the operator usually doesn't feel the object, and fine manipulation cannot be recorded properly this way;

  • high defect rate: as far as is known, only a portion of the collected data is suitable for training.

Robots That Learn on Their Own

The robot acts autonomously, and a system evaluates whether the attempt was successful. This is reinforcement learning. For example, around 2016, Google had what was called an "arm farm." Manipulators tried to grasp objects on their own, and a successful grasp was counted as a success.

Such farms are rarely encountered now. But in the long term, the main source of data will likely be robots working in the real world. Robotics researchers call this the "data flywheel." Robots work, their data improves models, robots with improved models are deployed more widely, and more data is generated.

Physical Intelligence, for example, is testing this approach at a chocolate factory near its office. There are two problems. There are still very few robots with AI engaged in real work. And their data is monotonous: the same task under the same conditions, which is of little use for training.

Simulation

Instead of a real robot, a digital one is trained in a virtual world, usually using reinforcement learning. There is also a "teacher/student" scheme. The teacher model sees everything happening in the simulation. The student model sees only what a real robot would see: images from cameras and its own state. The student learns to imitate the teacher.

Simulation is cheap and fast, and it's easy to scale up to thousands of robots:

  • Figure AI taught its humanoid to walk by training it with reinforcement learning on thousands of simulated copies;

  • OpenAI taught a robotic arm to assemble a Rubik's Cube, spending the equivalent of 13,000 years of simulated experience;

  • by 2019, Waymo self-driving cars had driven 10 million miles on real roads and 10 billion in simulation.

The main problem is the sim-to-real gap. The data is only as good as the simulation is accurate. For locomotion tasks, it works reasonably well: the physics of walking on uneven terrain or a drone flying along a route are well-studied and amenable to digital modeling. However, manipulating objects, especially soft and deformable ones, is much harder to model accurately.

A partial solution is domain randomization. What friction or elasticity will be like in the real world is unknown in advance, so these parameters are varied randomly in the simulation. This prepares the model for a wide range of values. In the Rubik's Cube project, OpenAI went further and developed automatic domain randomization (ADR), which generates increasingly complex conditions. According to the team, the reason was that friction, elasticity, and the dynamics of such objects are too difficult to model accurately.

The result was modest: with the most complex initial cube positions, the arm succeeded only in 20% of cases. Perhaps this is why this technique is now rarely used for manipulation training. However, for locomotion, random variation of parameters like the friction coefficient has become standard.

Recording Humans

It's possible to record humans performing tasks and then transfer their movements to a robot. This is partly why humanoids are so popular: for a robot with a human body, recordings of people at work are a natural source of data.

Options:

  • First-person video (egocentric video), i.e., footage from a camera mounted on the head. Figure AI launched the Index program and sends head-mounted cameras to people. The company might even send a team to take care of your household chores and record them at the same time.

  • Additional sensors. The now-defunct startup Eidon AI attached sensors to arms to record joint positions. Neocambrian AI announced Mitts – haptic gloves that record touch data with high precision.

  • UMI (Universal Manipulation Interface) – hand manipulators with cameras and a "claw" gripper (multi-fingered ones also exist). A person operates them as a robot would, and the video is then used for training robots with similar grippers. This is mainly how Generalist AI and Sunday Robotics collect data.

Pros: much cheaper than teleoperation because it requires a camera, not a robot. Easier to collect diverse tasks. Real physics, not simulated.

Cons: A human is not a robot, and their movements do not fully match the machine's capabilities. With UMI, this discrepancy is minimal. The models behind many of the most impressive demos (e.g., at Generalist AI) are trained on such data.

Internet Videos and Combinations of Approaches

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models are built on top of Vision-Language models trained on text and images. There are a huge number of videos on YouTube and TikTok, so many are looking for ways to train robots on them as well. For example, Nvidia Cosmos is a foundational model trained specifically on such videos. It can be used as a backbone for a robot control model.

In practice, approaches are combined. Digit from Agility Robotics walks using a small neural network with 1 million parameters, trained with reinforcement learning in simulation. Its manipulation skills were apparently taught through behavioral cloning. Dyna Robotics appears to have combined simulation and videos of humans.

How Much Data Has Been Collected?

Datasets for robots are growing:

  • Open X-Embodiment (Google DeepMind, 2023) is compiled from several dozen datasets: over 1 million trajectories, over 160,000 tasks, about 4,000 hours;

  • Pi 0 (Physical Intelligence, October 2024) is trained on 10,000 hours of robot data;

  • GEN 1 (Generalist AI, 2026) is trained on approximately 500,000 hours of UMI data;

  • Dyna Robotics and Nature Will claim over 1 million hours of first-person video;

  • Figure AI, through Index, collects 30 minutes of video every second, which amounts to 43,000 hours per day.

By one estimate, the entire industry collectively collects about 500,000 hours of training data per month.

Why Comparing with Text is Difficult

Firstly, the data is not equivalent. Teleoperation and UMI recordings are more valuable than simple first-person video, and a diverse dataset is more useful than a monotonous one. Secondly, video is less information-rich than text because adjacent frames are almost identical. Robotics researcher Chris Paxton notes: the closer the training sample is to independent and identically distributed (iid), the better machine learning works. Internet materials do not fully meet this ideal, and recordings from robots even less so.
