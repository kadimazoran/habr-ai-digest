Provocative statement: my chess engine aims to surpass all existing ones in skill and speed. Of course, with caveats (more on that later). The engine shows excellent results but inexplicably loses in some situations. To fix this, one needs to analyze a large amount of data (game records against competing programs), identify anomalies, make changes to algorithms, adjust coefficients, run test matches, and compare results with previous versions. Data analysis using an AI chat showed dubious benefits: Claude misses important details, makes unfounded assumptions, and suggests inefficient solutions — work slows down and goes down the wrong path.

However, AI excelled at writing scripts to control competing programs: under careful guidance, it learned to select the opponent's level, recognize the position and the last move made, the time on the clock, and signs of the game ending. Moreover, the scripts work without contacting the AI itself or the Internet (saving tokens).

The Chess.com app is controlled only by mouse. Claude took over my cursor and didn't let me work in parallel. The idea came to move everything to a virtual machine and connect two mice with separate drivers, but at that moment I considered it overkill. Therefore, matches were simply run at night while I slept.

I reviewed the reports of the first games directly in the Terminal as tables that Claude printed. These quickly revealed some engine problems (e.g., lack of time when playing at high levels, when each move was considered for too long). But when there are many tables, comparing them is inconvenient. And thousands of games awaited us.

A statistically significant result is only achieved through numerous repetitions of the experiment, so matches consisted of 10–60 games. The engine played at the same difficulty level, alternating white and black pieces against the same opponent. Statistics for each game were recorded in a CSV file, resulting in a summary table for the match. Information about each move was not included in the table — there would be too much data. For now, it was just necessary to look at the overall picture and find suitable high-level analysis tools.

From Tables to Graphs

I loaded the match data into Apple Numbers (an analog of Microsoft Excel), added 4 columns to format the data for convenient display, and built a graph. It turned out exactly as needed! The approximate playing strength of the engine at each of the seven levels is visible, as is time consumption and anomalous games (such games can be analyzed separately from the logs).

Everyone Loves Dashboards

At this point, I decided to create my own convenient tool for viewing and comparing data. I chose this stack: Claude monitors games, collects statistics, and writes CSV files to the 'runs' directory. The server is written in PHP, it reads files from 'runs' and transmits them to the frontend. The frontend has standard HTML/CSS and minimal JavaScript. For the future, I had an idea for acceleration: drawing graphs directly in canvas. But first, I needed to define the graphs themselves.

It is important to note that statistical data includes not only the score in games but also internal engine variables: stack usage, percentage of predicted opponent moves, engine's position evaluation, probability of the position being in the cache, search depth, number of evaluated positions, and much more. External data also includes average and maximum time per move and remaining time on the clock after the game ends.

Obviously, you can't just dump this much information onto the user. But I decided to prove it by contradiction :-) I fed Claude Design a CSV table and a graph from Apple Numbers, explained in detail how to build such a graph, described the page layout, and asked Claude to figure out how to display the rest of the data. Of course, I wrote "make it in the style of modern trendy dashboards." Any UX designer would laugh at such a naive request, but that's exactly the kind of amusement I was looking for. According to my quality criteria, this is such bad UX that explaining and correcting anything is pointless. Not finding a pencil at hand, I drew a rough sketch in Figma. Yes, it looks ugly, but it's quite suitable as an illustration for the technical specification that I wrote for Claude in text.

After a two-day session with Claude Design, the result was exactly what was needed. From left to right, the dashboard displays 7 difficulty levels: at the first level, the engine thinks superficially, and at the seventh, its thoughts can reach maximum depth. Hovering over a game indicator highlights indicators in other blocks — this turned out to be very convenient. Clicking on a graph expands it. Initially, I made it expand on hover, but that was too fussy. I've kept the automatic height increase for now — the mechanism helps fit more information on small screens.

Each project requires an individual, contextually optimal way of displaying information. There are no "universal dashboards" or "proven solutions" — the former are found in student works on Behance and Dribbble, and the latter are only suitable for trivial cases. So, for the chess project, I devised two suitable types of information display.

These graphs show the change in difficulty level over time for all games in a match. Semi-transparent lines fill the graph area, thus revealing a trend (if any). The end of a game is marked by a sharp drop in the line — thanks to this, it's possible to highlight a specific game with the cursor (its line becomes opaque). Using this visualization, it was possible to identify and solve problems of losing on time and declining game quality due to time constraints.

The pie chart shows the budget allocated to a given level (the maximum number of positions the engine is allowed to explore at its difficulty level) — this is the gray disk. Different levels have different budgets (you can see this on hover), but the display is normalized so that disks of different levels have the same diameter and represent 100% of the budget. The green sector shows two values: the percentage of moves that exceeded the budget (sector angle) and by what percentage the budget was exceeded (sector area). Exceeding the budget is very good, despite the name: it means the program thought during the opponent's move and managed to think more than allowed. People do this too.

Remember when I said you can't just dump all the information onto the user? The red part of the pie chart shows the underutilization of the budget. It's in its correct place: the gray sector corresponds to moves that did not exceed the budget. Noticeable unspent budget can mean both "good" and "bad." The opponent's moves were predicted, and the engine didn't have to spend its own time/budget — that's good. The game dragged on, time was running out, the engine had to cut short the search to make a move, the budget was not fully spent — that's bad.

Edge Cases, Errors, and Empty Statuses

The number of observed variables grew, and the logs of past games did not contain corresponding entries, so it was necessary to learn to work with incomplete information. As soon as the first such case arose, the principle "no information — don't display the block" was applied to the entire project. However, in a couple of cases, missing information could be approximately restored (e.g., predicting a drop in game quality based on the number of moves made and time on the clock). It didn't make sense to pre-process these scenarios because the set of observed variables was not fixed. Problems were solved as they arose. Well, statuses like "loading" were implemented simply, as the dashboard runs locally on a computer with a fast SSD. After all, my goal was to create a tool in 2 days.

On the third day, I added a chatbot to the site so that one could discuss statistics and chess in general with it. Usually, chatbots spend the site owner's tokens. I came up with a different approach: the user is offered to connect their own Claude or ChatGPT and chat at their own expense. With Claude, this works even on the free plan. With ChatGPT, it's more complicated (it can be done, but in this case, it would be over-engineering).

Compromises

For now, it's not possible to delve into the details of the game move by move. This is rarely needed, and one can get by with reviewing logs. If you don't want to read logs — there's a chatbot on the dashboard that can provide all the details about a game move. In the future, I'd like to add an indicator for each move's evaluation: how the engine evaluates the position, how Stockfish evaluates it, and the material advantage. These values are similar in meaning, and they can be displayed on one graph. A separate graph will show the time spent on a move and a summary histogram of time consumption (separately for the opening, middlegame, and endgame, because the engine behaves slightly differently in different phases of the game).

The dashboard is not intended for player analysis, so evaluations like "number of successful moves" and "missed mate in 2 moves" are not needed. This answers the question that probably arose at the beginning: "why didn't you look at how competitors do it in your research?" This project solves a completely different, unique problem.

The dashboard works locally, and the data loading time is negligible. But I posted an online version — there's already a noticeable delay: in series of 60 games, the dashboard loads 427 tables, and only then draws the graphs. In such cases, any UI designer's reflex kicks in: add an animated loading icon. I did it differently. First, I optimized the graph rendering — where CSS and HTML calculated relative values like percentages, but the values were actually fixed, I recalculated them into pixels. The browser profiler showed a twofold acceleration in rendering. Second, I plan to write a script that loads game data into an SQL database instead of storing it as files on disk — then reading will be faster. And third, I plan to read data from the database not all at once (420 games), but gradually, one game at a time for each difficulty level — then graphs can be drawn one by one. Thus, the graph area will fill up gradually as data is read — this is the best loading animation! And most importantly, when working with large data, you don't have to wait for the full load — you can start working with what has already loaded.

What AI Did for Me

Since the days of Photoshop, I've had a habit of giving meaningful names to all objects. You'll never find "Frame 3754629" in my Figma. This is extremely important for collaboration. Now, in the age of AI, the habit of embedding meaning into names has provided a manifold acceleration: it's enough to combine the text strings Blitz, Rapid, and Classical into an auto-layout named "Tabs" for Claude to understand what styling and behavior to apply. I named the columns and sectors of the diagrams roughly the same as the columns in the data table, so the AI linked them without any questions.

Also, Claude Design determined the font hierarchy from my Figma sketch and applied it throughout the project. The infographic colors were correctly assigned to the corresponding statistical variables. I didn't have to construct a design system — clear names of layout elements became perfect answers to the questions "how, what, and why."

On its own, Claude added horizontal markers to the graphs. It turned out nice the first time. But with additional colors, I had to tinker, even resorting to manual intervention.

AI did a good job of creating a dark theme, but it failed the contrast standards (accessibility) — this had to be negotiated separately, and I had to choose from the proposed options.

The icons for light and dark themes turned out well the first time. The rest had to be redone. After all, the neural network doesn't have a natural taste for such things.

Of course, Claude wrote all the HTML, CSS, JavaScript, and PHP. After being asked to find unused, redundant, and slow code, the AI reduced and accelerated its creation by half.

Result

In this project, I addressed not only how to display data but also what data to show, what data is missing for decision-making, and what information needs to be collected and added to the engine.

The dashboard helped identify not only algorithm shortcomings but also outright bugs (in anomalous games). Thanks to visualization, it was possible to tune the smooth increase in difficulty depending on the level. And most importantly, to make important decisions and product changes:

  • Level warm-up was added: the first moves are considered weaker, the level increases with each move until it reaches the set one. This solves two problems: UX (we don't make the user wait long at the start of the game) and timeout (the program saves time for the end of the game). By the way, we don't make excessively fast moves either — Steve Jobs even talked about this when working on the first chess games for Macintosh.

  • Level decrease: when there is little time left until the end of the game, the program lowers the playing level to avoid flagging. If the end of the game is postponed by adding time for quickly made moves, the level automatically increases. This rule applies to levels 2–6.

  • Budget decrease at level 7: due to warm-up in fast games, level 7 was not reached, and in long games, it dropped too early. Instead of decreasing, it was decided to keep all parameters of level 7 and only lower the number of moves to explore. Thanks to the position evaluations accumulated in the cache, this still allowed making strong moves in less time.

  • Ten-second rule: the search for variations is interrupted if less than 10 seconds remain on the clock. In this case, the best move found up to that point is executed.

  • Abandoning the idea of counting thinking time during the opponent's move towards the budget (initially, half of the spent resources were deducted from the total budget allocated to the corresponding game level). This did not resemble playing against a live opponent.

If you compare the statistics of old engine versions with new ones (click the calendar icon there), you can see that the game against strong opponents has improved, and the engine has become friendlier towards weaker opponents. All this fine-tuning (and sometimes radically coarse) became possible thanks to unique data visualization tools.
