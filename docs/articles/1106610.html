I started looking at the tables because of the gap between the promise of automating code writing and the expectation that this would make all development cheaper: the former doesn't yet explain how much the latter will change. Dario Amodei, CEO of Anthropic, said on March 10, 2025, in the Redwood Research blog:

I think we'll be there in three to six months—where AI is writing 90 percent of the code.

Later, Satya Nadella, CEO of Microsoft, described the situation at Microsoft on April 29, 2025, on CNBC:

Maybe 20%, 30% of the code that is inside of our repos today and some of our projects are probably all written by software.

Nadella is estimating the code in his own repositories. So how can I calculate the cost of an application from these percentages? The client needs a working product, and I need to understand how much time it will take to develop it. The proportion of AI code by itself doesn't answer this question.

Therefore, I decided to check three things: how estimates for typical features are changing, how many hours the team writes off, and whether AI agent involvement is visible in the project history. This is where it becomes clear how much can be lost if you simply divide the budget by the actual outcome and call the result acceleration.

To do this, I combined three sources: estimates, Jira, and GitLab. Each has its own purpose, and it will become clear later why one of them is not enough.

Before comparing, I'll define the boundaries so we don't argue later about what this data doesn't show at all:

  • Projects were not randomly assigned to development with or without agents. Regular projects here are for checking against the estimate, not for measuring the speed of the same work without AI.

  • I am comparing 2 AI-first cases with old budgets. Both are web, both use Next.js and Nest.js. This is not yet a basis for applying the same coefficient to mobile applications or all development.

There's an assumption with the website that significantly affects the result. The owner estimated readiness at 80%, and I conditionally took 80% of the budget: 742 × 0.8 ≈ 594 hours. However, 80% readiness doesn't necessarily mean 80% of the effort. There's no comparison of estimated hours for specific closed tasks here, so the ratio 594 / 240 ≈ 2.5× depends on this estimate.

For the expedition service, the problem is on the other side: part of the work wasn't recorded in Jira. I added approximately 120 hours to the 311 hours as an estimate, resulting in about 431 hours. What I take away from this for my work: old hour norms should be re-verified. But I wouldn't transfer the coefficient from two web cases to a promise for any future project. The website's readiness and the gaps in expedition service tracking haven't disappeared from the calculation.

In the 2027 estimates, I would start by reviewing familiar tasks. If authorization is already estimated at different hours, why automatically carry over the old figure to the next project? Meanwhile, the hourly rate changed more modestly: median 3,900 → 3,600 ₽/hour. I don't have enough data to promise that all applications will now uniformly become cheaper.

An experiment where access to AI was randomly distributed and the time to complete real tasks was measured allows us to check if AI always saves time. The research group METR, July 10, 2025, obtained a result with the opposite sign:

when developers use AI tools, they take 19% longer than without—AI makes them slower

In METR, 16 experienced developers solved 246 tasks in familiar open-source projects, and access to AI was randomly assigned. My comparison is different: agent implementations from 2026 versus estimates. Therefore, I cannot put my coefficients next to their result and declare that I have refuted the study. But their result also doesn't automatically apply to any new project. This data doesn't explain why the results differ.

Savings in implementation still leave open the question of maintenance costs: if the code needs to be refactored more often, the initial hour calculation will not be enough. Data on code duplication forces us to look beyond project delivery. GitClear, January 2026, reports an increase in duplication from 40.3 (2023) to 73.0 blocks per million changed lines (2026, first half), an 81% increase. The costs of further maintenance for my cases were not measured in this sample, so I wouldn't attribute increased costs to them based on someone else's research.

The prevalence of AI doesn't yet indicate stable delivery. In the DORA report, September 2025, 90% of respondents use AI, while 30% have almost no trust or no trust at all in its code. More active AI use in the report is associated with both faster release of changes and a decrease in delivery stability. Therefore, to development hours, we need to add observations on how the team delivers changes and how stable this process is.

My conclusion for now is this: estimates for familiar features now have fewer hours, and two agent implementations spent less than the old budget. This is enough for me to revise estimation norms. There isn't enough data to promise any client several times the acceleration.
