Speech Synthesis at Long Distance: A 1h 58m Novel vs. 14 Test Phrases

In the first article of the series, there was a table showing how a synthesized voice read an entire book: Shamil Aladin's novel "Merdiven" ("The Staircase"), 16 chapters, 15,444 words, 1 hour 58 minutes of playback. In the second, I promised to explain in detail what broke down over the long haul. Here it is.

In short, the voice held up steadily for the entire two hours. Almost everything that broke was not in the model itself, but in the surrounding pipeline: text segmentation into sentences, stitching short phrases, trimming breaths, extracting text from the book file, and time-stamping. The test set of 14 phrases, which I previously used to compare model versions, didn't catch any of these problems, and in one case, couldn't distinguish a good edit from a harmful one.

A disclaimer, as in previous articles in the series: model names, data sources, internal scripts, and training/decoding parameters will not be mentioned here. There will be metrics, testing methodology, and pitfalls.

What I Measured With and On What

The main tool is back-reading. We synthesize a sentence, run the audio through our recognizer (the very same one from the second article), and calculate the CER of the hypothesis against the text that went into the synthesis input. The figure per chapter is corpus-based: the sum of corrections to the sum of reference characters, not an average per clip. Clips where the recognizer went into a loop and made more corrections than words in the reference are marked separately by the harness. This field appeared back in the ASR experiment, and it came in handy here, as discussed below.

The blind spots of the instrument are known in advance. It doesn't hear stress and breaths, can't check numbers, and it's a model itself, which can have its own glitches. All this had to be covered by ear.

Before the chapter, a check for leakage was needed: if the text was in the training data, the model doesn't read, it recalls. I compared word 6-grams. Both sides are reduced to a common rough Latin skeleton before comparison: the training materials are in Cyrillic for me, the book is in Latin. Such a reduction loses differences only towards extra matches, so its zero is honest. First chapter: 0 matches out of 1,545 six-word sequences against approximately 1.38 million words of the training corpus. Later, the same check was performed on the entire book: 0 out of 14,656.

The very first measurement on the chapter was a lesson. On the test phrases, CER was around 14%, on the chapter's prose, less than 2%. The model didn't get better overnight. The test set is deliberately hostile, and ordinary text is not, and on ordinary text, the model is almost transparent to the recognizer. I haven't reported the figure from the test set as the quality of real reading since then. It's only good for comparing versions among themselves, and below there will be a case where it couldn't even do that.

The overall verdict on the first chapter was unexpected. The voice held up: the similarity of voice embeddings with the first minute of the chapter remained within 0.96–0.98 for all twelve minutes, the tempo was even. Errors were found not in the model itself, but in the preparation of text and audio around it. I wrote this part myself, and that was good news: such things can be fixed without retraining.

The Base and the Voice, in Two Paragraphs

This was detailed in the first article; here is only what is needed for further discussion. Pre-trained models from neighboring languages read our къ as a regular k. Therefore, I chose the base for the voice based on one criterion: it must already be able to pronounce this sound. I found a multilingual model that had heard a lot of Kazakh, Uyghur, and Bashkir. It doesn't have Crimean Tatar, and this is circumvented by writing: our къ, гъ, нъ are rewritten into Bashkir ҡ, ғ, ң, which the model knows, and then it reads our text as Bashkir.

The voice is taken separately. A fine-tuned model learns the language, not the timbre: it takes the timbre from a short sample, a few seconds of recording with precise text. "Merdiven" is read in Sevil's voice, and each sentence of the book is voiced from the same seven-second sample. The model, the timbre, and the surrounding pipeline turned out to be independent things, and they could be fixed separately.

First Chapter: Three Fixes, and None in the Model

Stitching Short Sentences

The shortest clips in the training recordings lasted 2.94 s. From this came the seemingly ironclad logic: a remark shorter than 43 Cyrillic characters is better not given to the model separately, but attached to the next one. I didn't check this assumption. In vain.

In the first run, out of 147 fragments, 38 contained more than one sentence. And on these, the model began to omit entire sentences:

"I was mistaken. He, it turns out, is not a teacher" turned into "He, it turns out, is not a teacher." The stitched fragments occupied 25% of the chapter's characters and accounted for 43% of the errors.

Then I finally checked the assumption itself: I read the chapter without stitching and broke down the errors by sentence length.

First chapter without stitching, length in Cyrillic characters, i.e., in the form the model sees the text. The gray zone is what was previously attached to neighbors. There is no break at 43 characters, and the improvement goes up to about 70.

Short sentences are indeed worse: there are about two and a half times more errors in them. But there is no break at 43 characters, for which the stitching was conceived: the 0–24 and 25–42 bins are the same, and CER continues to decrease up to about 70 characters. The stitching boundary was not set where the real threshold lies. And most importantly, the costs of the two evils are different: the penalty for short remarks costs about 2 percentage points of CER on 15% of the text, while stitching cost 3.3 points on 25% of the text and additionally lost entire sentences. I turned off stitching.

Trimming Breaths

The synthesis sometimes starts a phrase with a breath, like a live speaker, and I wrote a trimmer. How it cut the first word, I already told in the first article: it looked for the first voiced frame, and considered voiceless consonants before it as a breath. Another example from the same chapter: "Çañ qaqıldı" turned into "Qaqıldı." In 111 out of 147 sentences, the cut fell inside the first word. The new trimmer looks at volume: a consonant is quiet, but much louder than the pause before it.

Breaths: 89% That Weren't There

The first check reported that 131 out of 147 sentences, or 89%, start with a breath. The figure was alarming until it turned out that the trimmer was reporting on itself: it considered everything before the first voiced frame as a breath, i.e., the same voiceless consonants. When I defined a breath as a stable audible noise before a word and measured it by level, there were 30 out of 147 left, one per five sentences.

Only this measurement, like the trimmer itself, looked exclusively at the beginning of the phrase. Scanning the entire waveform found breaths between words in 76 out of 195 sentences, exactly where none of my detectors had looked before. I learned to suppress them to background level without shifting any timestamps. The cost to intelligibility is zero: 68 out of 76 modified sentences are recognized byte-for-byte the same as before suppression, and none of the 119 untouched ones shifted.

Tempo and Pauses

The machine reads faster than a live speaker: 17.4 Cyrillic characters per second versus 14.5. The chapter, which I expected to be 14.9 minutes long, fit into 12.3. For an audiobook, this sounds a bit rushed.

I didn't invent the pauses between phrases; I measured them from a live speaker reading for ten minutes: 129 pauses, a median of 288 ms, and only three longer than 0.6 s in all ten minutes. Hence my 300 ms between sentences, slightly more when changing speakers, and slightly more between paragraphs. An important detail: these are actual pauses, not insertions. Each clip already has its own silence at the edges, so the assembler measures it from both neighbors and only adds the difference. This detail will resurface in the section on time-stamping.

The result for the chapter: CER 1.84% instead of 2.56% in the first run (in the re-measurement for the table above, the same configuration gave 2.57%). The model is the same. Only what I fed it and what I did with the audio afterward changed.

J: The Difference 14 Phrases Didn't See

The story of "nijde" being read by the machine as "nizhe" is in the first article. The reason was in rewriting letters: it had a fourth rule, дж → ж, and the model was literally fed the Russian word. The continuation is important here.

For the fix, I compared five ways to write this sound. On the 14 test phrases, the two best options, the regular дж and the Tatar letter җ, were indistinguishable: both corrected the word in three renders out of three. But choosing based on phrases was impossible for another reason. By removing the rule, I consciously diverged the model's input from how the text was written during training: during fine-tuning, the model never saw дж in this form. Therefore, the decision was made on the entire chapter. I re-voiced all 195 sentences with each candidate and compared them on raw files, before trimming, so that only the spelling differed:

Book: A Chapter-by-Chapter Pipeline

The remaining fifteen chapters went through the same pipeline, one chapter at a time: synthesis, assembly, breath cleaning, word time-stamping, selective timestamp verification, full back-reading, and packaging for the reader. Each chapter has its own log of stages, so a broken connection or a sleeping laptop costs one stage, not the book. I didn't re-voice the first chapter: its text, after all corrections, matched the previous one byte-for-byte. The entire book found things that a single chapter didn't, and it all turned out to be in text extraction. Three asterisks, used to separate scenes in the book, were passed to the model as text, and it honestly "read" them. The last paragraph of the novel, the author's date "1940 s.", was missed by the filter because it's not a bare number, and the novel ended with the year spoken aloud. And five sentences were broken in half where a paragraph ended with an ellipsis and continued in the next.

Assembly That Refused to Assemble

The reader format has a contract: words of a sentence, stitched back together with a single space, must yield the original sentence text byte-for-byte. The assembler checks this for each sentence and writes nothing if it's violated. In the seventh chapter, it refused. The book file had a double space within one sentence. The audio was fine, and nothing would have been visible on the screen. But the reader, which draws text word by word, would have silently lost this space. I fixed it at the source: extraction now collapses spaces (this affected the seventh and fourteenth chapters), and the seventh chapter was re-voiced with the corrected text. This was the second time a program that refused to work found something that no listening would have found.

At the same time, the order of operations changed. A triggered protection in one chapter no longer stops the queue: the error is recorded, the remaining chapters proceed, and the return code carries the error. A book that stopped overnight on the seventh chapter means a lost night.

Chapter IV, and Why an Average for the Book Would Be Wrong

I checked each chapter separately, and one looked bad: the CER for the fourth chapter was 8.28%, four times higher than the median for the book. I went to listen, and the chapter was normal. The entire error was caused by one two-word remark, "– dep tüşündi.": the verification recognizer got stuck and repeated it twenty-five times in a row, the CER for this single clip was 30.7, or over three thousand percent. Without it, the chapter had about 2% CER, like all the others. The same remark caused a check failure in the second chapter (4.44% → 2.50%), and there was another such glitch in the thirteenth. It wasn't the reading machine that erred, but the verification one, and precisely on a very short clip.

Reader: Alignment to Text Known by Synthesis

The book was needed not only as an audio file. I wanted a reader: text on the screen, voice reading, current word highlighted, clicking on a word transfers playback there.

This requires the time for each word, and here the technique that started the whole project came in handy: forced alignment, synchronizing audio with pre-known text. Only now, the known text is exactly what the synthesis received as input. I don't align the twelve-minute chapter file. Each sentence is aligned separately, with its own text, and then converted to the chapter's total time via a single offset table managed by the assembler. The aligner refuses to write anything if a sentence is missing, the word count doesn't match the text, time is not monotonic, a word ends beyond its own file, or the sentence table is misaligned with the chapter file.

Result for the entire book: 15,449 tokens on screen, 100% of words in all 16 chapters have timestamps, only dashes and non-pronounced symbols were left without marks. The sentence table matches the chapter file with 0–1 ms accuracy. 83 words out of 15,449 fell below the confidence threshold.

These questionable marks are not randomly scattered. Most of them are on the first word after a dash in a dialogue, and this is precisely where back-reading stumbles. Two independent instruments point to the same places. A low word score for me is now a reason to listen to the sentence, not to delete the mark.

The marks themselves were checked by a verification system that is bound to fail: short segments, cut precisely according to recorded times, are recognized, while the same segments shifted by 0.4 s are not. The figures for the book were in the first article; I won't repeat them.

Two more safeguards stem from past pitfalls. The first is from the story of time-stamping that drifted by 44 seconds by the end of the chapter (also first article). The same timeline was independently calculated by two pieces of code, and one of them accounted for the silence of clips twice: the very difference between target and actual pause. Now the timeline has one owner, and the assembler compares the end of the last sentence with the audio file length and refuses to work if they differ by more than half a second.

The second is from the дж story. I made the first reader assembly on the old voiceover, before the fix, and the only sign of this was that the voice said "nizhe." Now each chapter stores the SHA-256 of its audio file and the ID of the voiceover it was measured on. An outdated voiceover is visible from the data, not just by ear.

The reader works on ana-yurt.com, one page for both reading and listening. "Merdiven" can be opened and listened to in its entirety: ana-yurt.com/kutuphane/3444/read.

Why "Merdiven" specifically? I really love Shamil Aladin, and I wanted the first book read by the machine to be one of his works. Besides, the novel suited the style: straightforward narrative prose. Humorous stories or children's books, where vocal play is needed, the model was not yet ready to read.

Voice: The Sample Matters More Than You Think

Since the timbre is taken from a sample, choosing the voice comes down to choosing a few seconds of recording. And the sample turned out to be more important than I thought.

Pauses. I first tried my own voice on individual phrases and heard long, inappropriate pauses in the middle of sentences. I suspected the model, but the sample was to blame: in 10.72 s of recording, there was 0.77 s of silence before words and 1.77 s after. The model takes not only timbre from the sample but also tempo, i.e., how much time to spend on each letter. With such a sample, the clone read about 22% slower and distributed the extra time with pauses within phrases. I trimmed the silence at the edges, and the median of total pauses within a sentence dropped from 930 to 85 ms (27 sentences with 6 seeds), and the proportion of sentences with no internal pauses at all increased from 4% to 41%. Blind comparison of the old and trimmed sample, all else being equal, yielded 7:6, p = 1.000: indistinguishable by ear. For such a fix, this is an ideal outcome: the defect is gone, the voice remains.

Muffledness. Then the voice seemed muffled to me. The microphone was to blame: a lavalier mic on the chest is positioned to the side of the mouth and loses the 2–8 kHz band, which was missing, and the flat EQ didn't fix it. At the synthesis output, this is about 4.8 dB. You can't draw this back with filters: what the microphone didn't record isn't in the recording. I had to re-record the samples.

More Male Speech in Training. There was also an attempt to help the male voice with data: I added more male speech to the training materials. Blind comparison: the new version lost to the previous one 3:11 with nine draws, p = 0.057. Strictly speaking, this is not proof that it's worse. But there was no reason to keep it either, especially since the entire advantage was given by one voice, a female one, with a score of 7:1. I postponed the fundamental frequency measurement in advance and only revealed it after counting: for this same voice, the pitch shifted down, from 208 to 199 Hz. The ear and the instrument pointed to the same voice. I didn't keep this version.

Once I sorted out the pauses and the microphone, I decided to try my own voice for the entire book. Thus, the second book in our library – Yunus Qandym, "Kuresh meydanyny ot basmaz", 28 chapters, 43,701 words, 6 hours 29 minutes of playback – was read by my voice. It can also be listened to: ana-yurt.com/kutuphane/3516/read. It was read by the current version of the model, while "Merdiven" was read by an earlier one. You cannot compare versions based on these two books: the texts are different, and the voices are different.

I also chose this book not by chance. Its author, Yunus Qandym, is my uncle, and I really wanted his book to be voiced by my voice. Usually, when you hear your own voice in a recording, it sounds foreign and strange. Here, everything was different. When I heard the first sentence in my own voiceover, my happiness knew no bounds: the quality turned out to be very good.

What Hasn't Worked Yet

Stress

This story went in circles, and it's dearer to me than the others. I noticed that the name "Riza" was sometimes read by the machine as "Ryza." A check on eight different sentences for each name confirmed: "Riza" shifts to the back vowel in 4 out of 8 sentences, "Aziz" in 5 out of 8, and never in 16 control sentences (exact Fisher's test, p = 0.0066 and 0.0013). "Sabri" and "Dilyaver" have no defect at all.

A fix was also found: write "iy" instead of "i" at the input. The back vowel then disappeared completely, not a single case out of 16 for each name, and the CER of the name itself dropped: from 0.125 to 0.103 for "Riza," from 0.300 to 0.169 for "Aziz." The rest of the sentence remained unchanged, as it should. The instruments were pleased.

I listened blindly and did not accept it. The score was 4:3 in favor of the original, i.e., a coin toss, and I wrote in the note to the blind page: "if I hear I, then the stress is incorrect." The fix returned the correct vowel and at the same time shifted the stress to the first syllable, while ours is on the last. Both versions were incorrect, each in its own way.

I wasn't too upset. These are early models, and I allow such errors in them. I decided to return to this problem later.

I returned to it from another angle: I started measuring the stress itself, not the vowel. In our language, the last syllable of a word is usually stretched a bit, and live speakers stretch some words a lot, while others hardly at all. To make the comparison fair, the model read the same sentences from the training recordings as the speakers, so each word has a human pair with the same text.

On average, the model stretches the last syllable as much as humans do: 1.143 versus 1.156 for 125 pairs, p = 0.33. At this point, I could have stopped with the conclusion "everything is fine," and it would have been the wrong conclusion. If you build a regression of stretching in the model against stretching in humans for 22 "word × position in phrase" cells (in logarithms), the slope is 0.556 ± 0.115. The model reproduces only about six-tenths of the human range: it under-stretches the words that speakers stretch the most, and over-stretches the flat ones. The defect is not in the average, but in the compression.

The next suspect remained: how the model pre-determines the length of the entire phrase. For the same text, the total render length varies by only 0.46% from seed to seed. The worst cell is missing about 69 ms on the last vowel, which is about 1.4% of a five-second phrase, three times more than the freedom allowed by such a budget. If the length is predetermined, the last syllable can only be lengthened at the expense of neighboring ones. This is still a hypothesis, and testing it is already work with the model itself, not with the input text.

Breaths

Breath trimming never worsened anything: on the blind page, 8 draws, 2 in favor of the cleaned version, 0 against. But it doesn't resolve the complaint. Short breaths of 60–120 ms are poorly caught by the trimmer, 42% of them remain. My voice has the most breaths: in one measurement, 15.5% of the audio compared to 1.0% for Sevil. Trimming silence in the sample itself reduced the proportion of breaths by more than half, from 12.1% to 5.1%, but not to zero.

Pauses on Emotional Remarks

It seemed to me that where the text was more emotional, the machine made longer pauses. I checked this on 27 sentences in three emotionality bins, which were equalized for length and number of punctuation marks during the selection stage, with 6 seeds for each. There is no correlation with emotionality: ρ = +0.076, p = 0.70. Pauses follow sentence length (ρ = +0.886) and the number of punctuation marks within it (ρ = +0.658), and emotional phrases in prose are simply more often longer and richer in commas. Twenty-seven sentences exclude a strong effect; to catch a weak one, about 85 would be needed.

For me, the issue is still not closed. I could only measure the total duration of pauses, not where they are placed, and a misplaced pause in an exclamation is more jarring than the same pause at a comma.

Dialogues and Numbers

The model sees the dash before a remark, but reads a change of speaker as a new sentence, not a new voice. The difference is currently only a slightly longer pause when changing speakers.

I convert numbers to words in advance, but checking their pronunciation by back-reading is impossible: the recognizer records what it hears as numbers, and "1924" returns as "19124." Numbers are currently only checked by ear.

What I Learned From This Book

The voice turned out to be the most reliable part of the entire chain. Almost everything that broke was in the preparation of text and audio around the model: stitching, trimming, letters, time-stamping, double spaces. Some of this was found by instruments and safeguards, and the ear would never have found it: the real length threshold of 70 characters, the double space, the stuck verification in the fourth chapter. Some was found by ear, and the instrument couldn't even evaluate it: "nijde," stress in "Riza," breaths between words.

I drew two practical conclusions for myself. The test set is suitable for comparing versions, but I now make the decision on what to release based on the entire chapter. And the assembler, which can refuse to assemble, pays off: the double space in the seventh chapter would not have been found by any listening.

When I finished listening to the book, I felt proud: the work done was not in vain and yielded excellent results. But this is no reason to relax. There are still places in the book that need refinement, which means we still have a lot of work to do.
