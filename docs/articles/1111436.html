In November 2026, a wave of bans and verification requests (read: bans) swept through Claude users. People started losing their paid accounts mid-work, with even those using their own VPS and paying with personal foreign cards falling under the blocks.

To understand which combinations still work and what almost certainly leads to a ban, I surveyed nearly 300 subscribers to my channel. Among them, 189 people have already lost their accounts, and 108 continue to work. Below is what's really happening with the blocks, what actions trigger them, and how not to lose your work data.

In November, bans started requiring document verification

As early as the summer of 2026, Claude almost always closed accounts without any explanation. You'd just get an email saying, sorry, friend, but we won't let you work.

But in the fall, instead of bans, people started receiving verification requests. In September and October, documents were requested from more than half of those banned, and in November, from everyone without exception.

The mechanism is simple: the service requires you to confirm your identity via Persona or age via Yoti, asking you to upload a passport or take a selfie. Most of those who submitted documents were banned immediately, and some others a few weeks later. Those who refused verification could download their old chats. But they were not allowed to communicate with the neural networks.

Naturally, verification requests only come for paid accounts (well, few people use the free ones).

A personal VPS doesn't protect from bans

Everyone around (including me) said that the best protection is to buy a rented server with a dedicated IP, install Claude there, and not worry.

But it turned out that's not the case. My channel's audience consists of advanced users, so most subscribers were indeed using VPS. In the survey, users of personal VPS were banned even more often than those using a regular VPN – 71% versus 65%. Only those who constantly changed VPNs, countries, and browsers were banned more frequently.

I don't think the problem is with VPS itself. It's more likely a coincidence due to the audience.

Personal servers have two weak points. Firstly, regular VPS are located in data centers, and their IP addresses are easily distinguishable from those of home internet providers. Secondly, a server doesn't protect against accidental leaks. One participant on the Pro plan had their personal VPS freeze for a couple of seconds – the built-in killswitch didn't have time to cut off the traffic, and the account received a verification request. For others, requests were sent from a Russian IP when they reconnected a frozen VPN right in the middle of a task.

Another common mistake is configuring VPN bypass for Russian websites (split-tunneling). One user set up this mode for local services but accidentally routed auxiliary Anthropic domains, which artifacts use, outside the VPN.

In general, the best way to avoid a ban without the hassle of VPS and VPN is to actually change your location ;) Among my subscribers, only one person out of twenty living abroad was blocked.

Your own foreign card is also not a guarantee

This is also an interesting thing: many wrote that virtual cards are nonsense and you need to pay with plastic.

On the one hand, this is true. People who paid with virtual or crypto cards and used VPN/VPS were banned in 4 out of 5 cases.

On the other hand, plastic cardholders also got hit. Their owners were blocked in about 7 out of 10 cases. Few paid through the App Store and Google Play, but most lost their accounts there too.

Owners of personal cards most often linked the ban to a mismatch between the country of payment and login. For example, a subscription was paid with a Singaporean card, but they logged in through Poland or the USA, and even with an old web session from Great Britain. There were also strange cases: one person lost access after paying with a friend's virtual card, even though the friend's account continued to work.

Which plans get banned more often

And another popular myth is that only the $200 Max subscription gets banned, and others are left alone. Not true ;)

In terms of ban numbers in my survey, the regular $20 Pro plan leads – simply because most people use it. But if you look at the proportion of blocks within each plan, the risk increases with the price: on the free plan, slightly more than a third of VPN and proxy users were banned, on Pro – two-thirds, and on Max 5x ($100) and Max 20x ($200) – already three-quarters.

How a person works with the neural network also significantly affects this:

  • Only regular chat: 56% of paid users were banned.

  • Claude Code: among those who work through Anthropic's console agent, 76% were blocked.

  • High load: for those who ran agents in multiple windows and frequently hit limits, the ban rate reached 72%.

  • Third-party programs and API: here, about 60–67% of participants were blocked.

At the same time, some VPN and VPS users are still working without a single verification request – there were 39 such people. Mostly, these are Pro plan owners with old accounts (over six months old), although among them are those who calmly use Claude Code.

What happened right before the ban

A quarter of those banned noticed nothing unusual – they worked as usual and lost access. For the rest, something from the following list happened before verification or ban:

  • Network failure or change. One participant restarted a poorly working VPN five times while Claude was performing a task, and immediately received a verification request.

  • Payment issue. Another participant didn't have enough money on their card for the automatic debit. The subscription lapsed, she topped up her account and paid manually – immediately after that, the service asked for her passport.

  • New device. One respondent had worked only from a computer for a year and a half, and a couple of days after the first login from a phone, received a verification. Another participant connected a second laptop for a minor task – before that, she even asked Claude itself if it was possible, and received an answer that there were no restrictions.

  • Russian context. A user of Max 5x with the Cowork agent was working in the nalog.ru personal account before the ban. Another participant was looking for a clinic in Moscow 10 minutes before verification. Another person connected a Russian financial service via MCP and was immediately banned without verification. A VPS owner in Poland with a Singaporean card had "entrepreneur from Belarus" written in their profile.

  • Settings change and discussion of verifications. One participant, before being blocked, sent a screenshot of a verification request from another account to the chat. Another participant disabled Anthropic data transfer in the settings the same day.

Ban emails rarely explain anything. Most received a standard boilerplate about suspicious activity or violation of the Usage Policy. Unsupported country was mentioned for about one in eight, and in Claude Code and API, sometimes it just said organization has been disabled.

What happens if you try to pass verification

If you submit a Russian or Belarusian passport, the account will almost certainly be closed: in the survey, 82% of such attempts ended in an immediate ban or a ban in the following days, and some accounts were closed later.

With foreign documents – your own, a friend's, or through intermediaries – the chances are much higher, but still without guarantees: slightly less than half of such accounts continued to work after verification.

Even successful verification doesn't solve the problem forever. One participant passed the first verification with a Russian internal passport and worked fine for three weeks, but on a subsequent request, was immediately banned. Another created a new account with foreign documents and their own VPS: after a month, they passed the first verification, and eight days later, they received another one. There's also a looping bug: a person uploaded a Russian international passport, the service processed it, and showed the verification window again – there's no ban message, but the account cannot be used.

Why are they banned at all?

In the Chinese Claude community, a theory about a hidden penalty system is popular – it's already nicknamed "social credit." The idea is that upon registration, an account receives a reserve of trust points. Some actions replenish it (old account, working through desktop or mobile app, enabled memory), others deduct from it (IP change, multiple devices, purchasing an expensive plan immediately after registration, virtual cards, Linux, constantly hitting limits, and active work with the new flagship model).

When the rating drops to a threshold, the account is put under observation. Chinese users consider a sudden authorization reset to be the main warning sign – if you change your card, IP, or switch to Max at this moment, a verification request will come almost immediately.

Much of this aligns with my survey: virtual cards, VPN changes, and high load indeed resulted in the highest ban rates. Four participants specifically mentioned Fable 5.2 – two actively burned through weekly limits on it, and a third had it check a project immediately after renewing their subscription and received verification (later managed to get the account back through an appeal). A fourth actively used Fable and once hit the five-hour limit before the ban.

Stories about session resets also matched. One respondent was logged out of all web sessions the day before their subscription ended, and after re-logging in, a verification appeared. Another participant, after two bans, has kept a third account for five months: they only use the desktop application and a bit of Claude Code, don't log in from a phone or browser, and don't turn off their VPN at all.

The data only diverged on one point: the Chinese community believes that language and country mentions in correspondence have no effect, while in my survey, several people received verification immediately after tasks with a Russian context.

What happens after a ban

Getting an account back is almost impossible. Out of 110 people who filed an appeal, only four had their access restored – and three of them were banned before the November wave of verifications.

The situation with money has also worsened. In the summer, Anthropic refunded payments to more than half of those who applied, but in November – only to a few. Most did not see their money back, although several users managed to get it back through their bank, Apple, or Google.

The good news is that chat histories can usually be saved: 54 people managed to export their chats, while 17 lost their history. After the ban, a third of those banned moved to other services – Codex, Gemini, OpenRouter, and similar. Another 40 people created new accounts and continue to work, but 28 participants have already managed to get banned a second time.

What to do right now

Neither your own VPS nor a foreign card guarantees that Claude won't ask for your passport tomorrow. Therefore, it's better to take precautions in advance:

  • Don't store work materials only in chats. Keep instructions, prompts, and files locally or in git. One participant on Max 20x, after receiving a verification request, calmly switched to Codex the same day – their entire knowledge base was already on their computer.

  • If you receive a verification request – retrieve your data first. Before uploading your passport, export important correspondence, files, and instructions from your projects. After submitting documents, access to the account may be closed instantly.

  • Don't mess with payment and login schemes. Top up your card in advance so your subscription doesn't lapse, don't jump between VPN countries, and don't log in from new devices unnecessarily.

  • Prepare a backup tool. Transfer your main instructions to Codex, Gemini, or another service in advance so that losing your account doesn't stop your work.
