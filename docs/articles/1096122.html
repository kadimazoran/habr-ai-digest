Artificial intelligence has driven chip sales to their fastest growth since the 1980s, with TSMC and Samsung reporting record profits, according to The Economist.

However, chipmaker stocks have fallen more than 10% this month as investors question how long the boom will last. The downturn may be milder than the boom: few firms make cutting-edge chips, they fund expansion with cash rather than debt, and take-or-pay contracts shift some risk to buyers.

Two laws of the semiconductor industry

For more than four decades, the semiconductor industry has been governed by two laws as reliable as any in physics. The first is that the number of transistors, the tiny switches etched onto a chip, grows relentlessly. The second is that every boom is followed by a bust.

The first is working beautifully: powerful artificial-intelligence processors contain more than 200bn transistors, and the industry reckons that number could reach a trillion by the end of the decade. The second is what is worrying investors about how long this upswing will last.

The scale of the boom

The industry is used to booms, but not like this one. AI has created an insatiable appetite for the chips used to train and run AI models. In the first half of 2026, semiconductor sales grew faster than at any time since the 1980s.

This month TSMC, the world’s biggest contract chipmaker, reported a sixth consecutive quarter of record profits, and Samsung Electronics, the South Korean chipmaking giant, reported a fourth successful quarter in a row.

Yet investors are starting to get nervous. Despite the impressive results and upbeat forecasts, an index of leading semiconductor firms has fallen by more than 10% since October 6th. Their fears were not helped when, on October 12th, Hock Tan, the boss of Broadcom, a big chip designer, compared many AI investors to gamblers in a casino who “have no idea” what they are investing in.

Consolidation: four factories for the world

Even as the chip industry has grown, manufacturing has become far more concentrated. Most chip designers, such as Nvidia, whose graphics processors have powered the AI boom, do not make their own silicon wafers. Nor do software outfits, such as Google and OpenAI, which have also started designing AI processors.

Instead, they rely on foundries, and overwhelmingly on just one. In the early 2000s, about 25 outfits could make the most advanced processors. Today, just four can, and TSMC makes almost all the high-performance ones.

Memory chips, which are also vital for AI, have followed the same path. In 1990, more than 20 outfits made advanced memory chips. Today, almost all are made by just three – Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron. China has introduced new players in both processor and memory manufacturing. But lacking access to the most advanced chipmaking tools, it still lags behind.

Capital expenditure: record-breaking, but affordable

Consolidation has allowed a few dominant chipmakers to set the pace of expansion and dictate terms of sale. They are investing heavily to profit from this boom.

TSMC’s capital expenditure is expected to reach about $62bn this year, more than 1.5 times its 2025 figure. Analysts reckon it will reach about $76bn next year. Memory makers have also increased their investment.

Still, memory makers’ spending is small relative to the windfall profits from AI. TSMC’s capital expenditure as a percentage of revenue is expected to fall from 44% in 2023 to 36% this year. What’s more, the investment is being paid for with cash generated by chipmakers, not borrowed money. This year TSMC’s capacity expansion will swallow just over half its operating cashflow, and Samsung’s barely a fifth. The debts of these chipmakers look modest compared with their growing cash piles.

Chipmakers are enjoying “very favourable pricing conditions” and have every incentive to accelerate expansion to preserve them, says Bill Wiseman, of McKinsey, a consultancy. DRAM, a type of memory used in almost all electronics, costs about six times as much as it did in early 2023. The pace of capacity expansion is being held back, says Mr Wiseman, by the fact that many new chip plants are being built in America, where construction takes “about twice as long”.

Take-or-pay contracts: shifting risk to buyers

Consolidation has also given chipmakers more leverage in contract negotiations. Until recently, memory was sold mostly on the spot market or via short-term contracts. When demand fell, customers were not obliged to keep buying, and so prices collapsed.

Now, with capacity tight and few suppliers to choose from, buyers are signing contracts years in advance and on sellers’ terms. Nvidia has committed more than $250bn to secure supplies and components until January 2029.

In September Micron said that take-or-pay agreements, under which customers must either buy agreed volumes or pay up regardless, had locked in 35% of its revenue until 2030, with $32bn in commitments, most of which include cash deposits. Such prepayments help finance new factories. They also shift at least some of the risk of a crash on to buyers.

What this means for the industry and investors

The end of the AI boom will still bring chipmakers plenty of pain. If spending stalls, prices will fall, profits will shrink, and sky-high stock prices will return to earth. Customers will seek to renegotiate contracts signed at the market’s peak.

The second law of the semiconductor industry still holds. This time, though, the hangover will be shared by all the customers.

For tech companies, this means a few practical takeaways:

Reliance on a narrow band of suppliers. If you are building a product on cutting-edge chips, you depend on four factories, and primarily TSMC. Diversifying supply is a challenge that requires planning years ahead.

Long-term contracts as a tool. Take-or-pay contracts shift risk to the buyer. For chip-buying companies, this means commitments are locked in for years, and renegotiating terms in a downturn will be difficult.

Cyclicality is here to stay. The semiconductor industry is cyclical. Booms are followed by busts. The question is not whether it will happen, but when and how painful it will be.

Conclusion

The semiconductor industry is enjoying its fastest growth since the 1980s. TSMC and Samsung are reporting record profits, capital expenditure is rising, and contracts are being locked in for years.

But investors are nervous – and for good reason. Market consolidation means a few players control manufacturing, and take-or-pay contracts shift some risk to buyers. The second law of the semiconductor industry still holds: every boom is followed by a bust.

The question is not whether a correction will happen. It is how painful it will be, and who will be left standing when the hangover passes.
