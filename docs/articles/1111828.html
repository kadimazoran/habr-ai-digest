Step 1. Understand the Topic and Agree with the Supervisor

A student from another department approached me: by the middle of the semester, they couldn't reproduce a vulnerability for the testbed, even though it was explicitly stated in the topic. Replacing it was difficult; they didn't finish the work on time and had to retake the exam. I never fully figured out the reason for their failure. Therefore, it's worth agreeing on a topic that defines a problem and conditions, leaving the choice of solution after a comparative analysis. For example, a class of vulnerabilities and the purpose of the testbed, rather than a single specific vulnerability.

At our department, the work must be completed. Therefore, we check in advance that we can conduct an experiment and obtain data. Whether the hypothesis is confirmed and the method turns out to be better than others is a separate question. A negative answer can be a meaningful result; a non-working testbed doesn't provide such an answer on its own.

Check Several Topics Before Choosing One

Start with a list of topics and previous students' work:

  1. Choose a few areas you want to understand.

  2. Ask the AI to explain the task in simple terms and provide an example of its application.

  3. Find the first real sources and figure out what data and tools are needed.

  4. Clarify with the instructor what needs to be compared and what result to achieve within the allotted time.

Next, we'll work through one task: a camera is filming a parking lot, and we need to identify free spots. Using this example, we'll go through finding articles, comparing models, and selecting quality and processing time metrics.

For preliminary verification, ask the agent to find available data and implementations, match requirements with your hardware, and suggest a minimal example for testing. If the necessary data or tools are unavailable, this is a reason to discuss the topic with your supervisor before starting the main work.

First Contact with the Supervisor

Before the meeting, write down in your own words how you understand the task and prepare questions: what are we comparing, and who needs the result? Clarify four things with your supervisor:

  1. What problem are we solving, and under what conditions should the solution work?

  2. What are the limitations: hardware, data, software environment, deadlines, requirements for the result?

  3. What is given in advance, and what methods and technologies can be chosen independently?

  4. What needs to be achieved and demonstrated for the work to be considered complete?

You must bring a notebook and pen to the meeting. Or record it on your phone. Note down the task and the next step, rephrase it in your own words, and clarify anything unclear. After the meeting, save the decisions and questions in your project and discuss them with the agent.

At the defense, they might ask: "Why did you choose PostgreSQL?" The answer "because the neural network suggested it" won't help. If data requirements, compatibility, and deployment are specified in advance, you can show a comparison based on them. Limitations are needed before the choice, otherwise, it will be a post-hoc justification.

Example prompt for the agent at this stage:

Analyze the options for the topic "..." under the constraints "...". Find sources, available data, and implementations, and match the requirements with my resources. Suggest a minimal feasibility check and note potential obstacles. Prepare questions for the supervisor and topic formulations that allow for a choice of solution after comparative analysis.

Now the Student Has Their Own Junior

In an ideal world, a Doctor of Science divides the task among candidates, who then divide it among diploma students and coursework students. This used to work well: "Study the approach, find a bunch of articles, prepare a summary." Now, an agent performs such tasks. So why do we need juniors then?)) A question familiar from commercial development.

Therefore, consider a 2nd or 3rd-year student as a senior paired with their AI junior. They set the task, provide materials and criteria, and then check the result.

In the report "Master's Student vs. Claude Code: Who to Give the Hypothesis for Verification?" at Turbo ML Conf, Vlad Kurenkov discusses how a student becomes a researcher: they complete tasks, run experiments, gain intuition, and gradually learn to ask questions themselves (slides). In my opinion, it's the same in development: by doing projects, you learn to choose technical solutions.

Where to get expertise if you're just entering the field yourself? Analyze articles, ask for explanations of methods with examples, and ask questions. Do you need to Google yourself? I don't know. Before, you had to master search tricks; now, the agent quickly iterates through queries and finds materials. But you definitely need to read the original articles carefully: conditions and limitations get lost in summaries.

It's useful to switch roles:

Ask me questions about my work one by one. After my answer, show me what I explained inaccurately and suggest which part of the materials to re-read. Also, ask about related approaches and the reasons why I didn't choose them.

And be sure to give the work to the agent for review:

Reread my work. Find errors, inconsistencies, contradictions, and unsubstantiated conclusions. Compare it with the sources. First, provide your remarks, indicating the fragment and the reason.

If the only answer I can give is "that's what the agent wrote," I need to delve deeper. By the next meeting with the supervisor, I should have read articles, considered solutions, and formulated specific questions.

For a one-time task, a prompt and verification are sufficient. A skill can save the procedure for preparing an MR (merge request): check coursework changes against requirements, perform project checks, and prepare a description of the result. Permanent requirements for commits, pushes, and MR formatting should be written in AGENTS.md.

A skill can be connected automatically based on the task description. Its name and brief description are included in the context beforehand, and the full instruction is loaded when the skill is selected. Inside, there can be scripts, templates, and example results: they help define the sequence of actions, checks, and the format of the abstract.

A separate skill is not needed for reading local PDFs: modern agents select available tools themselves. You no longer need to suggest library names as before. A skill helps to solidify the analysis procedure.

Save Materials and Work Rules

In README.md, record the task, constraints, current status, and launch commands. Ask the agent to read the file and rephrase the task: it's better to notice discrepancies before starting work.

Store the history of text and code in Git. For transferred documents, use a version in the name; for large external data, use a link and version in README.md.

With a student budget, you might want to share subscriptions. But a shared account violates the service's rules: you could lose access. Local Codex has a session history on the computer, and I save materials as regular files. Keep articles, notes, and code in the project folder: even if account access is lost, these files will remain on your computer.

Friends also told me that when you reach the limit, you can change your account :) You shouldn't do that, I personally don't, and I know you won't either.

Recurring requirements for the agent can be placed in AGENTS.md at the root of the project. A minimal example:

Research materials are in this project folder. Original articles and translations are stored in papers/. Verified abstracts and my observations are collected in papers/papers.md. Numbers from publications should be accompanied by a link to the file, page, and table. My own measurements should be accompanied by a link to the launch results. If a value or condition is not specified, state so. Do not add based on assumptions. Separate the source authors' results from assumptions and my conclusions. Before writing a section, propose a plan with theses and sources.

In nested AGENTS.md files, you can store requirements for text or code check commands. You don't need to list them in the root file. When launched, Codex CLI reads instructions from the repository root to the working folder; it doesn't scan all subfolders in advance. Launch it from the correct folder or explicitly instruct it to read additional rules. After changing instructions, start a new session and ask it to list the read AGENTS.md files with their paths.

Step 3. Find, Install, and Test a Skill

We look for ready-made skills on skills.sh and delegate the selection to find-skills. I'll show the path from installation to testing on a scientific article.

Install find-skills in the Project

Dependency preparation and installation can be delegated to the agent:

Check for the presence and compatibility of Node.js, npm, and npx versions. If anything is missing, help install it. Then, install find-skills from vercel-labs/skills into this project for Codex. Verify that the skill is available and show the path to SKILL.md.

If dependencies are already present, run in the terminal from the project folder:

npx skills add vercel-labs/skills --skill find-skills --agent codex
npx skills list --agent codex

Confirm the download of the skills utility if npx prompts you. --skill selects the skill, --agent codex selects the agent; the second command lists installed skills. The project will get a .agents/skills/find-skills/ directory. If Codex doesn't see it, restart the application and open the same project.

Assign a Specific Task to find-skills

Now, in the Codex chat, type:

Select a skill for parsing a PDF of a scientific article.
I need pages, tables, experimental conditions, and contradictions.
Show several options, links to their SKILL.md, required tools, and installation commands in this project for Codex.
Explain which option is suitable for a single PDF and why.

In Codex CLI and IDE, $ explicitly selects a skill; in CLI, /skills opens the skill list. In other interfaces, type "Use find-skills." The first test is easier with an explicit call.

Select and Install the Found Skill

The selection of "best skills for science" will quickly become outdated, so I delegate the search to you and the agent. Open the suggested SKILL.md files: are they suitable for the downloaded PDF, do they require external search and additional services? Popularity does not replace result verification.

Choose a suitable skill and instruct the agent to install it in the project. For reading verification, provide the exact path to the PDF; if the file is unavailable, the agent should explicitly state this.

Test the Skill on a Familiar PDF

Place a familiar article in papers/ and invoke the chosen skill:

Use the selected skill.
Parse papers/001_author_original.pdf for my task: [task description].
Read the full text. Extract the method, data, equipment, metrics, and limitations. For results, indicate pages and tables.
Compare the authors' conclusions with the numbers and experimental conditions.
If information is not provided, state so.
Separately show what is applicable to my task and what still needs to be checked.

Compare the numbers, pages, conditions, and known weaknesses with the original. If you get an abstract without pages and conditions, clarify the task and repeat the test.

If the Task is Repetitive – Write a Skill

If you often tell the agent "do this, then this, check it like this," it's worth considering a skill. For example, for an abstract with pages, experimental conditions, and your own observations. In the skill description, specify which tasks it is intended for.

For your task, start with the skill-creator from your agent's provider, if such a skill is available. Describe what is repetitive, what materials are provided as input, and what result is expected. It will help clarify requirements, assemble the skill, and prepare tests.

Below is an educational example to better understand the skill structure. For Codex, its folder in the project will be .agents/skills/research-notes/, and the SKILL.md file might look like this:

--- 
name: research-notes
description: Parsing a full PDF of a scientific article for a literature review. Use for preparing an abstract with verifiable results.
---

1. Check the title, authors, and publication version.
2. If the full text cannot be read, report it.
3. Open references/note-template.md relative to this skill's folder.
   Fill in the abstract using this template.
4. For numbers and conclusions, indicate the page, table, or figure.
5. Compare the conclusions in the text with the tables.
6. Separate the authors' results from your assumptions.
7. List the parts that require human verification.
8. Save the abstract to papers/papers.md, without deleting previous notes.

The template in references/note-template.md could be:

# 001 · Title, Authors, Year, DOI, Link
Authors' Task:
Method and what it was compared against:

Results: numbers, units, pages, and tables:

What is applicable to my topic and what else needs to be checked:

For the first test in Codex CLI or IDE, invoke $research-notes and provide a familiar PDF; in other interfaces, explicitly ask to use the skill. Additional examples and scripts can be added as needed.

Verify That the Skill is Helpful

Tests for skill usefulness are called evals: they require tasks and pre-known expected results.

  1. Prepare several familiar PDFs and write down what the agent should find in them: specific numbers, conditions, known contradictions.

  2. For a control run, use a copy of the project outside the original repository, without the skill being tested. In a new session, check the list of available skills: the desired one should not be picked up from parent or user directories. Then, assign the task.

  3. In another new session, perform the same task with an explicit skill invocation. The model, source files, notes, and task formulation should be identical, without the results of the previous run.

  4. Compare the numbers, pages, and completeness of the response. Record omissions and errors in a separate project file, e.g., evals/checks.md. For the educational research-notes, you can use .agents/skills/research-notes/evals/checks.md.

  5. Modify the instruction and repeat the test, including on an article not used when writing the skill.

Example of verifiable expectations for an article about YOLO, which we will analyze below:

- PDF page 9 and table 2 are indicated.
- For YOLOv5 at the 25th epoch, precision values of 1 and 0.99497 are listed.
- For YOLOv8, precision values of 0.99933 and 0.98955 are listed.
- No overall winner is declared based on a single precision metric.

One successful response proves little. Repeat the tests; separately test the automatic skill selection for a suitable query.

Step 4. Collect Articles and Verified Notes on Them

"The truth of this world is stored in PDFs" – I heard this phrase from my academic advisor. He, in turn, read it somewhere; I don't know the original author. Many current scientific works are available in PDF. Articles in peer-reviewed journals undergo expert review, which increases trust in the work but does not eliminate errors. A preprint may not have yet passed such a review. The value of a publication lies in understanding the methodology, conditions, and basis for the conclusion. The PDF format itself guarantees nothing: it can also contain a promotional brochure.

For an introduction to a topic, monographs, reviews, and Wikipedia with primary sources are useful. To justify a method, you need to open the publication itself and understand its status. DOI identifies the work but does not replace reading.

If I find an article, I save it, assign a number, translate it if necessary, read it, and write down questions. For example, a library might look like this:

papers/
├── 001_author_original.pdf
├── 001_author_ru.pdf
├── 002_author_original.pdf
├── 002_author_ru.pdf
└── papers.md

In papers/papers.md, there is a table and notes by number. For each, you need the method, conditions, results with page numbers, limitations, and applicability to my task. The research-notes template above is suitable.

We compare numbers, formulas, and controversial statements against the original. If "under these conditions" is omitted in the summary, the conclusion becomes much broader.

Find Articles and Save the Full Text

  1. Formulate a query from the object and action, including in English. For free parking spots, try parking occupancy detection comparison, then refine the query based on the found works.

  2. If you have the title or a fragment of an article, find the authors and DOI. Search for the title in quotes with filetype:pdf.

  3. Look for the full text from the publisher or author, on arXiv, CyberLeninka, or through available versions on Google Scholar.

  4. Open the PDF and check the title, authors, and completeness of the text. Save the file in papers/, and record the link and source information.

The search can be delegated to the agent. The result should be the articles themselves with source information; a list of titles is not enough.

For searching with an agent, there are scientific services, such as Consensus. They expand the selection but do not guarantee a complete review and do not replace checking the articles.

Find Information Within Your Library

Once the PDFs are collected, give the agent access to the folder and set a specific task:

Find articles in the papers folder that compare the methods I need. Indicate the file, page, and table. Extract the metrics along with the conditions: data, equipment, settings, and units of measurement. Save the findings in papers/papers.md. If only an abstract is available, note it separately.

If an article is not yet clear, discuss it in short iterations:

Explain the task using my example and clarify what is missing. Here are the source materials: [files or links]. Separate facts from assumptions. For verifiable statements, indicate the source and location. Now, find the weaknesses in your answer and ask me questions about the topic.

Your Abstract First, Then Analysis with the Agent

For key sources, I would go through this cycle:

  1. Read the article and independently note down ideas, limitations, and questions.

  2. Give the agent the same PDF and the task description: "What is useful here for my topic and why?" You can explicitly invoke the selected skill.

  3. Compare the two analyses: what I noticed, what the agent found, what we both missed.

  4. Verify controversial points against the original and save the result in papers/papers.md, adding your own observations.

A brief summary helps recall the article, but the applicability of the method to our task needs to be justified separately.

Step 5. Set Criteria and Conduct a Comparison

A metric describes a property of a solution: number of errors, memory consumption, processing time. You can separately evaluate the useful result per unit of cost, for example, the number of correctly recognized spots per second. A criterion, however, sets the rule for selection based on metrics.

Let's return to the parking lot. The procedure is as follows:

  1. Describe the scenario: we need to identify free parking spots from video.

  2. Clarify what we are detecting and which error is worse: failing to notice a free spot or incorrectly identifying a occupied spot as free.

  3. Set constraints: for example, what delay is acceptable for receiving the answer.

  4. Select quality and cost metrics, then write down the selection rule.

  5. Explain your choice and agree on it with your supervisor.

The agent can suggest criteria options. For example: maximize F1, and in case of a tie, choose the shorter processing time. Or, set a minimum acceptable F1 and find the fastest among the suitable options. These are different rules, and they might yield different winners.

Precision shows the proportion of correct detections among all detections, recall shows the proportion of target objects found among all target objects. F1 is their harmonic mean. For a specific task, you also need to define what constitutes a target object and a correct detection. Without this, even a familiar metric name does not make the results comparable.

The selection rule should be fixed before reviewing the final results. If the difference is small, check the variance between runs and the accuracy of measurements. The coincidence of rounded F1 values in a table does not inherently mean equal quality of models.

When Two Table Rows Cannot Be Directly Compared

In one article, the model was tested on clear frames and a powerful graphics card. In another, on less clear video and weaker hardware. You can put the numbers into one table, but you can't attribute the entire difference to the models themselves.

Alongside the metrics, you need the conditions: data, hardware, settings, units, and measurement method. If the conditions are incompatible, note it or repeat the comparison in a common environment.

Task for the agent:

Compare the selected works based on the agreed metrics. For each value, indicate the file, page, and conditions under which it was obtained. Find differences in data and hardware. If data is missing, write "not specified." Separately show which conclusions can be drawn from the sources and for which a personal experiment is needed.

After preparing the table, ask it to review the comparative analysis itself:

Compare the comparative analysis with papers/papers.md and the original PDFs. Check the definitions of metrics and units. Find comparisons made under different data or hardware. Does the choice of method follow from the given criterion? For each remark, indicate the text fragment, source, and reason. First, provide a list of remarks and questions.

We review the remarks against the sources, fix the choice, and identify questions that still require data.

Step 6. Transition from Requirements to Implementation and Experimentation

Input data, constraints, and expected results are formalized into a specification before implementation begins. This approach is called Specification-Driven Development, or SDD. In my work, I used create-specification. An error in the requirements easily turns into meticulously executed unnecessary work.

Workflow:

  1. Using the installed create-specification or a regular prompt, formalize the input data, constraints, and expected result.

  2. Read the specification and correct it before starting implementation.

  3. If there are several changes, create a short plan. For minor edits, a separate plan file is not necessary.

  4. Set the agent a goal: implement the agreed requirements, perform checks, and save commands and results.

Before the experiment, we record what we are changing, what we are comparing it with, what conditions we are maintaining, and what we are measuring. We save commands, code and dependency versions, parameters, data, and initial results. In case of randomness, we record its settings and repeat the runs.

In one experiment with a supercomputer job queue, we wanted to compare two processing order variants. On the simulator testbed, two jobs were supposed to arrive simultaneously. In the source file, they were assigned the same time, but when launched, they arrived in the system at different seconds. The second variant of the algorithm was not run then: first, the job submission needed to be fixed. Therefore, we check what happened in the system and only then draw a conclusion on the research question.

For a Ph.D. thesis, the question of scientific contribution remains: what is new, and how is it justified? Simply using an agent does not answer this.

Step 7. Write the Text According to the Agreed Plan

Prepare a plan for the comparative analysis section. For each point, indicate the thesis and the materials it is based on. Note where data is still lacking and ask me questions. Do not write the section itself yet.

After agreeing on the plan, we work paragraph by paragraph. For example:

  1. "Write the first paragraph according to the plan."

  2. "Remove general phrases, explain the term, keep this fact."

  3. "Rewrite only this paragraph and show the changes."

  4. "Now, the next paragraph."

This makes it easier to discuss the presentation than editing ten pages at once. Accepted requirements are saved in AGENTS.md.

When the fragments are satisfactory, we assemble the section: good paragraphs individually might repeat or contradict each other.

For the next pass, I set a separate task:

Review the section against the agreed plan, papers/papers.md, and the original PDFs. Is each thesis covered? Have my corrections been saved? Are there any repetitions, contradictions, or conclusions beyond the data? Are all numbers and references verifiable? What questions remain unanswered? For each remark, indicate the fragment, source, and reason. First, provide a list of remarks, then we will agree on a new version.

After corrections, I read the section as a whole: the agent's review does not replace the author's reading.

Step 8. Present the Results and Questions to the Supervisor

It's useful to approach your supervisor with a project solution. Before the meeting, gather:

  1. A brief description of what has been done since the last conversation and what conclusion has emerged.

  2. papers/papers.md with verified abstracts and your remarks.

  3. The corresponding section of the text, parameters, and experimental results.

  4. Slides that explain the same idea, if you are preparing for a defense.

  5. Specific questions and proposed solutions.

Instead of "What should I do next?" it's more useful to say: "I compared based on these requirements, I propose this option, I have doubts about this limitation.".

We discuss difficulties immediately: what was checked, why it's not working, and what options remain.

As a supervisor, it's important to me that the student can explain their solution and defend it. In the parking lot example, I would ask why this particular model was chosen, what its result in the table means, and on what video it was tested. This is where everything saved during the work comes in handy.

With an agent, you can reach such a conversation faster: gather sources, prepare experiments, and discuss weaknesses. But you still have to understand the results yourself. If, by the defense, the student can independently spot an agent's error and correct their solution, that's a good result for a coursework or diploma in my eyes.

It's interesting to see how universities will change their requirements for coursework, diplomas, and defenses if part of the work can now be delegated to an AI agent.
