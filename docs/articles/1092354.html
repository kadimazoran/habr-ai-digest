1. The Pace of Change

People in IT are no strangers to change. In our lifetime, the internet went from a novelty to something taken for granted, the mobile revolution and smartphones took off, clouds ceased to be a niche, Go and Rust emerged, and frameworks like React for the web and Jetpack Compose for Android appeared.

And yet, nothing compares to how profoundly and rapidly AI is changing the industry now. Here's how industry veteran Martin Fowler put it at The Pragmatic Summit:

“Nothing has ever hit this hard before. This is a different order of magnitude than anything we've encountered before.

On a smaller scale, we were actively involved in the spread of object-oriented languages. Many were scared of them, but we weren't particularly, because we were inside that process.

The internet had a huge impact on all of us. And, of course, we promoted agile. [Agile] changed many organizations very significantly; you could see it even by how desperately they resisted.

But [with all these changes] we had to constantly explain how important and valuable they were and convince people. It might sound strange now, but even about the internet, there were those who didn't consider it important!

With AI, no one argues whether it's important. You can't put on blinders and pretend this thing means nothing.”

I think along the same lines. We are clearly at the beginning of a massive technological shift, after which software will be made differently than in recent decades, although some aspects of development will remain the same. At the very least, the tools and best practices will change, and everything is changing faster than ever.

2. What Has Changed?

#1. Almost No One Writes Code by Hand Anymore

We are already in Q4, and it's evident that since late 2025, when models significantly improved in programming, this has become a mega-trend. In the very first article of this year, we asked ourselves what will happen to the profession when AI writes almost all the code.

Now, much suggests that most engineers have stopped writing code by hand. It's telling that even the creator of Ruby on Rails recently started a debate about the "death of manual programming." I myself am inspired by the new capabilities of AI and am interested in observing this revolution from within. As I wrote in that analysis, my own way of writing code in 2026 will surely change drastically, and there will be many consequences.

More on this in a recent issue of The Pulse.

#2. Multiple Agents in Parallel Are Becoming the Norm

I love talking to engineers from AI labs: their work habits are often months ahead of the rest of the industry. Boris Cherny, the creator of Claude Code, shared on the podcast how he works now:

“I have five terminal tabs open, each with its own repository checkout. I go in circles and run Claude Code in each. And in parallel with local ones, I have another 5-10 Claudes in the web version.”

And a few months later, on last week's podcast, Peter Mattis, co-founder of Cockroach Labs, shared something similar. He's an exceptionally productive developer who, even before AI, wrote around 100,000 lines of code per year:

“I often do multiple things in parallel. My cognitive limit is around 5-10 simultaneous agent sessions. But sometimes, within those sessions, a bunch of sub-agents are running.”

I also asked a former colleague from Uber, also a very productive engineer, how he works now. Here's what Dima Zaitsev (now an engineer at Linear) replied:

“Before, everything was simple: one mouse, one keyboard, one screen. It was physically impossible to do multiple things at once. Now, that limitation is gone.

As a result, I literally have 5-10 worktrees locally, and I switch between them. I give a task to one agent and, while it's working, I go to another to check the result or look at the code.”

Almost all the most productive engineers I know used to write code by hand a year ago, but now they don't and run multiple agents in parallel. That's a change!

#6. IDEs Are Disappearing

I have great respect for Steve Egge's ability to spot and understand trends at their very inception. On a podcast episode, he stated that the era of IDEs has effectively ended:

Gergely: “Speaking of developer work: you said something that struck a chord with many. At the AI Engineer Summit, you declared that if you're still using an IDE, you're a bad engineer.”

Steve: “Well, yes, a little provocation doesn't hurt. Let me put it this way. Okay, I won't call you a bad engineer, because I know very, very good engineers, better than me, who are still at the first or second level of my [AI usage] scale. But I genuinely pity them.

I pity them like I've never pitied anyone in my life. Grown adults, good engineers, or former good ones. And they say, 'Well, I use Cursor, sometimes I ask it questions. The answers are impressive. And then I review its code very carefully.'

And then they commit it, and I think, 'Dude, you're going to get fired. And you're one of the best engineers I know!!'”

Steve describes his AI usage scale as follows:

  1. AI is not used.

  2. AI agent in IDE with strict permissions.

  3. AI agent in IDE in "YOLO" mode (permissions disabled).

  4. AI agent in IDE, you no longer look at the code, you communicate with the agent.

  5. CLI is primary, IDE is abandoned.

  6. Multiple agents in parallel.

  7. Ten agents or more.

  8. Custom orchestrator running 30+ (or 100+) agents.

Steve believes that engineers who have "gotten hooked on AI" will abandon IDEs, and the market confirms this prediction:

  • Antigravity 1.0 was the last major IDE based on a VS Code fork: it was released in November 2025, and no new ones have appeared since. And in May 2026, Antigravity 2.0 was released, and the product moved away from the IDE concept.

  • Codex launched in February 2026 not as an IDE, and the team is happy with this, although at the end of 2025 they were still doubting whether to move away from IDEs.

  • Cursor moved away from IDEs. In April 2026, it re-launched without an IDE interface and now closely resembles the desktop applications of Codex and Claude. In the summer, the team told me that they support the "legacy" version on a VS Code fork for existing enterprise clients, but see the future in agents.

  • JetBrains also seems to be rushing to move away from IDEs. The company made some of the most developer-loved IDEs, but is now switching to JetBrains Air and agent-based development environments.

When I discussed with industry legend Kent Beck why IDEs are gradually disappearing, he said something interesting:

“It's not that humans no longer need overview and context for decisions. It's just that the context itself has changed.”

I think, in reality, the IDE is transforming into something else, better suited for working with agents. Steve Egge also said that since we're no longer writing code, the IDE should evolve from an editor into an interface for dialogue and observation.

I would add that it should also become an interface for verification: helping to understand if what the agent did works, which tests it passes, what it looks like, and allowing hands-on interaction. Such tools will surely emerge and spread quickly.

#8. Development Often Starts in Slack

After visiting startups, I saw that more and more development is initiated directly from Slack. At OpenAI, they write "@Codex, implement this," at Anthropic "@Claude, do this," at Linear "@Linear, handle this." More and more startups are connecting their code agent, often custom-built, to Slack.

Code agents work really well when they are deeply integrated into the company's stack: an agent in Slack launches an agent that writes code in the cloud.

#9. "Software Factory" Agents Are Being Built Everywhere

We analyzed in detail how OpenAI is building an agent-based software factory, and many readers responded with "we have that too!": their companies are building similar factories.

Such a factory integrates AI into a bunch of existing systems like CI/CD, so you can, for example, communicate with CI/CD via Slack. And entirely new systems with agents inside are emerging: agent-based code review, OpenAI's agent-based deployment system, Perf Factory.

#10. Migrations No Longer Take Years

Many migrations that previously would have taken years are now completed in weeks or months:

  • Anthropic: migrating Bun from Zig to Rust in 11 days (estimated at 1.5 engineer-years).

  • OpenAI: migrating the API layer from Python to Rust, and the migration takes about five months, whereas without AI it would have taken several years.

  • Airbnb: migrating 3500 end-to-end test files from Enzyme to React Testing Library took six weeks (instead of years).

  • Asana: migrating 4000 end-to-end test files from Enzyme to React Testing Library took two weeks, although the work was estimated at five years.

  • Uber: migrating 600,000 JUnit 4 tests in 15 million lines of code (!!) to JUnit 5 took four months (instead of the expected several years).

#11. The Cost of AI Has Become a Significant Headache

In May, we wrote about the trend of reducing AI costs in development departments, and last month about how companies are switching to open-source models to save 50% or more on tokens. A good example is Uber: they are spending more and more on tokens, but costs haven't increased since May because the company runs open-source models and intelligently distributes requests among them.

Three weeks after my article, Bloomberg confirmed the same trend. As I noted in the original analysis, most companies save primarily by using cheaper models and smart routing. If you do only two things, start with them.

#12. Projects Are Done by One or Two Engineers

Caitlin Less, who leads Claude Platform development at Anthropic, shared how her team works. Quote from our analysis:

“It's often impossible to work on a single project with more than two people. Each engineer is already running multiple agents, and you end up fighting with your own agents bumping into each other in the same code. If you add more people, especially each with their own agents, there's just no room for everyone!”

I'm seeing the same thing now in both small and large companies.

Other Changes

  • #13. Narrow specializations are disappearing. Demand for iOS, Android, and frontend seems to be falling, while demand for "universal" engineers is growing. We analyzed this trend with numbers in the article on the developer job market in 2026: demand for mobile and frontend development is declining, while demand for AI and FDE is sharply increasing.

  • #14. Teams are shrinking. Projects are being done by fewer engineers, and in startups, teams seem to be downsizing.

  • #15. Fewer junior engineers are being hired. In the same job market analysis, we wrote that it has become harder for graduates and interns to find jobs.

  • #16. Agent infrastructure is becoming a separate discipline. Most companies from medium-sized and up are building agent platforms, and infrastructure teams are increasingly becoming agent infrastructure teams.

3. What Broke?

Ideas about how much code is changed and how often

It used to be thought that code volume grew roughly linearly. But with agents, both the number of lines and the number of commits are growing exponentially, and GitHub data shows this:

Human Code Review Is Dead

An engineer from a mid-sized startup told me something that has long been an open secret in the industry, even in places where code review is formally in place:

“Everyone is performing a review theater. But with the volume of changes hitting you, people just go for the path of least resistance: they stop reviewing and just put LGTM on everything.

We ourselves are gradually winding down this process: we explicitly allow cutting corners in reviews where permissible, and productivity has significantly increased as a result.”

When I quoted the bolded phrase at LDX3, the audience reacted strongly. "Code review theater" is indeed present in most companies today. No engineer can review 5-10 times more code, so most don't look closely.

AI-Only Reviews Are Gaining Momentum

Quality and Reliability Are Declining

This is noticeable in many digital products we use. Here's what Mario Zechner, creator of Pi, said on the podcast:

“Everything is broken. There's definitely a feeling that software has become a fragile mess, where 98% uptime has become the norm, even for large services. And you encounter strange bugs in interfaces that the QA team should have caught. Yes, this was the case even before agents. But it seems the process is accelerating.”

We wrote about declining quality with the spread of AI back in March in the article, “Are AI Agents Actually Slowing Us Down?”

Infrastructure Capacity Is Lacking

Everyone knows about the GPU shortage and memory prices. And now, CPU shortages are being added to the list. As I wrote last month:

“It seems that without long-standing relationships with a cloud provider, it's now almost impossible to get CPUs on spot instances. Specific CPUs need to be reserved months in advance, and providers sometimes refuse altogether because they don't have enough CPUs of the required type.”

If your company consumes a significant amount of CPU, you should secure capacity now. Here's what I wrote recently:

“It's definitely worth securing additional CPU capacity right now. I'm hearing rumors that some cloud regions are no longer taking new clients because all CPUs are already allocated. In other places, negotiations are also difficult. I also hear that clients are already paying for capacity reservations that won't be available in data centers until December. From the providers' perspective, this looks predatory, but the demand is such that they are apparently distributing new capacity this way, while earning much more than usual.

If your company has fluctuating loads and you used to rely on spot instances, it might be time to switch to fixed capacity, even if it's more expensive. And if you anticipate significant growth, doing so now will preserve your room to maneuver with some providers or in certain regions.”

Concentration and Personal Productivity Are Suffering

Dima Zaitsev (now at Linear, formerly my colleague at Uber) told me something about AI and productivity that I recognized in myself:

“You're constantly switching context: there's always another agent waiting for your response.

And there's simply more work. When AI was just gaining momentum, I felt more productive than my colleagues: I could do in an hour what took others a day. Now, everyone is expected to handle multiple tasks in parallel. As a result, it feels like I have more work than before AI.”

Engineering Leaders Are Taking Creative Leave

An interesting and unexpected trend: CTOs, VPs, and Directors of Engineering are resigning, often without moving to specific new roles. Here's what a current CTO, who asked to remain anonymous, said:

“These changes [with AI] awaken in those who love to build, the desire to build. You want to create things again, do more and faster, with better tools, increase the 'talent density' in the team, where fewer strong people do more, and so on.

This is an amazing time to create something, and it will only get better. People just want to be closer to it. They want to build themselves and see how far they can go with new tools.

Among my acquaintances, there are quite a few CTOs who have stepped down. And even more who, in large companies, are moving from management back to engineering, without direct reports. And their stories are mostly as I described: they simply see that this is a truly exciting time to create.

After all, that's why they got into the profession in the first place.”

4. What Has Remained the Same?

In my LDX3 talk, I also discussed what has hardly changed with the advent of AI.

Teams are still important. Here's what Caitlin Less, head of Claude Platform development, said in July analysis:

“Sometimes people tell me, 'We have two people and a bunch of agents.' And I reply that our setup is different. I still have teams that own their part of the software, develop it, hold on-call rotations, and so on. AI greatly empowers each of these individuals, but the size and shape of the team have remained roughly the same. We still have the same two-pizza teams.”

Caitlin works at one of the most AI-centric companies in the world. If even there, teams are as important as they were before AI, it's safe to say that team structure is relevant to the entire industry.

Planning hasn't gone away, at least for complex work. Complex projects still have a long planning phase. Again, Caitlin, this time on how Claude Managed Agents were planned:

“We planned more or less the old way, as before AI. You know, every team has this one project that everyone eventually gets to for some version, and they discuss it in circles until they finally start working on it? For our team, that was Managed Agents. When we started, we had documents with ideas and proposals from two years prior.”

Tests and verification are still very important. Here are the words of Jared Sumner, creator of Bun, who is now at Anthropic:

“AI writes practically all the code, but we also delegate practically all the tests to AI. Before AI, we spent about as much time on tests for production software as on the code itself. With AI, it's the same. You need a way to trust your code, and tests are perhaps the best one.”

In all the companies I spoke with before the talk, a lot of effort goes into verifying what the agents produce. And this is logical: code review no longer works as it used to, there's more code, and you need to be sure it won't crash production.

Non-engineers still don't deploy code to production. For the past few weeks, social media has been buzzing with claims that with AI, product managers, designers, and non-technical staff are deploying code to production themselves. I decided to check and asked AI labs, startups, and other companies about this.

The result is this: I haven't found a single company where product managers, designers, or non-technical staff deploy code to production.

However, I have heard of cases where such employees, with the help of agents, make bug fixes (often without realizing it themselves!) or new features that are then sent for review to developers. And they prototype a lot. But engineers still decide what can and cannot be released to production.

We are rediscovering old patterns that work well with AI. This came up in our podcast with Matt Pocock. Matt noted that agents tend to build software layer by layer, which leads to bugs at the layer interfaces. Reading the book "The Pragmatic Programmer," he stumbled upon the concept of a "tracer bullet." When he started asking the agent to build the application using "tracer bullets," i.e., implementing the "golden path" first, the agent's code improved.

Now, according to Matt, he reads classic software development books looking for other "keywords" that effectively guide agents.

In general, I've noticed that "old" best practices help make software better even with AI: unit tests, the "golden path" first (also known as the "tracer bullet," well-thought-out application architecture, and even design patterns. It's funny that these practices are decades old.

5. What's Next?

Where the industry is heading can be understood from trends that are already underway and will almost certainly continue.

Cloud agents and harnesses will dominate. Most developers in companies will move AI agent execution from local machines to the cloud. Ramp is setting an example; we wrote about this in the analysis of their cloud agent Inspect. It seems the principle of "build your own cloud harness for agents" is gaining traction in leading tech companies.

I also expect Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceX, and others to actively promote their cloud code agents, and for startups with their own cloud harnesses to become more numerous.

We, engineers, will stop reading code. When exactly this will happen – this year, next year, or later – it's too early to say, but most likely, few of us will continue to read the code written by AI agents properly.

I always listen carefully to Charity Majors, co-founder and CTO of Honeycomb. She approaches new technologies with default skepticism, yet she is an outstanding engineer and speaks her mind. On our podcast in August, she posed a question:

“What does it take for you to confidently deploy code without reading or understanding it? Because that is engineering.”

Charity noted that operations and QA have had decades to learn how to deploy code they didn't write or understand to production while ensuring it works. It seems most developers will inevitably have to join them. The irony is that development has long been based precisely on engineers writing code and understanding it!

Companies will build entirely new types of internal infrastructure. We will see familiar systems being "reinvented" for agents:

  • CI/CD with agent evals as part of the pipeline;

  • agent software factories and an understanding of what's practical and what's not within them;

  • agents as part of deployment, observability, and incident management;

  • agent observability will likely become a separate field and specialization: how to ensure that agents interacting with customers behave as expected, and that the systems agents build and modify actually work as intended?

The "golden age" of refactoring, migrations, and complete rewrites is coming. Migrations and rewrites that have been postponed for years because they themselves would take years, should now be completed in weeks. So, there's almost no excuse for further delays.

Engineers today better understand what agents are capable of in rewrites, refactoring, and migrations. We will tackle technical debt much faster, and there will be no more reasons to tolerate a system in a state that doesn't satisfy us.

Startups, when hiring, look at how confidently a person works with AI and their attitude towards it. I've written little about this trend, but it's already here: startups are selecting engineers who are positively inclined towards AI. Here's what a VP of Engineering at a Series D startup told me:

“We started assessing a positive attitude towards AI during interviews. We need people who want to push the boundaries of what can be built with AI alongside us.”

Code agents are already everywhere, and agent systems, it seems, will spread even wider very soon. This will create high demand for engineers who want to and can build next-generation systems, so enthusiasm for this topic will become a basic requirement.

It's similar to how startups look for engineers who share the idea of rapid growth, and companies prefer those who are willing to work with the established stack and don't bring their personal preferences.

Demand for engineers with deep domain knowledge will increase. In New York, I spoke with Titus Winters, lead author of the book "Software Engineering at Google." He shared an observation:

“To succeed anywhere, you need intelligence, wisdom, and charisma. Intelligence is how to do it. Wisdom is what to do. Charisma is convincing others to do it.

When intelligence becomes commonplace, wisdom and charisma become much more important.”

In development, "wisdom" is most easily gained by becoming an expert in the subject matter. Working in fintech? Study the financial industry and its clients; this will make you more valuable and in demand. The same applies to agritech and any other field.

And finally: remember why you got into IT. At the end of the podcast with Peter Mattis, co-founder and CTO of Cockroach Labs, we discussed how exhausting it is to try and keep up with the current pace of change. Here's what he said:

“Remind yourself why you got into this in the first place. I came into development because I love building things. Now I can build faster and without some of the compromises I had to make before!”
