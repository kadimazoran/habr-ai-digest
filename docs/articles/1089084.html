But that's not for me.

Fifty switches a week

First, I clarified: is something wrong with me, or with everyone?

In 2004, at Microsoft Research, eleven people recorded every switch between tasks in a table for a week. Among them were a broker, a computer science professor, a developer, a network administrator, and even a boat salesman. They noted what the task was, what documents were needed, how difficult it was to return, and what they forgot.

Here's what they found:

  1. An average of 50 switches per week.

  2. It's hardest to return to long projects. They take twice as long as ordinary tasks (120 minutes vs. 45), involve more documents, and are interrupted twice as often.

  3. 13% of entries were for "task tracking." People kept lists, copied files, and recorded disks before leaving home, just to avoid losing the thread.

Among the labels participants used to categorize tasks was "annual performance review." Reviews were a separate task even twenty years ago. It was also necessary to return to them.

Gloria Mark from the University of California, Irvine, observed 36 specialists for three days. On average, people had 12.2 parallel "work spheres" per day, and they switched between them every 10.5 minutes on average. More than 80% of interrupted work was resumed the same day. But before returning, they managed to do about two other tasks.

Sound familiar? A colleague with a mug of tea in the kitchen asks about the status. Another asks for clarification on a product. Essentially, it's the same work: consultation, and it's done well. Only it doesn't appear in the chronology later.

Each such conversation in the kitchen is also an interruption, and Mark shows that it changes the environment. You return to your desk, and there are new windows open and different papers lying around. It becomes harder to remember where you left off.

By the way, about "23 minutes to return to a task." This figure circulates in productivity articles: as if after any interruption, you need 23 minutes to focus again. I went to find out where it came from.

It turned out to be from the same interview with Mark. Mark measured how much time passed before a person returned to a dropped task at all. On average, it was 23 minutes and 15 seconds, and during that time, they managed to do two other tasks. She didn't measure focus. In popular articles, the time to return turned into the time needed to re-engage.

Other studies do not confirm this figure. Iqbal and Horvitz in 2007 returned to the original task in 11-16 minutes. And in Mark's own 2008 paper, people with interruptions even completed tasks faster, they just got more nervous. So, you can return to work faster, especially when you know where you stopped. For me, that's the main thing: remembering where you were interrupted, or having a tool that shows you where you are now.

In a survey by VK WorkSpace and Hi-Tech of 4,000 Russians, 42% admitted that they periodically spend more time searching for the right file or message than working with them. According to Microsoft telemetry, the average employee receives 117 emails and 153 instant messages per day.

It turns out that memory is not designed to store six months of work. If you have fifty switches a week, you have over a thousand in six months. You can't keep them all in your head.

A table in someone else's report

Next, I came across Tanya Reilly's report "Being the Glue." She talks about an engineer who spent two years helping newcomers get settled, assisting colleagues when they got stuck, keeping meeting minutes, and talking to users. Reilly calls this work "glue": it holds the team together, but it's not visible in the results. The engineer's reviews were excellent. But at her promotion, she was told she had no technical contribution.

I read this and recognized myself, only on a smaller scale: there's work for the team, close colleagues appreciate it, but it's hard to see in the results. My "glue" looks simpler. A colleague writes to me privately: "I need to calculate who reached their personal account after the webinar." I write a query, collect a table, send it. An hour of work. Over six months, dozens of such requests accumulated.

The table then lives in someone else's report. In mine, all that's left is a "thanks, it helped" in a private message.

It turns out that help leaves almost no trace where results are later sought. According to data from "LiderTask" and Strive, this is evident in the numbers:

  1. Only 10% of assignments contain a description, an assignee, and a deadline.

  2. 55% of employees assign or receive tasks in chats, 32% verbally.

  3. Managers spend 2.8 hours a day on discussions, while rank-and-file employees spend about one and a half.

A request to "take a quick look" comes in a private message, and the response goes there too. Nothing remains in the tracker from it. Nothing in the calendar either, if the call was for five minutes.

Reports are compiled based on tasks and releases, while help lives in correspondence. Even Gallup, which has been studying reviews for many years, explicitly states that helping colleagues needs to be included in the evaluation separately. It won't get there on its own.

Where memory is stored

I returned to the third line of the report and tried to understand what exactly I had forgotten.

The first thought was unpleasant: "Maybe six months really passed in vain?" No. In "Sent" items over this period, thousands of emails accumulated, and even more messages in the messenger. Almost all about work.

Then, maybe bad memory? Also not the case. As soon as I opened a specific conversation, I remembered everything: who asked, what broke, how it ended.

So. The memories are there, I just lack the entry point to them.

Psychologists have long noticed this mechanism. When a person knows that information is stored, they remember the information itself less well and remember better where it is located. The brain economizes: why store the content if you have the address. (Not all scientists could replicate this experiment. But when 35 similar studies were combined in 2024, the effect was generally confirmed.)

It turned out to be funny. For six months, I diligently stored my memory in emails and chats, and for the third line, I tried to retrieve it from my head.

But the longer I thought about it, the less funny it became. The same thing happened to me not only with reports.

A year ago, I set up an open LLM on a GPU server to compare models. Recently, I was asked how inference was configured there. From memory, I could only recall the model itself.

Then I opened the chat with the setup and my notes. In half an hour, I reconstructed the rest: versions, launch parameters, why the first option was rejected. The answer lay in the traces.

From this, my answer to my eternal question, what is more important for a specialist: knowledge or skills. I believe skills are more important. Let me explain with my example. By knowledge, I mean the information itself: all the commands to set up Llama and configure OpenClaw. By skills, I mean the ability to use help via –help, read documentation, and save accumulated experience in instructions to return to it later. In my opinion, the debate itself is one-sided. It's usually led by someone evaluating from the outside: they only see the result and don't look at how the person got there. For a specialist who constantly switches tasks, knowledge inevitably fades. What remains is the ability to reconstruct context from traces, search logic, and the habit of leaving traces in advance, all of which are skills that are trained just like any work tool.

Why do I favor skills? I have a long-standing example. Once, I was an active participant on the Ubuntu forum under the nickname graddata and helped newcomers with their tasks. The community grew, and my forum colleagues and I constantly updated the documentation.

What exactly I did then, I no longer remember. Whether I patched the kernel or wrote a driver for a printer. But I still have the forum, and it works as a knowledge base. I can easily find the right thread with a search or formulate a query, and an AI assistant can help assemble a script or a bash command from it.

The skill and accumulated experience remained with me. The knowledge, alas, has been forgotten.

However, external memory has a catch. In psychologists' experiments, people didn't notice that the data in external storage had been replaced and incorporated the false information into their own memory. It's not enough to store traces; they need to be verified. Especially when someone else, like an AI, retells them. Hence my practical rule: a summary must lead to the original source. This is where RAG comes to mind, but that's a topic for another article. Another question keeps me occupied: where to store the truth that no one will tamper with? For now, the benchmark for me remains a printed book on a shelf, where not a single line has changed for years.

Excavations by traces

Julia Evans described the same scene back in 2019. Before her review, she went through her code changes, tracker tasks, launch emails, and project documents. And she found forgotten things: mentoring an intern five months ago, a security project, help with migration. She advises not to rely on memory and to keep a document of achievements.

My process is similar, only I start with excavation:

  1. Calendar for the half-year. I list one-off meetings with people outside my team: these are most often instances of help.

  2. Sent emails. Sent items contain what I did, incoming items contain what they wanted from me. Besides, there are fewer of them.

  3. Private messages in the messenger. I search for keywords like "thanks," "helped," "worked," "calculated."

  4. Task tracker: closed tasks, reviews, comments.

  5. Documents I created or edited: wiki pages, dashboards. A dashboard has a creation date, which is a trace with an exact number.

  6. For each finding, one line: what was done, for whom, what changed. Evans advises not to stop at "released a new feature" and to add what it led to.

Five sources converge into one chronology, from which both the report and a log for the next time are generated.

I tried keeping a log in a notebook and quit after a month. Something else worked. I now immediately document analytical tasks in the tracker with the request, source, and result, and a small script with AI automatically creates some of them on schedule. By the next report, this part of the chronology will be assembled automatically.

AI as an archaeologist

The manual excavation took me an evening and another morning: I had to ask colleagues for some of the data. With AI, the same search boils down to a few queries: I ask what happened on the project in March, and I get a summary.

The scheme is universal; everyone has their own set of sources. At the center is my corporate AI, VK AI Space. Through MCP, it's connected to (I'll warn you right away: this is a red zone for information security, almost like privileged access, so I have a separate process for this, and I approve each assistant's request myself):

  1. VK WorkSpace with all chats, projects, presentations, and images: at work, I live in it on my laptop, phone, and even in smartwatch notifications; it's my primary tool for communication and planning. VK WorkSpace has its own MCP server: in the boxed version, it already works with the messenger, and in the cloud version, it will appear very soon; colleagues promise to connect mail, disk, and calendar later. For the rest, including the disk with presentations and images, I'm currently using my own connectors.

  2. Internal task tracker, internal wiki, and internal BI system.

  3. Colleagues' reports on the shared drive.

  4. Code repository and other work storage.

  5. Data from our own cloud projects. We are IT professionals, after all, so I pull information on Kubernetes clusters and virtual infrastructure through OpenStack CLI, kubectl, and Grafana dashboards: for example, what happened to the project's resources last week and how much budget has already been spent according to billing data.

My main condition: the summary comes with links to the original messages, so any line can be verified. Otherwise, the retelling will subtly replace the fact, as in those experiments with external memory. I checked the mechanism and formatting once, and then I selectively look at the links.

Two agents you can build yourself

I use the corporate AI assistant I wrote about above. Research for the semi-annual report was reduced to a few queries: who I met with outside the team, who I calculated what for, what was discussed on the projects. It's a pity that kitchen conversations can't be digitized like this. AI glasses and an AI dictaphone are in my plans, but of course, I'll only record with the consent of the interlocutors.

I'll show two skills for an AI agent that I use myself. I'm intentionally omitting system names: the logic is the same in any stack, and each team has its own tracker, chat, and analytics system. By skill, I mean a set of instructions that the agent loads and executes identically for anyone who runs it.

The first agent remembers people and agreements. The second gathers a picture of the product: what has been delivered, what is urgent, and what is about to be released.

Work Memory Agent

Imagine a project manager who forgets nothing: meeting minutes, document versions, promises, deadlines, and the history of collaboration. The work memory agent does this job for me.

Its main idea is putting things in order. Every interaction with a colleague, whether it's a message, a task, a document edit, or a meeting, the agent assigns to a project and a person and accumulates it, rather than retelling it once. Over time, each project gets its own summary, and each colleague gets their own history of shared affairs.

What's on the shelves:

  1. People and interactions. Each colleague has their own card: role, department, shared projects, and history of who came to whom with what. A new colleague is studied by the agent based on their role and work intersections, without questionnaires or messages.

  2. Projects. A separate work chat creates a project, and its identifier doesn't change even if the chat is renamed. Private messages and general channels can relate to several projects at once.

  3. Meetings and minutes. Planned and actual dates, participants, agenda, minutes, and decisions. Rescheduling a meeting is saved in the history and doesn't overwrite the old date.

  4. Documents and versions. Each version is stored separately with extracted text and linked to the project and meeting. The author of a specific edit is verified separately: the date of the last page change doesn't prove it.

  5. Agreements. Request, acceptance, deadline, handover, confirmation. A request without acceptance remains a request, not someone's obligation.

Here's a fragment of a report for the fictional project "Webinar on Object Storage":

Task and assignee

Dates

Status and confirmation

Submit report plan, report author

accepted 10/08, deadline 10/10

Submitted 10/09: plan in message, receipt confirmed by reply

Approve title, coordinator

accepted 10/09, deadline not specified

Completed 10/10: explicit approval exists

Prepare final slides, report author

accepted 10/10, deadline 10/15

Accepted, completion not yet confirmed

Publish recording, assignee not assigned

request 10/11

Request without acceptance, responsible person needed

At the end of the report, the agent writes coverage: how many messages were received, how many were filtered as service messages, what remains unverified. Completeness refers to what was read, not the entire work on the project.

The rules it operates on:

  1. Each result is based on a verifiable object: a message, a task, a document version, a code merge. If there's no confirmation, the record remains undefined, not uncompleted.

  2. Submitting text, approving it, and publishing it: for the agent, these are three different actions, and it doesn't merge them.

  3. A short "ok" or "done" closes a task only when combined with the original request. Greetings and casual remarks are not analyzed.

  4. Participants are identified by a stable identifier, not by name. Matching surnames is insufficient; connections between systems are recorded with justification.

  5. Reading is done in chunks. The checkpoint shifts only after a chunk is saved, so after a failure, the agent continues from the same point, not rereading everything.

  6. Before mass reading, the agent shows the found volume and estimated time. I initiate it, not the agent.

What this gives the team:

  1. Team lead: who promised what, where things are stuck, and what deadlines have passed.

  2. Product manager: history of agreements on a product task from the first message to release.

  3. Developer: code merges and closed tasks as ready report lines.

  4. Everyone: searching for a colleague based on confirmed experience, not by mentioning a topic in a presentation title.

How to build one yourself (for engineers):

  1. Create a skill folder and a main file with modes: initial database training, update for a period, review of commitments, colleague search.

  2. Describe the schemas for cards: project, task, commitment, event, material. Store data separately from the skill, in a folder marked as a database, so the skill can be shared with colleagues without their private correspondence.

  3. Connect connectors for read-only access and check the actual fields: identifiers, paginated output, threads, version history. The list of available tools does not replace a successful call.

  4. Run a pilot on one project chat and one period. Then repeat on the same database with a new batch and check that identifiers are preserved and statuses are updated.

  5. Before a wide launch, test with fictional data: repeating a batch does not create duplicates, a renamed chat remains the same project, an ambiguous "ok" does not close a task.

Product Dashboard

The second agent answers the weekly question: what has been delivered to the client, what is about to be released, what is urgent, and where the product is heading. Manually, this involves sorting through thousands of tasks in the tracker. The dashboard compiles such a report in minutes. Its sources are internal only: the tracker and the wiki.

Everything the dashboard knows about products lives in the wiki, not in the agent. The agent only knows the sequence of actions and the address of the main dashboard page. The team edits products, requests, and report forms directly in the wiki. The next run then uses the new version, without reinstalling the skill.

What's in the wiki:

  1. Main page with assembly instructions. The agent reads it first; everything else is found via links.

  2. Tracker project map: which projects have what work, and which projects are just similar in name to the product.

  3. A page for each product: its boundaries, a grid of "what we're looking for × on which platform" with a ready-made query in each cell, sub-products, and topics within the product.

  4. Component directory: what this system is, with an explanation for the uninitiated.

  5. Report templates and a folder with ready-made product reports.

A product is not equal to a project in the tracker. One project serves several products; one product lives in several projects. Therefore, a product is defined by a slice: project plus component, a field in the directory, or a word in the name. Each slice has its own accuracy mark: "exact" if the selection is by field, and "approximate" if by text.

Next, the agent goes through six layers of the tracker, starting with releases: releases, development, client implementation, documentation for delivery to the closed environment, promotion, and customer inquiries. The report includes all layers with the number found, including zeros: zero for a layer is also a result.

The agent sorts what it finds into status buckets, with a separate query for each question:

  1. Delivered: tasks with a resolution date within the period, separately new features, releases, and fixes.

  2. Coming soon: reviews, testing, ready for rollout.

  3. Urgent: critical and blocking tasks, vulnerabilities, incidents, only open ones.

  4. In progress: large epics, excluding small tasks.

  5. Rejected: what didn't make it and why.

  6. Service: autotests, infrastructure, technical debt. This stream collapses into a single number.

Each of these rules appeared after research. For one of the products, there was movement in more than 3000 tasks during the period. Selection by the last modification date drowned in bots and autotests, and only a few new features reached the report. A query by resolution date found more than 15. For another product, selection within the development project showed zero tasks for the quarter, although in reality, the product was relaunched: three out of four facts were in projects that no one was looking at.

Strict dashboard rules:

  1. One report per platform: different product delivery options are not mixed in one report.

  2. Status is written verbatim. "Ready for rollout" and "rolling out," "rejected" and "done" mean different things, and retelling erases this difference.

  3. Each number and each thesis with a link to the task. An empty slice means "no changes" and also goes into the report.

  4. Client and employee names do not appear in the report or export; the client is masked by industry (Information Security).

  5. Text generated by the model is marked and checked before being shown externally.

Who will find it useful:

  1. Team lead: what's urgent, what's stuck in review and testing, what's rejected and why.

  2. Product manager: what was actually delivered to the client on a specific platform during the period, with ready-made text for release notes.

  3. Developer: their releases in the context of the entire product, and the service stream doesn't clutter the picture.

How to build one yourself (for engineers):

  1. Connect connectors to the tracker and wiki for read-only access. Start with one product and one report format.

  2. Create a main page in the wiki with assembly instructions and five sections: products, components, directories, templates, ready reports.

  3. Create a project map: what layers you have and which projects only resemble the product by name.

  4. Create a page for the first product and run its queries on real data, recording the result on a separate verification page.

  5. Write the main skill file: the address of the main page, the nine-step procedure, and strict rules. Queries and formats should not be in the skill; they live in the wiki.

  6. Compile the first report and compare it with what the product owner knows. Record each research finding as a rule in the wiki, then replicate the page for other products.

How they connect

The work memory agent stores who agreed with whom about what and what confirms it. The product dashboard shows what of these agreements actually reached the client, with a link to each task. Together, they fill the third line of the report: my help to colleagues is visible in the agreements, and its result is in the delivered releases.

Access under supervision

This section is for information security and engineers. Both agents read chats, tasks, and documents, so the question "is this safe?" is paramount here.

I grant access to sources on a granular basis: only for a specific session and only after additional two-factor authentication. Yes, information security is vigilant, and rightly so. Every assistant's request to chats, tasks, and documents is logged and monitored, so the AI sees exactly what I've allowed for that time. Essentially, this is the logic of PAM systems for privileged access: rights are granted for a limited time, with confirmation, and with recording of all actions.

The model runs where the data resides, within the company's perimeter, and sees only what is accessible to me by rights.

Connecting MCP is not as simple as it looks in the announcements. Each source requires its own connector, approval from the system owner and information security, permission setup, and verification that the assistant sees only what is allowed. There is no "connect all" button here; each source is added separately.

Recent reports show the consequences of the reverse path when work correspondence is copied into public chatbots:

  1. According to "Solar" data for the first half of 2026, almost 40% of requests to public AIs contain confidential data.

  2. According to "Informzashchita" data, 20% of organizations with data leaks attributed them to AIs outside of information security control. A year ago, this figure was around 12%.

Copying six months of correspondence into a public chatbot for a nice report means causing exactly such an incident with your own hands.

A conversation about work

I know I'll be challenged: reviews have been criticized for a long time, so is it worth adapting to them? But the report itself for the period remains a mandatory task, one for all employees, so you can't escape it.

They are probably criticized fairly, especially when an employee is not given the tools and time for reports, and the habit of keeping them has not been instilled. According to Gallup, only 14% of employees fully agree that reviews inspire them to grow. Kluger and DeNisi compiled 607 effects of feedback: on average, it improves results, but more than a third of the effects were negative.

The more feedback shifts attention from the task to the question "what kind of person am I," the weaker the effect. Specific comments on the task improved performance. Ratings only increased anxiety about oneself.

It turns out the problem is not with the report itself. It begins when they start evaluating the person instead of talking about the tasks. A report based on traces is precisely about tasks: what was done, for whom, what changed.

And it's needed not only for reviews. Evans notes that a manager remembers even less about you and cannot defend you without such a document. The same document will be useful when changing managers, when handing over a project, and simply to see where six months have gone.

In short, for a note:

  1. Six months of work don't fit into memory: fifty switches occur per week, and consultations in the kitchen and private messages leave no trace. The period is reconstructed from traces, not memory.

  2. What I observe: work for the team is valued by close colleagues, but it doesn't make it into the tracker. I start research for the report from the calendar and sent emails.

  3. I immediately put meeting minutes and agreements into the internal wiki, and the work memory agent sorts every interaction with colleagues by person and project. This part of the chronology is already assembled for the report.

  4. What actually reached the client is shown by the product dashboard: product knowledge lives in the wiki, selection is by status buckets, each thesis with a link to the task.

  5. I check the AI assistant once for the collection mechanism and formatting; after that, it works on its own. I grant access to sources on a granular basis, for a session and with a second factor; each request is approved (Information Security).

  6. Knowledge fades, skills remain. Therefore, I've decided the question "knowledge or skills" in favor of skills, and it now sounds different: where are my traces, and can I find them?
