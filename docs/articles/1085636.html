So they can be used for work if good enough quality is sufficient, rather than maximum. On the other hand, Gemini 3.8 Flash is very cheap, so I see no point in saving money. Details are in the ranking.

A month ago, I compared Opus 5, Sol 5.6, and Gemini (Flash and Pro) in literary translation and editing. The method was as follows: one model translated a chapter, and a known good LLM (Sol or Opus) edited it. The measure of quality was the number of edits by the editor model. The fewer edits, the better the translation. The method turned out to be quite effective. But in the end, I had some complaints about it. It wasn't precise enough, and users in the comments said a proper benchmark was needed. I built one, and it's now integrated into the BookTrans pipeline. The benchmark is now easily reproducible.

Why the Edit Count Stopped Being Satisfactory

The number of editor edits seems like an objective measure; it's just a number, easy to work with. But this method has a lot of drawbacks.

Subjectivity of the Judge. Sol edits twice as many paragraphs as Opus, but this is a matter of taste, not meaning. Translations could only be compared if they had the same judge. This is inconvenient.

The Count Favors Caution. According to the pipeline's conventions, the editor does not see the original. Therefore, a smooth translation that omits a metaphor and simplifies a sentence receives fewer edits than a bold and accurate one.

Numbers Mean Nothing Without Manual Reading. Every time, I had to read four translations alongside the original, and the reading was crucial.

The benchmark was intended to eliminate these and other flaws while preserving what the edit count does well: measuring translation quality in a real pipeline, with its prompts and required response formats.

How the Benchmark Works

The idea is borrowed from the industrial standard for translation evaluation, MQM, where quality is described by error markup by category, and also from the practice of school exams: the checker has an answer key. The LLM judge receives a list of checkpoints where a trap is set in the text and the answers, and for each, it responds "passed" or "failed." It responds in a special formalized format, and then a program calculates the final score.

Text. The story "The Skylight Room" by O. Henry, published in 1906, was used as the basis; its copyright has long expired. The story has been significantly reworked: the action has been moved to the present day, outdated phrases have been replaced, names and titles are different, and the ending has been rewritten. The plot is preserved; it's a cohesive story of 66 paragraphs and a quatrain in the epigraph, about 3100 words, just one standard chunk for the pipeline. One hundred traps have been embedded in the text across twelve areas, each worth one point, so the result reads as a percentage.

A few traps in detail to clarify what is meant

The emergency doctor is mentioned for six consecutive paragraphs without gender indication, and then turns out to be a woman. In English, this is trivial – in Russian, the translator must choose the gender of the verbs in advance, and a model that doesn't read to the end writes "the doctor said." This is three points out of a hundred, and more than half of the runs, including flagships, fail the first one.

The word "fair" appears three times: "a fair shot" in a shooting range (a good shot, counted), "the street fair" (a street market), and "a fair complexion" (light skin). Three out of four models translate "a fair shot" as "an honest shot."

Miss Worcester says "Well, really!" five times, and this is her characteristic. A translator who translates it differently five times erases this characteristic.

The surname Beauchamp is pronounced "Beecham" in English, but models in two out of five cases write "Beauchamp," as in French. Cholmondeley is "Chumley," and about a third of translations give "Cholmondeley" phonetically. However, the Irish Siobhan is known as "Shivan" by almost everyone.

Key. The text is accompanied by correct answers for the judge: one hundred lines of the form "checkpoint, area, paragraph address, what is considered correct." The key is written in English and does not depend on the translation language, so the same benchmark can be used to test translation from English to any target language; the judge translates examples for Russian into another language by meaning. The translator does not see the key.

The text and key are provided compressed. The repository is public, and the open answer key would sooner or later end up in the training data of models that specialize in translation. Neither documentation nor tests quote the text used in the benchmark for translation.

Penalties. In addition to the key, the judge notes fabrications (facts, actions, or evaluations not present in the original) and untranslated fragments. The code, without judge intervention, calculates lost paragraphs, mismatched markup, characters from foreign alphabets (hieroglyphs in a Russian text), and extra attempts. Regarding attempts separately: when a model responds incorrectly, the pipeline prompts again with a hint, up to five times. Without penalty, a model that gets the format right on the third try would receive the same score as one that got it on the first, even though it costs three times as much in time and money.

Score. The score for an area is the number of passed checkpoints; the total score is the sum of areas minus penalties, normalized to one hundred. The judge does not provide any "score from 0 to 100," and this is crucial: a free-form evaluation can fluctuate by tens of points between runs, while a "yes/no" answer to a specific requirement is reproducible. The same translation, submitted to the judge a second time, yielded scores of 82 and 83, with a discrepancy of one point out of a hundred.

Three Runs. A single translation from a model is not enough for ranking. For average models, two translations of the same text can differ by 10–20 points; for strong models, by 3–7. Therefore, by default, the text is translated and judged three times; the runs are simultaneous, and the report includes the median and the spread.

Judge. By default, Claude Opus 5.5 is used at medium effort, with Sol 5.6 as a backup. If a judge from the same family as the translator is used, the benchmark will produce an error: models are blind to their own calques, and therefore Anthropic models are judged by Sol. I also tested Sonnet 5 as a judge: on the same translations, it differs from Opus by 4–7 points out of a hundred, systematically forgiving softened profanity, and is not cheaper. Therefore, Opus was chosen.

How penalties inverted the ranking, and why they had to be calibrated

The first version of the key penalized minor fabrications with three points, while a trap was worth one. On codex models, this yielded a counterintuitive result: the cheap Luna outperformed Terra and Sol. Analyzing the verdicts showed that the order of traps was reversed: Sol passed 90 points out of 100, Terra 89, Luna 85. Luna failed the doctor's gender in all three instances, translated "too lovely for anything" literally, and confused "a fair shot" with "an honest shot." But Luna had no penalties, while Sol had three "fabrications" for nine points: "rich, ruddy" for one "flush," "out of ten targets" instead of "out of ten."

Therefore, in the next version of the key, penalties for minor fabrications were reduced (1 point, major 3). After this, the codex models fell into the expected order with gaps of 4–8 points.

How to Run

The benchmark is included in BookTrans starting from version 1.10.77. A book is not needed; the text is in the package. Only the target language and the translator are required:

uv tool install booktrans        # or: pipx install booktrans 
booktrans --bench --to ru --translator codex:gpt-6-astra:medium

The model is named the same as in any other pipeline key: agent, model, effort separated by colons. Western models are accessed via their native CLIs (claude, codex, agy), Chinese models via any OpenAI protocol endpoint. I used the byNara router, which has all the Chinese models I'm interested in and a free plan, and the OpenCode Go subscription with Kimi, MiniMax, and Qwen; the endpoint address and API key are placed in environment variables or in configuration files:

export OPENAI_BASE_URL=https://router.bynara.id/v1
export OPENAI_API_KEY=sk-…
booktrans --bench --to ru --translator openai:deepseek-v4.1-flash:medium

After a few minutes, a working folder benchmark-en-ru-<provider-model-effort>-<date>.work appears in the current directory with three translations inside (they are worth reading) and a report next to it. The first line of the report is the median, followed by a table of runs, areas, failed checkpoints with reasons and quotes from the translation, penalties, cost, time, and a ready-to-use row for the results table. The judge can be changed with the --judge key, the number of runs with the --bench-runs key, and a custom text+key set can be provided via --bench FOLDER. Details are in docs/bench/README.md.

There were three judges. Most models were judged by Opus 5.5, Anthropic models by Sol 5.6: a judge from the same family is excluded. GLM 5.3, Kimi K3, and the third run of Haiku were judged by Opus 4.6. This was a forced measure: a judge uses about 30,000 tokens per run, and the subscription limits for Opus 5.5 and Sol were exhausted before the model queue. On translations by Astra and DeepSeek, re-judged for verification, Opus 4.6 gave medians of 91 and 80 compared to 90 and 79 for Opus 5.5, but it is softer on individual points, so the GLM and Kimi rows should be considered preliminary. The same judge on the same translation yields a one-point difference, but I cannot rule out a two-to-three-point shift between judges from different families. Therefore, adjacent places with a difference less than the spread should be considered as one place.

The list is instructive. Half of the lines are LLM habits: softening profanity, using three dots, calquing "are you okay?" These can be addressed with prompts. The other half are tests of comprehension: the reversed deal, "honest shot," green color instead of inexperience, and the male gender of the doctor distinguish models that read the text to the end from those that translate paragraph by paragraph.

Wordplay is a challenge for everyone. "Higher and lower" in the original means both a floor above and a lower price, and a Russian equivalent with both meanings was found in only 12 out of 44 runs. The pun with gamma and alpha relies on the fact that in American schools, grades are given by letters; in Russian, "I would have given it an alpha" is not understood, and it would have been more honest to say "a five," as done in 14 out of 44 runs.

Conclusions

The best translation today is provided by Gemini 3.8 Flash and GPT-6 Astra. There is a one-point difference between them, which is less than the spread. Astra is more consistent: its three runs fell within 88–92, while Flash had one run drop to 82. Flash, however, is twice as fast and is a light-class model, not a flagship. Google deserves praise: its inexpensive model translates into Russian better than any other.

Claude 5 was somewhat disappointing. Opus 5, Opus 5.5, and Fable 5.1 all scored the same, 78: Opus 5.5 showed no improvement over Opus 5. Opus 5.5 and Fable had the same failures: meaning accuracy 9 out of 15 and character gender 5 out of 8. However, they are among the best in calques and imagery, scoring 9–10 out of 11. Claude writes smoothly but paraphrases, and the benchmark catches this. Fable was the most disappointing: it's Anthropic's super-flagship, costs four times as much as Opus 5.5, and offers nothing extra for the money. If comparing super-flagships, GPT-6 Astra translates noticeably better: 90 versus 78. If you're going to use Claude, I'd recommend Opus 5.5: the score is the same as Opus 5, but it's cheaper and faster.

Now, about the cost. DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and V4 Pro performed on par with Opus and Terra. This means you can get a translation quality comparable to Claude Opus with a Chinese model, but not the level of Astra or Gemini Flash. V4.1 Flash is unstable: three translations of the same text ranged from 71 to 85, meaning that with a book of thirty chapters, some chapters will be noticeably worse than others. V4 Pro is more consistent but uses calques: 2 points out of 11 compared to 9–10 for the leaders. "Are you okay?", "I'm okay," "It's not about how wells work... It's about what you're looking at." And both are slow: 45,000 tokens of output per chunk for Flash versus 17,000 for Opus 5.5, and 17–21 minutes per chunk via the router versus two to four for Western models.

GLM 5.3 rose higher than all other Chinese models: 86, on par with Sol. It didn't miss any calques, scoring 11 out of 11, and meaning accuracy was 14 out of 15. However, each of the three runs only got into the correct response format on the second attempt, and one run dropped to 78. Kimi K3's median score is on par with Opus, 78, but it's the most unstable model in the table: in two out of three runs, it only got into the format on the fourth attempt, and in one of them, it also included fabrications in twelve places and scored 41.

For final translation, saving money on Chinese models is not yet feasible: the best results come from Western models, and GLM 5.3, the closest to them, is unstable from run to run. For a draft that will still go through a strong editor, DeepSeek is suitable and cheaper than Opus. And if you need to save money without losing quality, Gemini 3.8 Flash provides it.

Other Chinese models—Qwen, MiMo, MiniMax, Hy4, LongCat—are currently being tested; their results will be added to the table in the repository and in the comments.
