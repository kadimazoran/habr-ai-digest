In the article "AI and the Inflation of Quality," I wrote that the sharp decrease in the cost of intellectual labor is rapidly changing our requirements for its results. If an additional check takes a few minutes, people start doing it. If ten options can be quickly reviewed, the first one that comes to mind no longer seems sufficient. Yesterday's "too long and expensive" becomes today's "why not?", and quality begins to grow along with capabilities.

This process has another aspect that is somewhat lost in the endless comparison of humans and machines.

We are used to counting AI individually. Can one agent write code better than one programmer? How much more productive is one analyst with AI than an analyst without it? How many employees can a person with a good electronic assistant replace? These are reasonable questions that lead to equally reasonable labor market arithmetic: if one employee now does the work of two, the second becomes redundant.

A Division of Mathematicians

But then OpenAI demonstrated a different way of counting intelligence. To work on the Navier-Stokes problem, they launched about 10,000 agents that worked for 88 hours. And what's more interesting to me in this story is not another round of discussion about how smart the models have become, but the number itself.

Ten thousand.

If you put ten thousand good live mathematicians on one problem for 88 hours, I would also expect something interesting from them. The question is how exactly to put them to work. Ten thousand mathematicians need to be divided into groups, directions assigned, intermediate results collected, duplication noticed, dead-end branches closed, useful findings transferred between groups, and people constantly reassigned to where progress is being made. After some time, the mathematical problem begins to compete for attention with the problem of managing a division of mathematicians.

Human intelligence scales very poorly. Specialists can be hired by the thousands, but a thousand specialists do not turn into a specialist capable of simultaneously testing a thousand hypotheses. With each new person, not only an additional brain enters the system, but also the need to manage that brain, transmit information to it, and get results back. At some point, the cost of coordination begins to consume a significant portion of what we gained in quantity.

With agents, this problem doesn't disappear, but it becomes a technical one. They also need to be coordinated, they can also repeat each other, make mistakes, and go down a dead end as a crowd. It's just that to launch another thousand agents, you don't need to find a thousand specialists, give them laptops, onboard them, and rent a neighboring building. You need computational resources and a system capable of managing this whole zoo.

Kilogram for Kilogram

And here I have an association with the Sun.

A human body, per kilogram of mass, produces significantly more energy than an average kilogram of solar matter. If we consider specific power, the Sun looks quite unconvincing. It's just that its mass is about two nonillion kilograms, and with such a mass, mediocre specific power results in a star, near which human existence is possible only at a very respectful distance.

We are now very carefully comparing humans and AI kilogram for kilogram.

What if the strength of AI is not at all in the fact that one instance will someday become smarter than one human?

Human intelligence has so far been an almost custom-made product. A good mathematician can be trained, hired, lured away from a competitor, but you can't copy them ten thousand times and throw all the copies at one problem for three days. We are not even considering that finding a mathematician capable of understanding what the Navier-Stokes problem actually is, is another challenge. Therefore, most of our intellectual labor economy is built around the scarcity of people and the need to choose which tasks are worth spending their time on.

Swarm

With machine intelligence, this limitation begins to weaken. Instead of one very smart assistant for a task, you can unleash a swarm. Some agents explore solutions, others try to refute them, and still others check intermediate results. If a certain direction looks promising, more resources are sent there. If it turns out to be a dead end, it is closed. It is not necessary for every member of the swarm to be a genius. It is enough for the entire system to produce a useful result cheaper than the alternative way to obtain it.

And then the conversation about the labor market becomes somewhat more complicated than "one person now works for ten, so nine are fired.".

The Price of the Matter

This arithmetic is only valid under the condition that the amount of work is given in advance. There were a hundred tasks, after the advent of AI one employee solves ten, which means that to complete the same hundred tasks, ten employees will indeed be needed instead of a hundred. But in the real economy, the number of tasks is not fixed. What is fixed is the boundary beyond which a task becomes too expensive to solve.

Any company has plenty of such tasks. You can analyze processes more deeply, check more options, make individual solutions for small clients, recalculate forecasts more often, investigate minor deviations, automate operations that save ten minutes, test ideas whose probability of success is too low to dedicate a specialist's month to them. All of this is not necessarily useless. It's just that today the cost of the answer is often higher than the cost of the question.

It is this boundary that AI is pushing.

In "Inflation of Quality," the discussion was about one consequence: when an additional iteration becomes cheaper, we start doing more iterations and raise the requirements for the result. Scaling agents provides another consequence of the same process: when additional intellectual power becomes cheaper, we start setting tasks that were previously not worth setting at all.

Problems Don't End

This is also important because solving problems does not reduce their number.

Solving one problem allows us to build something that was previously impossible to build, after which the new system reveals its own set of problems. We solve them, get an even more complex system, and discover the next set. Cheap computations have not led to humanity finally calculating everything necessary. They have led to us starting to calculate weather, genomes, customer behavior, the movement of every package, the position of every car, billions of advertising auctions, and a huge number of other things that, at the previous cost of computation, simply did not exist as economically meaningful tasks.

The same thing can happen with intellectual work. The cheaper it becomes to solve a problem, the more problems fall into the realm of economic rationality. The more we can solve, the more complex systems we can afford. And complex systems quite reliably generate new tasks for those who built them.

Labor Market

This does not mean that AI will not replace anyone. It will. If a person's work consists of a set of operations that a machine performs cheaper and well enough, no one is obliged to keep a person for the sake of tradition. Some professions will shrink, some will disappear, and within the remaining ones, the distribution of work will change.

But an increase in productivity does not necessarily imply a proportional decrease in demand for intellectual labor. To assume that, we would have to assume that humanity needs a certain volume of solved problems, and after reaching it, we can all go home.

So far, we are behaving in the exact opposite way.

Each new level of capability quickly becomes ordinary, then mandatory, and then becomes the basis for the next level of requirements. This was the cause of the inflation of quality. Now, to this is added the ability to scale not only the number of attempts by one person but also the intellectual power available to them.

Therefore, in a few years, a valuable employee may not be someone who, with the help of AI, has learned to do the work of three people. This is a rather boring limit to the application of technology. Much more interesting is a person capable of setting a task that would previously have required a department, then unleashing hundreds of agents on it, understanding what produced by them makes sense, and based on the result, setting the next task that yesterday no one could afford to reach.

And this is a completely different labor market forecast. In it, AI does not simply take existing tasks away from people. It simultaneously sharply expands the space of tasks whose solution becomes accessible.

Kilogram of the Sun

We are still trying to understand the future by measuring intelligence on an individual basis. One person versus one model. One programmer versus one agent. One mathematician versus one artificial mathematician.

Perhaps this is the correct unit of measurement for a test.

For assessing consequences – I'm no longer sure.

A kilogram of the Sun also loses to a kilogram of a human, but the Sun is not bothered by this at all.
