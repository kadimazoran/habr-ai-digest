Qwen3.5-0.8B is a model with 752 million parameters, 18 GatedDeltaNet recurrent layers, and 6 attention layers. In tested runs with BF16 weights, it often fails to complete its reasoning within the 32,768 new token limit. The MIT CSAIL paper Sparks of Weight-Space Programming (Takehi et al., 2026) shows that editing weights without fine-tuning using MIT-p50c improves accuracy on MATH-500 from 7.9% to 48.1%—averaged over four vLLM runs with settings from the model card: a temperature of 0.6 and a penalty of 1.5 for already used tokens. The base model reaches the length limit in 93.0% of generations on this benchmark. Does the reduction in looping without random token selection and repetition penalties persist? What weight geometry differences accompany the behavioral change?

The study compares the geometry and behavior of MIT-p50c and SGNE-CC (Spherical Gated Norm-preserving Edit, Column-Constrained—a spherical weight edit preserving norms with recurrent gates, 3.13 MiB in size), obtained without fine-tuning by rotating output columns while preserving their original length. This method is termed DNA weight editing.

1. Weight Edit Geometry: MIT-p50c vs. SGNE-CC

In each of the two modified output matrices of MIT-p50c's feed-forward blocks—layers with indices 15 and 19, totaling 128 columns—the first singular mode accounts for over 99.999% of the increment energy. The norms of individual columns are increased by up to 114.3%. All 18 GatedDeltaNet recurrent layers, comprising 75% of the network's depth, remain untouched.

SGNE-CC modifies the directions of 802 output columns in 15 feed-forward layers and 1,182 columns in 9 recurrent layers. Attention layers are unchanged; additionally, one gate bias row in layer 16 is modified. The maximum deviation in the norm of a modified column after BF16 rounding is approximately 0.16%, with a median rotation angle of 8.8°. In the matrices of layers 15 and 19, the first singular mode accounts for 67.6% and 73.4% of the increment energy, respectively. Layer numbering starts from zero. The model's architecture, tokenizer, and execution program remain the same.

2. Mathematical Tests with a 32,768 Token Generation Limit (MATH-500)

Fig. 1. At zero temperature and without repetition penalty, SGNE-CC completes generation more often than MIT-p50c. A stratified sample of 100 problems from seven sections of MATH-500; A100, BF16. Strict scoring requires completion of generation and a correct result in the final answer box.

Under strict scoring, the base model solves none of the 100 problems, MIT-p50c solves 4, and SGNE-CC solves 44 (McNemar's paired test, p < 0.0001). The average response length for SGNE-CC and MIT-p50c is 6.0k and 31.6k tokens, respectively, with the generation limit being reached in 10% and 96% of cases. For MIT-p50c, reasoning remains incomplete in 96 out of 100 problems; for SGNE-CC, there are 4 such cases.

In a problem involving the conversion of Cartesian to polar coordinates, MIT-p50c arrives at the correct result early but then repeats a verification paragraph 451 times until the generation limit is reached. SGNE-CC provides the final answer and stops at 2,394 tokens. When using the fallback method of extracting the answer from incomplete reasoning according to the MIT rule, SGNE-CC's advantage is maintained: 45 out of 100 versus 20 out of 100, with the base model achieving 7 (p < 0.0001). The primary MIT rule, which does not require generation completion, yields 44, 5, and 1, respectively.

Following the author's protocol, after freezing the weights, 49 problems were selected from 300 problems outside the calibration set for separate verification. At zero temperature, SGNE-CC solves 24 out of 49, while the base model solves none (p < 0.0001).

Fig. 2. In model card mode, SGNE-CC generates four times shorter than the external MIT-p50c benchmark, while accuracy comparison depends on the engine. The bars for the base model and MIT-p50c in the graph show the average of four vLLM runs from the MIT repository; SGNE-CC represents a single generator run on PyTorch.

Model card mode changes MIT-p50c's result on the same hundred problems from 4 to 45, while SGNE-CC's total score remains unchanged at 44 out of 100. In a direct run on PyTorch across all 500 problems, the base model solves 39 (7.8%), MIT-p50c solves 259 (51.8%), and SGNE-CC solves 235 (47.0%); nominal p = 0.048. The average length for SGNE-CC is 4.0 times shorter: 5.3k versus 21.1k tokens. The generation limit is reached 17.6 times less often: 1.6% versus 28.2%.

When compared to the first external MIT run in vLLM—241 out of 500, or 48.2%—no statistically significant difference with SGNE-CC was found (p = 0.67). This does not establish model equivalence. Compared to the average of four vLLM runs, SGNE-CC's responses are 4.1 times shorter, and reaching the limit is 20.7 times less frequent.

Fig. 3. On problems with difficulty levels 3–5, SGNE-CC reaches the generation limit significantly less often; MIT-p50c solves more problems. Direct run on 367 MATH-500 problems in model card mode.

On difficulty levels 3–5, the 32,768 token limit is reached by 124 MIT-p50c responses out of 367 (33.8%) and by 7 SGNE-CC responses (1.9%)—a difference of 17.7 times. The average length for SGNE-CC is 3.7 times shorter: 6.3k versus 23.5k tokens. Correct answers are 143 (39.0%) for SGNE-CC versus 156 (42.5%) for MIT-p50c, p = 0.24; the base model solves 17 out of 367 (4.6%). In elementary and high school algebra, MIT-p50c performs better: 96 out of 142 versus 77 out of 142 for SGNE-CC. Across the sum of four sections—advanced algebra, calculus, geometry, and combinatorics with probability theory—the scores are reversed: 37 out of 178 for MIT-p50c versus 46 out of 178 for SGNE-CC.

On MGSM-240, SGNE-CC's shorter generation is accompanied by a significant loss in accuracy compared to MIT-p50c: 100 correct answers versus 137, considering the consolidation of translations for 40 problems. In logic and code generation, both edits yield more correct answers than the base model; SGNE-CC, in particular, generates shorter responses than MIT-p50c and rarely reaches the limit. In multilingual generation, the automatic filter checks for response completion and repetition thresholds but not content accuracy. In model card mode, both edits exhibit occasional hieroglyphic insertions; SGNE-CC still shows repetitions in German—4 cases out of 8.

Column norm preservation has been verified on the weights. To isolate its causal contribution from the edit's rank, layer set, and the gate bias in layer 16, separate experiments are needed.
