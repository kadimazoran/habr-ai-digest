AI helps find vulnerabilities faster, analyze fixes, and prepare exploits. The window for technological work doesn't get longer. The team still has compatibility checks, approvals, and systems that need to work. The attacker only needs to find one suitable vulnerability.

The more findings, the more pressing the question: what to fix first? Behind every update are people and time. If discovery accelerates while remediation remains at the same level, the queue of open issues grows.

Our approach to solving this problem is the open catalog cKEV Index. The catalog contains prioritized vulnerabilities based on the Urgent Patch Score (UPS) methodology. It compiles issues requiring urgent attention and events that explain their priority: exploit availability, confirmed attacks, and other evidence. The catalog presents the Urgent Patch and Emergency / IR stages with event history.

Home Alone

To accelerate an attack, it's enough to quickly understand an unfamiliar application, modify an existing exploit, or scan more systems, all of which AI is already helping with.

The September Anthropic report describes two illustrative cases. In the GTG-50014 campaign, attackers used AI at various stages, from reconnaissance to exploitation and processing of stolen data. One of them organized mass analysis of Android applications to find keys and credentials. In the GTG-50029 case, a lone attacker used agents for reconnaissance, code analysis, and validation of findings. Claude helped him develop and debug an exploit that worked on at least four websites.

In these cases, AI helped a small group and an individual perform work that previously would have required more specialists. Agents speed up the analysis of unfamiliar applications and the preparation of attacks against them. Humans choose the victims and manage the results.

Currently, the growth in capabilities is not only in finding unknown vulnerabilities but also in reducing the cost of developing exploits for already disclosed flaws – 1-day vulnerabilities. For attacks on mass-market products, the latter path is particularly attractive: a description of the problem and a released fix provide a ready starting point.

The usual buffer time between the publication of a vulnerability and the appearance of a working exploit may no longer exist. After reading a bulletin, you'll have to monitor what happens next.

Don't Boil Over

Researchers and developers use the same capabilities. One example is the open-source framework rust-in-peace, which is being developed for analyzing program security using AI agents. It combines several search methods, hypothesis testing, bug reproduction, and fix verification. The project's public registry contains about a hundred findings reported to open-source project developers, including the Linux kernel.

Not all found vulnerabilities are critical, and not all affected products are widely distributed. But this is an excellent demonstration of the problem: finding real, confirmed vulnerabilities is no longer a bottleneck. Each of them still needs to be fixed and the patch delivered to users.

Even for major developers, this work is already affecting their update release schedules.

For instance, in the August publication Where is Exchange SE CU1 anyway?, the Microsoft team explained the delay in the first cumulative update for Exchange Server Subscription Edition by the fact that the use of AI increased the volume of findings. Each of them needs to be confirmed, reproduced, fixed, and checked for regressions. Meanwhile, monthly security updates continued to be released. But for the CU1 release, the team waited for a quieter month: releasing a large update and immediately forcing administrators to install another one would mean doubling their workload.

Another example is Oracle, which in its July Critical Patch Update reported fixing 1434 different CVEs. At that time, it was the company's largest security update release. Among the reasons, Oracle cited expanded product coverage, the use of AI for problem detection, and accelerated development of fixes. Yes, this is a quarterly update, and not all vulnerabilities were found by AI. But the trend is clear. By our estimate, we are still far from saturation.

For a customer, each such release turns into a to-do list: check which systems are affected, find responsible parties, test the update, install it, and ensure the problem is resolved. This is handled by the same people who maintain the infrastructure and release new features.

Mind Games or How to Cope with the Next Wave

If a team receives thirty tasks every week and completes twenty, the queue grows by ten. Sorting will determine which twenty to do first. The rest will have to wait, and the next batch is already on the horizon.

What comes to mind when faced with the task of threat prioritization?

CVSS Base describes the technical severity of a vulnerability; additional CVSS metrics consider threats and environmental factors. EPSS estimates the probability of a CVE being exploited in real attacks within the next 30 days (CVSS documentation, EPSS FAQ).

However, choosing a patching strategy based solely on EPSS is not feasible. The paper Prediction Meets Patch Queues: Empirical Limits of EPSS-Only Prioritization Using CISA KEV Additions in 2025 examined a common approach: fix everything with a predicted probability above a chosen threshold.

A high threshold leaves out some vulnerabilities that are already being exploited. A low threshold drastically increases the volume of checks and fixes. In a resource-constrained model, the team faces a growing queue, and some critical tasks are still delayed. Moreover, a delay can occur even before the task is assigned: the necessary score may not be available yet, or it crosses the threshold too late. Additional staff will fix more tasks from the queue, but they won't see the vulnerability missed by this selection process.

In a real infrastructure, this is compounded by questions: is the vulnerable product installed, is the necessary function available, what privileges does the attacker require, what can they do? How long will the update take, and can access to the dangerous function be restricted until then?

The situation also changes. On Monday, a defect description is published; on Wednesday, details of its reproduction appear – PoC; and on Friday – a ready-to-use exploit module. Later, the developer confirms the vulnerability's use in attacks. The technical severity remains the same, but the urgency has increased.

UPS considers this history when choosing the next action.

Time, Forward!

The methodology is described in the study UPS Meets Patch Queues: Evidence-timeline prioritization under limited capacity, cadence, and compliance gravity.

UPS collects verifiable signals: publications, appearance of exploit tools, attack intelligence. Each event has a date and source. As information becomes available, the vulnerability moves to the next phase of work. A strong signal can immediately raise its priority: it's not necessary to go through all stages sequentially.

Each phase can be linked to team actions. Specific deadlines and responsibilities are set by the organization itself.

At the Emergency stage, the team checks if its systems are affected and looks for traces of a possible attack. The decision to start an investigation is made based on the results of this check.

The UPS taxonomy separately considers the publication of PoC, the availability of a ready-to-use exploit, and confirmed attacks. Maximum urgency is set for strong signals. Repeated retellings of the same message are filtered out so that the popularity of the news does not multiply its weight.

The timing of when the information became available is also important. If an attack was learned about on Friday, then the decision made on Monday must be evaluated based on the data the team had on Monday. The signal history preserves these grounds and helps understand when exactly the need to act faster arose.

Instrument Readings

The open cKEV catalog Index shows the two most urgent UPS stages – Urgent Patch and Emergency / IR. The card includes the cKEV score, phase, and a brief event history. Additional fields contain descriptions, CVSS, EPSS, exploit information, links, and recommendations – as data becomes available.

When developing the catalog, we aimed to link the final priority not only to a numerical score but also to the reason for urgency. Are attacks already exploiting the vulnerability? Is a ready-to-use exploit available? What is known about the required privileges and configuration? An empty field means the information still needs to be clarified.

The phase is considered separately: strong evidence can increase urgency even with a low score. Events that influenced the transition between phases are preserved in a shortened timeline, after which the record can be matched with affected systems and necessary actions.

The catalog includes vulnerabilities with confirmed exploitation as well as issues for which other serious reasons have emerged to accelerate remediation. For example, patch preparation may begin after a ready-to-use exploit becomes available, even before reports of affected organizations.

Thus, the structure of the record reflects the sequence that was implemented within the methodology: event → reason for urgency → phase → affected systems → action.

Matter of Technique

To turn a vulnerability record into a working task, we link it to a specific system, responsible party, and remediation method. A simple product name match is not enough: the version, configuration, enabled features, and access to them must be confirmed.

  1. First, we match vulnerability information with inventory and external perimeter scan results to determine which systems are actually affected.

  2. Then, we assess the potential consequences of exploitation and choose the most immediate action: installing an update, restricting access, disabling a feature, or changing the configuration. A temporary measure is only worthwhile after verifying that it effectively closes the dangerous path.

  3. After that, we assign a responsible person and a deadline. The task specifies the service, the required change, and the method for verifying the result. We consider that a single update package may cover multiple CVEs, and one CVE may require separate changes across multiple systems.

  4. The work doesn't end after assigning the task. We monitor new events: the appearance of a ready-to-use exploit, confirmed attacks, or bypasses of fixes may require re-evaluating the deadline. For such cases, a clear procedure for accelerated approval is needed in advance.

  5. After installing the update, we check the running instance of the application, ensure the correct configuration has taken effect, and re-verify that the vulnerability has been resolved. If signs of exploitation are found, the task transitions to investigating a potential incident.

Let's consider a hypothetical situation. A security bulletin is released for an external service. The update is scheduled for the next maintenance window, the team has identified affected instances, and prepared a test environment. Then, a ready-to-use exploit module appears, and the vulnerability moves to Urgent Patch.

Part of the work is already done: systems, responsible party, update procedure, and verification are known. The deadline can be revised, and a temporary measure can be implemented if necessary. If analysis only begins after an emergency notification, all these actions will have to be performed simultaneously.

This is the practical value of the early UPS stages: earlier signal classification allows preparatory actions to begin before an emergency situation arises.

Groundhog Day

How many tasks should be started in advance? Deep analysis of every weak signal will quickly consume all available time. Waiting for confirmed attacks will leave the team scrambling to prepare and install updates at the last minute.

The UPS study examined this trade-off in a limited-capacity queue model. The sample included 245 vulnerabilities added to CISA KEV in 2025. With varying capacity constraints, 35–53% of tasks were completed by the vulnerability's inclusion date in KEV due to earlier signals. The benchmark was the date of inclusion in the catalog, by which time exploitation was already confirmed (study and materials).

For your team, it's worth calculating the costs of initial analysis, identifying affected systems, testing, approval, remediation, and re-verification. This will reveal how many tasks can be prepared in advance and where work accumulates.

Initial matching with inventory can be done for a broad list. Detailed patch preparation – for selected issues affecting your systems. Emergency cases require a pre-approved workflow.

If the queue is still growing, conditions must change: close unnecessary external interfaces, automate testing, consolidate changes, or add personnel. The organization may accept some risks for a defined period. Such a decision must have a responsible person, a justification, and a review date.

Two Queues

For a security solution developer, there's a different queue: a new defect needs to be investigated, a detection method developed, and its accuracy and safety verified. The appearance of an exploit may require urgently revisiting a known vulnerability and refining the detection.

UPS helps choose which of these tasks to accelerate. For example, the availability of a ready-to-use exploit module increases the priority of developing a detection for the corresponding vulnerability. Confirmed attacks require checking if we can detect the issue for customers and what recommendations we can provide them.

For the customer, the queue consists of changes to their infrastructure. Here, vulnerability information explains the urgency, and the service owner plans the update considering its business impact.
