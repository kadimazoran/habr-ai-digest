A Little About Interviews

Recent interviews have made me think about how the value of technical expertise is changing in the job market. I'm not talking about AI starting to write code instead of developers – enough has been said about that. I'm more interested in something else: if technical implementation is becoming more accessible, what exactly does a company want from a person hired for a leadership position?

One of the recent conversations was about the position of CTO in a large company. The second was about a new project in fintech that required a manager. The project was to be launched, developers were already in place, but processes needed to be built from scratch. In both cases, the task was not just to manage development. It was necessary to form a working environment around oneself, grow a team, organize people's interaction, and ensure that all this worked as a single system.

I have been developing for a long time, and for the last eight years, I have been a manager. Almost a year ago, I left employment to understand new AI tools and test them on my own engineering experience. During this time, I created several tools for my tasks.

I didn't prepare software specifically for interviews. On the contrary, I made it because I myself lacked a convenient and sufficiently rigorous development process with AI. But something unexpected happened during the interviews: talking about my experience took 20 minutes, and then we moved on to the software I wrote, and, using it as an example, discussed approaches and solutions for the rest of the time.

Why is that?

When a Resume Is No Longer Enough

A resume tells you where you've worked, what positions you've held, what technologies you've encountered, and what projects you were responsible for. This is useful information, but it doesn't always help you understand how exactly a person makes decisions.

You can be involved in development for many years, know architecture, have experience working in large companies, and successfully lead teams. But when faced with the task of building a new process from scratch, an employer needs to understand how you will act in situations where there is no ready-made solution yet.

How will you decompose the task? How will you estimate the workload? How will you distribute responsibility? By what criteria will you know that the process is not working well? And, finally, how will you develop people so that the team doesn't depend on your personal involvement in every little thing?

These questions can be answered with words. But it's much more interesting when you have your own working tool that already implements some of your approaches to solving similar problems.

In my case, that's exactly what happened. I explained why I wrote the tools in a certain way and what problems I was trying to solve. The conversation shifted from general questions about experience to specific engineering solutions.

For example, I talked about a system for calculating product complexity based on COSMIC. This is a method of functional software measurement that allows estimating functional size through data movements. Instead of relying solely on a developer's subjective assessment, functionality can be formalized to obtain a measurable value.

What's important here is not the fact of using COSMIC itself. I'm interested in the possibility of transferring a similar approach to the development process, where the assessment should have clear grounds, rather than being a number that someone called out in a meeting.

If we are building a new product, we need to understand what makes up its complexity. What functional requirements form the scope of work? What can be estimated now? What decisions have not yet been made, and how will they affect further implementation? How to correlate the estimate with the team's capabilities?

COSMIC itself won't answer all these questions and won't turn functional size into an accurate timeline forecast. But it provides a foundation on which to build a further estimation model. And that's much more interesting than just asking a neural network to estimate how long development will take.

When I talked about this in an interview, it wasn't just about the technical implementation of the tool. We discussed the approach itself: how to make the estimation well-founded, how to use it in practice, and how such solutions can fit into the company's processes.

And here I saw an important point for myself. My personal tools became a way to show not only what I can do but also how I approach solving problems that arise for a technical leader.

Your Own Tool as an Extension of Engineering Experience

I've already written in an article about SpecaFlow that I started working on it due to the limitations of vibe-coding and standard Spec-Driven Development. I wanted not just code generation based on a description, but a consistent process with formalized requirements, planning, result verifiability, and change control.

As development progressed, it turned out that the task was much broader than I initially assumed. I had to delve into pre-project surveys, requirement formalization, roadmap design, layered development organization, and methods for controlling document consistency throughout the entire cycle.

Then came tests first, deviations, stage passports, and a mechanism for learning from each feature. Each of these solutions arose not because I wanted to add another feature to the product. I encountered a specific problem, looked for its cause, and tried to change the process itself so that the problem wouldn't repeat in the next development cycle.

For example, if during implementation it turns out that the initial requirement cannot be met as formulated, the model should not silently change the plan and continue working. The change must be agreed upon, documented, and propagated to related documents. Otherwise, after several iterations, we will get code that works but no longer matches the documentation, and the documentation will not match the original intent.

This is a quite familiar situation for someone who has been involved in developing large systems. The only difference is that now AI performs part of the work, which means that familiar engineering problems take on a different form and sometimes arise faster.

When I demonstrate such solutions in an interview, it allows for discussion of very specific things. Why did I choose this approach? What are its limitations? What will happen if the team works differently? How to ensure quality control without turning the process into endless document coordination?

It seems to me that this is where the part of expertise that is difficult to convey through a list of technologies is manifested. Behind each solution lies a specific problem that I have already tried to solve, and experience that led me to choose this particular option.

Of course, my tool is not a ready-made answer to all of a company's tasks. SpecaFlow was created for specific purposes, and corporate processes may have completely different constraints. But the engineering experience I invested in its creation is not limited to the product itself.

If I looked for a way to formalize requirements, control changes, and ensure the verifiability of implementation in my tool, then the same way of thinking is applicable when organizing a team's work. The specific solutions may differ, but the task remains recognizable: to make the process clear enough for people to work collaboratively and independently.

What Does Team Management Have to Do with It?

At first glance, it might seem that creating your own tools is a developer's job, and an interview for a leadership position should primarily discuss people management. But for me, these areas are not so sharply divided.

When you are faced with the task of growing a team and building processes, it's not enough to just assign tasks and appoint responsible parties. You need to understand how work flows from the appearance of a requirement to the finished result, where losses occur, and what solutions will eliminate them.

In one team, the main problem might be the quality of requirements. In another, constant changes in priorities. Somewhere, developers don't understand the basis for architectural decisions, and somewhere, each participant estimates task readiness in their own way. You can't take one universal regulation and expect it to work equally well everywhere.

Therefore, it is important for a manager not only to know existing practices but also to be able to understand the specific situation. Identify the problem, determine its cause, choose a suitable solution, and verify if it yielded the expected result.

This is exactly what I did when developing my tools. I wasn't just adding features to an application, but trying to understand why the existing way of working didn't suit me and what needed to be changed to get a more predictable result.

In a company, a similar approach can be applied at the team level. For example, if we want to improve the quality of task estimation, it's not enough to demand more accurate numbers from developers. We need to define what exactly we are estimating, agree on measurement rules, document assumptions, and see how the estimates correspond to actual results.

If we want to delegate work to AI agents, it's not enough to give everyone access to the model. We need to define the boundaries of autonomy, prepare the context, agree on checks, and understand when the decision should be returned to a human.

And if you need to develop people, the task is not for the manager to know the answers to all questions and provide them on demand. It's necessary to create conditions under which specialists will understand the goals, see the limitations, make informed decisions, and be responsible for their own area of work.

It seems to me that this is one of the main tasks of a technical leader: not to substitute for the team, but to organize its work so that it can solve problems independently.

AI Has Changed More Than Just Development

When I left employment, I was interested to see how far one could go with AI in software development. At first, like many, I experimented with rapid prototypes. But I quickly encountered the fact that getting a working first version is much easier than maintaining project quality in the long run.

The more I delved into this issue, the clearer it became that the problem wasn't just about the quality of code generation. A significant part of the complexity lies around it: in task definition, design, work organization, and result control.

Now, after the interviews, I've seen another side of the same story.

When a company hires a technical specialist, it doesn't just need a person who knows how to use new tools. It needs a person who can integrate these tools into a working system, determine where they are truly useful, and organize work taking into account their limitations.

If an agent can complete a task in a few minutes that previously took a developer several hours, it doesn't mean the entire process has become proportionally faster. More time may be required for context preparation, result verification, or error correction. And if the initial task is set incorrectly, automation will only lead to an incorrect result faster.

Therefore, deep technical knowledge still matters. Moreover, it is sometimes precisely this knowledge that allows for the maximum benefit from AI. A specialist who understands architecture, the subject domain, and the consequences of engineering decisions can set a more precise task and notice problems that would remain unnoticed by a person without the necessary experience.

But the practical application of this knowledge is changing. Previously, a significant portion of time was spent on direct implementation. Today, some operations can be delegated to agents, leaving design, coordination, verification, and decision-making to humans.

And with this, expectations of employees are also changing. As tools become more numerous and the entry barrier lowers, specialists are increasingly expected to be independent. Not only the ability to complete the assigned task but also the ability to understand what needs to be done, how to organize the work, and by what criteria to evaluate the result.

For a manager, this is even more noticeable. They need not only to work more effectively themselves but also to build processes where dozens of decisions by individuals and agents combine into a coherent result.

And here, my experience developing my own tools proved useful not only to myself. It allowed me to concretely demonstrate in an interview how I think about such tasks and what solutions I have already tried to implement in practice.

Instead of a Conclusion

I don't believe that having your own product automatically makes a person a good manager. You can write a complex system and still not be able to build a team. Just as you can successfully manage a large organization without creating your own tools.

But in my case, developing for myself became a way to continue engineering practice beyond direct coding. I looked for solutions to problems that are well-known from working in large companies, and gradually turned my experience into working mechanisms.

In interviews, this turned out to be more important than I expected. Discussing my tools allowed me to move from listing competencies to discussing how I make decisions and how I could apply this experience to the company's tasks.

Perhaps this is one of the effects that AI will bring to the labor market. When technical implementation becomes easier to obtain, the human ability to organize work around it plays an increasingly important role. To understand the problem, find an approach, select tools, verify the result, and build a process that can be reproduced without constant manual control.

Deep technical knowledge doesn't disappear anywhere. It becomes the basis for higher-level decisions. And the value of a specialist is increasingly determined not only by what they can do themselves but also by the work system they can build around themselves.
