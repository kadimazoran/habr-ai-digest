In 2023, RAG was de facto the only viable way to inject knowledge into LLMs. The context window was then 4–8K tokens, equivalent to about 10–20 pages of text. Attempting to load 400 pages of documentation into the model ended disastrously—only a portion of the document was processed. Alternatives, like Long Context, lost in accuracy—LLMs often remembered only the beginning and the end, with the middle being processed as a residual, which could lead to hallucinations.

But Why RAG?

Let's ask ourselves, why RAG? On one hand, it's convenient—natural language search over a knowledge base. But on the other hand, this solution stemmed from a lack of choice. As I mentioned, the context window was limited, and the model simply couldn't see the entire document.

RAG quickly took hold and became a sort of "lingua franca" for working with knowledge bases. It allowed for rapid processing of corpora of any size, whether megabytes or terabytes, with the vector database handling everything as resources permitted. RAG also provided links to sources. In enterprise and corporate settings, this is crucial for understanding that the AI hasn't fabricated anything and for referencing specific documents in reports. Furthermore, it helped save on tokens: instead of filling the model's context, RAG provides chunks. And the infrastructure—LangChain, vector databases (Milvus, Qdrant, etc.)—was often developed with RAG in mind. Ready-made solutions, guides, tutorials, courses—all were built around it.

Developers also became accustomed to RAG as the benchmark solution, and when faced with the need for documentation search, it's the first idea that comes to mind.

However, RAG isn't always suitable. Sometimes, a model can process documentation itself thanks to a large context window, making building a pipeline less cost-effective. For example, Long Context can easily replace all of this. Or can it?

Let's Analyze Long Context

Before we claim RAG is unnecessary, let's understand where, how, and why Long Context might fail, because 1M tokens don't always solve all problems.

Earlier, I mentioned that LLMs tend to focus on the beginning and end of a message. In 2023–2025, researchers (Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts and Found in the Middle: Calibrating Positional Attention Bias Improves Long Context Utilization) discovered that LLMs do not read context uniformly. The model's attention follows a U-shaped curve—information at the beginning and end of the context is processed well, while the middle falls into a "black hole."

Although the original "Lost in the Middle" paper used older models with small contexts, the problem is also present in modern models, albeit less pronounced due to the larger context. The U-shaped curve itself is a consequence of the transformer architecture (simplified, of course, but our article isn't about transformer specifics), not a training artifact.

The 2026 paper Lost in the Middle at Birth: An Exact Theory of Transformer Position Bias mathematically proves that the U-shaped curve is a consequence of the transformer architecture, not a training artifact.

The reason is a combination of causal masking (each token only sees preceding ones) and residual connections, which together create an asymmetric attention distribution. RoPE (Rotary Position Embedding), used in most modern models, further amplifies this effect through a decay mechanism: the further apart tokens are, the weaker the attention between them.

And in the current year, 2026, if relevant information is scattered across the context, with a lot of noise in between, and you need to extract multiple facts, accuracy drops.

RULER Benchmark: Effective Context Size is Less Than Advertised

The classic "needle in a haystack" (NIAH) test only checks the superficial ability to find a single fact. NVIDIA and other researchers created RULER—a benchmark that tests the ability to find multiple facts, trace a chain of connections (A → B → C), calculate a sum or average across the entire context, and understand value changes over 100K tokens.

Researchers tested 17 models with advertised contexts ranging from 4K to 128K tokens. On the simple NIAH test, almost all show near-perfect accuracy. But on RULER, the picture changes radically.

Only half of the models can effectively work with 32K tokens—even though they all claim support for 32K and above. Almost all models fall below an acceptable threshold long before reaching their advertised maximum.

Another concerning pattern: as context grows, models rely more on parametric knowledge (what they learned during training) and copy from the context instead of reasoning.

NVIDIA researchers formulate it this way: the advertised context is a ceiling, not a working zone. The effective context is usually 50–65% of the advertised. For modern models with 1M tokens, the real working zone might be 500–650K. This is already significantly better than in 2023, but there's still room for improvement.

To summarize the transformer architecture and the U-shaped curve: even with a model having 10M context, information in the middle will be processed worse than at the beginning and end. And if your document corpus consists of hundreds of pages where the answer could be anywhere, Long Context is not a reliable solution.

This is precisely where RAG shows its advantage: instead of hoping the model finds what it needs in 500K tokens, you explicitly provide it with 5–10 relevant chunks.

Context Fatigue (Context Rot)

There's another problem that is rarely discussed in Long Context articles but emerges in real-world use. Many might have encountered this: during a long conversation, the model might hallucinate facts, say things it wouldn't have said if asked in the first message. The model starts to get confused, refers to non-existent sections, mixes information from different parts of the document, and loses its ability to accurately locate information within the expanded dialogue context.

This phenomenon has been termed "context fatigue" or "context rot." It's directly related to what we discussed earlier: the more tokens accumulate in the window, the more sluggishly and poorly the model handles the task of extracting a specific fact, especially if that fact is not at the beginning or end, and even worse if there are multiple facts.

The model's attention is finite and uneven. When the context contains 500K tokens, of which only 2K are actually needed to answer the current question, the model expends resources filtering noise instead of answering.

Chroma Research conducted a study on 18 models and confirmed: performance degrades unevenly as context grows, and the model begins to follow outdated instructions from earlier turns, ignore fresh directives, or mix information from different parts of the conversation.

LongMemEval showed the same pattern: in dialogues of ~113K tokens, all models perform significantly worse on full prompts compared to focused ones. Adding irrelevant history forces the model to perform two tasks simultaneously—retrieval and reasoning—and both degrade.

Unfortunately, even RAG doesn't save the day here. You might get the right chunks for the first, second, and third questions, but by the fifteenth, the context is filled with previous answers, clarifications, corrections, and dead-end branches. The model, alas, can no longer distinguish what's important now from what was important ten turns ago.

Long Context works excellently for medium-sized texts. The first 10–15 questions yield 85%+ accuracy. But then the madness begins: the model gets overloaded, attention blurs, and accuracy drops to 65–70%. And this is with a cost 85 times higher than RAG.

RAG, in the same study, shows stable 85–90% accuracy throughout the session. Each query is independent—chunks don't accumulate in the context, and history doesn't clog the working memory.

The WiCER study on 17 RepLiQA domains (6,800 questions) confirms: full-context KV cache wins on curated knowledge (4.38 vs 4.08 out of 5) but degrades below RAG when scaled due to "attention dilution."

The context rot problem is solved by context management. Anthropic, in its recommendations for context engineering, suggests several strategies:

Compaction—when the conversation approaches the limit, summarize its content and start a new window with this summary. Claude Code works this way—it preserves architectural decisions, unresolved bugs, and implementation details while discarding redundant tool outputs.

Tool result pruning: If a tool was invoked deep in the history, its raw output can be discarded—it's no longer relevant.

Sub-agents: Specialized agents handle narrow tasks in clean context windows. Each sub-agent can use tens of thousands of tokens but returns only a compressed summary (usually 1000–2000 tokens).

In other words, a "retrieval pipeline" might be ideal, and the chunking strategy well-tuned, but if you're building a conversational interface on top of documents, managing that conversation itself is the weak point.

When you choose between Long Context and RAG, you're not deciding on the number of tokens, but on how the system will behave in long chats.

Long Context offers excellent quality for short sessions and is less resource-intensive but degrades with accumulated history. RAG, on the other hand, provides stable quality and predictable costs but requires infrastructure and resources.

RAG is Simpler Than It Seems

Finally, let's get to the core of the article—most people overcomplicate the RAG stack. They immediately jump to embeddings, vector databases, and re-rankers, when often the user just needs to find the document with the answer. You should start from the top (with the simple things) and move to complexity only when the data shows it's necessary.

Let's break down the six possible levels.

BM25 Only

Simple keyword search, the exact same algorithm used in search engines long before neural networks. BM25 ranks documents by term frequency, considering document length and term saturation.

This works when documentation is structured and users search for specific terms (function names, error codes, product IDs). If a person enters a specific term, BM25 will find it faster and more accurately than any embedding, because exact string matching is what BM25 does perfectly.

LLM Transforms the Query

Here, one model call is added. The user writes "how to fix what broke after the update," and the LLM transforms it into "rollback update instructions"—and then a standard BM25 search follows. This is suitable if queries are vague, users formulate questions in natural language, and the documentation is structured.

Hybrid Search (BM25 + Embeddings)

This is where embeddings first appear. BM25 selects candidates by keywords, and embeddings capture semantically similar documents. Results are combined using a weighted score (usually 0.3 BM25 + 0.7 cosine similarity, or vice versa). This works if the data is constantly updated and you need a balance of accuracy and completeness. It's a quite convenient and popular recipe, providing good quality without overcomplication.

Embeddings Without a Vector DB

Instead of storing all vectors in a database, documents are embedded at query time. This sounds strange but makes sense if you have 50 documents that change daily; storing their embeddings has little value as they quickly become outdated. Suitable if data changes frequently and daily, freshness is critical, and the number of documents is small (K = 20–50). The price for freshness is latency: 200–500 ms per query is spent generating embeddings.

Hot and Cold Tiers

An interesting solution based on the Pareto principle—20% of documents account for 80% of queries. Popular documents are stored in a vector database (fast but takes up space), while rare ones are embedded on the fly (slow but requires no storage).

Works if you have a clear separation between popular and rare content and are willing to maintain two levels of complexity for cost savings.

Classic RAG

All documents are pre-embedded, reside in a vector database, and a re-ranker re-orders the top-K candidates. This is the resource-intensive pipeline. Suitable if your data is stable, latency is critical, the corpus is large, and the documentation is extensive. The classic scenario is massive documentation that changes quarterly, with thousands of queries per day.

It's not always necessary to build RAG immediately, as simpler, more classic methods suffice for many purposes. If your documentation is small, you can get by using an LLM, or embeddings and BM25, or even a regular search.

But technical limitations are only half the story; the other half is money, and things get even more interesting here because economics can completely overturn a technical decision. Sometimes, Long Context is objectively worse in quality but so much cheaper to develop and maintain that the choice is obvious. Context Caching Breaks the Math

Everything we calculated above works without caching. However, modern providers offer prompt caching: if you send the same context prefix, it gets cached, and subsequent requests become significantly cheaper.

Many argue that for corpora up to 200,000 tokens, full context with caching can be cheaper than building a RAG pipeline. There's logic to this, because if the documentation is static, you pay for caching once, and then each request costs pennies.

But there's a catch that few mention. Caching only works for static prefixes. If you decide to insert relevant chunks into the middle of the context (as RAG does), the cache is invalidated, and you pay anew. Therefore, when using Long Context with caching, it's important to keep the static part—system prompt, instructions, the documentation itself—at the beginning, and dynamic elements—at the end.

If you do everything correctly, the economics of Long Context with caching become entirely different. Instead of $0.068 per query, you pay a conditional $0.01–0.02. The difference with RAG shrinks to 1.5–2 times, and considering the development and maintenance costs of RAG, Long Context might turn out to be the winner.

In conclusion, let's summarize everything into a payback formula: RAG Payback Point = Development and Maintenance Cost ÷ Difference in Query Price.

If you spent 200 hours developing a RAG pipeline (hypothetically $10,000), and each query saves $0.06 compared to Long Context, RAG will pay for itself after 166,000 queries. At 10,000 queries per month, this is 16 months; at 100,000 queries per month—less than two months…
