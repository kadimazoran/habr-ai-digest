I saw figures from the AIANA study — new analytics on the Russian AI market. The volume is estimated at 316.1 billion rubles (as of the end of 2025). The main customer, logically, is business: 71.9% of revenue comes from B2B.

98.4 billion rubles are for AI hardware and physical infrastructure. This is 31.1% of the market – the largest segment by revenue. For me, this is the main point: digital confirmation that the AI market is supported not only by models, applications, and prompts, but by "hardware." But then things get interesting.

The largest segment of hardware is computing "hardware." The first question that arose for me as someone from the energy sector was: was electricity forgotten in this segment?

As it turned out a couple of scrolls down, it wasn't forgotten. It's just that in business logic, energy is currently consciously left outside the AI market. Let's figure out why and what the risks are. How accurately does the picture without energy convey the real possibilities of launching new AI projects?

The Paradox of AI Market Product Logic

The study is based on product logic with three basic levels:

  1. Hardware and servers;

  2. Clouds and platforms;

  3. Applications.

There is also a share of smart user devices, "AI enablers." Services and cybersecurity are integrated through the basic levels as emerging market segments. That is, a total of six segments are identified in the AI market. Energy is not among them.

The approach is justified: energy serves the entire economy, not just AI. I don't dispute the study's methodology, as it's understandable for the IT market. Energy engineers have a different perspective.

We ourselves hit this wall. We started with the development of models and applied services — and eventually came to the conclusion that for AI, we need to design our own engineering base: power supply, cooling, redundancy, monitoring, and security.

Here's an example. For data centers, they are already starting to design their own gas generation for tens and hundreds of megawatts. If it's an AI data center, the cost of building and operating a distributed power center is included in the cost of the AI service. A new energy asset significantly affects costs, timelines, and the pool of those responsible for the project.

In short, 98 billion rubles is what is already within the AI market. Market boundaries do not always correspond to the necessary conditions for launching projects. An unforeseen cost item (in our case, energy) can become a critical limitation: if the available power does not appear on time, there will be nothing for the AI to run on, and the project will stall.

Clouds and Pilots Don't Cancel the Outlet

The AI market is B2B-oriented. Partially, businesses operate in the cloud, and partially, they launch AI pilots on their own or leased resources. In general, we are actively exploring models.

You don't necessarily have to buy your own GPUs and can go to the cloud. But servers, electricity, and cooling are not canceled in this case — the bill for them simply goes to the cloud provider. This means the market's need for them remains.

The number of pilots will decrease in the near future. Businesses are not interested in prolonged experiments — they need real, reliable AI tools integrated into processes and easy to use. Only after their appearance can we continue to "pilot" hypotheses.

To move from pilot to industrial operation, you need resources and a chain of responsibility. The closer the AI service is to the real business process, the more tangible the tasks of power supply, cooling, and dispatching become. At this stage, AI ceases to be just an IT topic, and CIOs, CISOs, energy specialists, technical directors, and business process owners get involved in the project.

And here we again run into engineering — not just energy, but the entire data center infrastructure. In my opinion, the structure of the AI market will gradually have to account for distributed generation and modular data centers for AI workloads.

Responsibility (Non-)Alignment

Currently, the AI market is highly fragmented. There are separate GPU and server suppliers. Separate data centers and clouds. Separate model developers. Separate integrators, and so on.

System integrators exist. But the distribution of responsibility zones when launching and operating AI projects is still not transparent.

The reason is simple: even when the supplier of each individual solution is strong in their area, if the technical solutions are not coordinated in advance, problems will arise during launch — and fixing them afterward will be difficult and costly.

This problem exists in almost all engineering systems and industries. Here's a simple example from the energy sector. A power plant, distribution equipment, and a control system are assembled by different suppliers. During commissioning at the site, everything works well individually, but together it doesn't start up properly because each supplier handled their part without coordination with other components of the power system.

Let's translate the example to "engineering AI":

  1. Servers were selected, but the thermal model was not calculated.

  2. Cooling was provided, but emergency scenarios were not checked.

  3. The platform was launched, but it was not determined who would control the quality of responses.

The list can go on indefinitely. The essence: the engineering works, the software works, the network works — but the system as a whole is unstable.

Energy may remain outside the AI market statistics, but it cannot be left outside a real project. Therefore, I expect an increase in demand for those who can integrate power supply, cooling, computing, and software into a working system and take responsibility for its launch and operation. At large AI sites, the integrator's competencies will determine whether the resources turn into a working service.
