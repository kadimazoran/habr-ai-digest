How an Attempt to Make Call of Duty Turned into a Spell

It all started when Matt Shumer showed Claude of Duty in July 2026 — his well-functioning browser shooter created in Claude Code with a single prompt. This had the effect of a bombshell because before that, no one had ever done anything like it with a single prompt. Later, he published the shooter's code and his prompt.

The very post

The post quickly gained millions of views. Since this game was of higher quality than anything other users had created with Claude Code, and it was claimed that the result was obtained with a single prompt, many considered it a fake. Among the skeptics were even very prominent AI specialists and well-known vibe coders.

But on July 27, 2026, Matt Shumer described a new prompting approach in detail in his article.

The agent was given an ambitious goal and a benchmark — the real Call of Duty. The work was proposed to be distributed among sub-agents, and the result of each was sent to a separate strict critic. The critic had to look at the image, compare it with the benchmark, and send back for revision anything that didn't measure up. Thus, instead of a single answer, a repeating cycle was formed: creation, comparison, feedback, correction. This scheme is directly written in the published prompt.

In further description, Shumer emphasizes two details: the verifier needs fresh context, and they must evaluate the real result — a working program, screen, or tests. This is precisely what makes the idea interesting for one's own projects: one can pre-define what constitutes a good result and organize its sequential verification. Description of the method

At the same time, Shumer himself admits: the project did not reach the level of the real Call of Duty. Therefore, this story shows a method of organizing work, and it's too early to promise guaranteed AAA quality from it. Project README

Subsequently, new skeptics emerged: for huge projects, this approach alone is not enough, and it consumes too many tokens. The problem is that the swarm of agents (executors and critics) consists of individual participants, each having only part of the overall context. As a result, when creating sufficiently large projects, the output is perfectly polished pieces of code (which consumed a lot of tokens) but poorly fitted together.

Therefore, a user cannot create a truly complex project with a single prompt using this method yet. One Vector — Two Coordinate Representations and One Geometric Object

On the left and right, the same vectors are depicted: the blue color denotes the original x, and the orange color denotes its image Ax. But the grid on the right is tilted, so the coordinates of the blue vector are different: instead of (3, 2), we get (1, 2). Two copies of the drawing allow comparing the representations simultaneously, keeping the geometric object itself in view.

This section explains the fundamental difference between a vector and its coordinates.

Coordinates describe how to express a vector using a chosen basis. The vector itself does not depend on the basis. Students need to grasp this idea.

This is a good pause for the audience: ask them to reconstruct the blue arrow from the right-hand representation — one b₁ and two b₂ — and verify that the arrow's tip has indeed remained in place.

How to See an Impossible Transformation

On slide 14, "The Impossible Image of a Pentagon," it is proposed to check whether one drawn figure can be a linear image of another.

In the original pentagon, the entire segment between points u and v exists, including its midpoint. But in the supposed image, the midpoint between Au and Av is missing.

For a linear operator A, ((u + v) / 2) = (Au + Av) / 2. Therefore, the image of the midpoint of the original segment must fall on the midpoint of the segment between the images of its endpoints. If this point is not present in the drawn figure, such a figure cannot be the image of the original.

Here, the student needs not to calculate another image, but to recognize an impossible result.

One can first ask to find the contradiction in the drawing and only then present the formula. One can suggest that it is enough to track one point.

It is also possible to stop here and have a useful educational discussion with students about how convexity and linearity are related.

A Third Coordinate Does Not Yet Create Volume

On slide 15, "Image of a Disk in Space," a third coordinate is added to the two: (x, y) maps to (x, y, x + y). Before showing the result, one can ask the audience: since there are now three coordinates, will the image of the disk acquire a three-dimensional volume?

All obtained points satisfy the equation z = x + y, so the image lies entirely in one oblique plane. The disk turns into an elliptical disk in space, but it does not gain volume. The third coordinate is calculated from the first two and does not add another independent direction.

In all three frames, parameters a and b are equal to 1, and point p = (0.8, 0.4) maps to the same point Tp = (0.8, 0.4, 1.2). Only the camera angles change: the figure's tilt on the screen is different, but the transformation rule and the result's coordinates remain the same.

One can ask students to find what has changed and what has remained the same, and explain why rotating the camera does not add volume to the image.

This way, they can understand the difference between the number of recorded coordinates, the dimensionality of the surrounding space, and the dimensionality of the image itself.

Where the Columns of the Product Come From

Here, the matrix product is explained through the already familiar idea: the columns of a matrix are the images of the basis vectors. After the action of A, these images become the inputs for B. Therefore, to obtain each column of BA, it is sufficient to apply B to the corresponding column of A. In the example, the first column (2, 0) remains unchanged, and the second, (0, 1), becomes (1, 1).

The illustration connects the new algorithm to the definition of the operator matrix.

A student can first find the geometric image of a basis vector, then write down its coordinates, and only after that recognize the column of the product in the result. The formula in the frame generalizes precisely this chain of reasoning.
