Three Questions for Those Promising Safe AGI for Humanity

*aAtS — and All That Stuff

Suppose tomorrow some company announces: we have created AGI/ASI/A[MEGA-SUPER-LUXURY-JOIN-NOW-WITH-A-HUGE-DISCOUNT]I aAtS — artificial intelligence at or far beyond human level, depending on the press release's boldness. And we have solved the alignment problem: the system understands our intentions, adheres to constraints, and acts as expected.

Excellent.

Imagine the next step. The state nationalizes such an intelligence and tasks it with defending national interests. It grants access to resources, infrastructure, and means of coercion. It allows it to select and execute actions that affect the standing of other countries. Of course, according to the rules. In the interest of security. Under responsible leadership. Such an AI doesn't necessarily need unlimited power. Significant authority is enough for the decisions of one owner to become a problem for others. This is where the three questions begin.

1. Are You Ready to Give Your AI a Shotgun?

You can trust a system to write code, plan logistics, and analyze research. This doesn't mean you're ready to let it independently apply force. Therefore, a "yes" answer only makes sense with a continuation: what to do, under what conditions, with what limitations?

What decisions does the system make itself? When must it stop? Who can revoke its authority? Who changes the constraints — and who can declare an exceptional situation? And who decides when the exception ends? If the answer is "no, such decisions must remain with humans" — that's a perfectly reasonable position. But then your trust in the system has a limit.

In its most general form, alignment means aligning AI behavior with human intentions and values. By itself, this doesn't promise to solve issues of political power. But if alignment with the owner's intentions is used as a basis for transferring authority, one must examine both the intentions themselves and the owner's right to implement them. "The system follows our instructions" is just the beginning of the conversation. What instructions are you allowed to give it?

Responsibility also doesn't disappear. If the system causes harm, who will be held accountable to the victims? Giving a machine authority doesn't mean solving who must compensate for damages. The program itself can be shut down. But this doesn't necessarily make things easier for those who were harmed. "We fixed the model" is a good response to a bug report. People whose lives it broke will need something else.

If the final decision remains with a human, the question of power doesn't disappear. A human in the control loop is the address where the question needs to be sent.

2. Is Everyone Else Ready for You to Do This?

Suppose you answer: yes, we are ready. Our AI is reliable enough. Tests have been passed. The state has permitted its use. According to its own rules. But have others accepted your right to transfer such authority to it?

This isn't about personal permission from every person on Earth. It's about the basis on which affected parties should recognize your decision: what agreements are in place, who represents their interests, and how are objections resolved? Tests answer the question of the system's operation. State permission answers the question of its admission within a certain order. Neither, by itself, explains why others should accept actions directed against them as permissible.

A national mandate is not global legitimacy.

Imagine an AGI that impeccably defends its state's interests. It doesn't deceive its leadership. It doesn't go out of control. It doesn't invent its own goals. It's tasked with strengthening strategic superiority. It finds ways to increase other countries' dependence, limit their capabilities, and shift risks onto them. If its authority allows it to do so, and its constraints don't protect affected people, then precisely executing the task amplifies the harm. For the owner, the system works perfectly. For others, it might look very different. When the owner's interests conflict with others', and the protection of others is insufficient, the danger can come precisely from an obedient AI. It doesn't need to rebel for this to happen.

"We are doing this for humanity" doesn't close the issue. Where did humanity participate in the decision? Who represents those who will bear the costs? Who can challenge the system's actions and achieve something other than an explanation that details are classified?

Even a safety report requires a question: whose losses are counted in it? If the system reduces risks for its own country by shifting them to neighbors, an internal report might honestly show improvement. But it doesn't prove the world has become safer. "The decision is made by our leadership, verified by our agencies, and the grounds are classified for national security reasons" — this describes the decision-making process. It doesn't yet explain how others are protected. The room where the decision was made is not the entire world.

3. Are You Ready for Everyone Else to Do the Same?

Now imagine that another country has the same AGI. One whose leadership you don't trust. Whose goals you consider dangerous. Which describes events completely differently. They also say: we've checked everything. Our AI is safe. We act for the common good. Are you ready to grant them the right to the same shotgun? You don't necessarily have to hand out weapons indiscriminately. "The same" means comparable authority under comparable requirements for reliability, constraints, and responsibility. Differences between owners can matter. But then it's precisely these differences that need to be presented. Are you ready to apply the same requirements to yourself that you propose for others — even if your rival meets them and you don't?

You can reject bad rules for both sides. A double standard begins where an exception is required for your own system that you are not prepared to justify on general grounds. As long as the AGI belongs to you, it's progress. When a rival gets the same thing, it's a proliferation problem of dangerous technologies. What has changed: the machine's capabilities, the conditions of its use, or just the owner's name?

Sometimes the answer truly justifies different decisions. Sometimes, behind the words about responsible leadership, lies a desire to maintain an advantage. Verifiable grounds for admission and readiness to accept an inconvenient outcome help distinguish these cases. An exclusive right to a powerful AGI requires more than confidence in one's own responsibility.

Please, don't put a halo on the shotgun. It scratches the barrel.

What If Everyone Answered "Yes"?

Suppose the right to have such systems is mutually recognized. Common requirements are established. Several parties receive permission to act within agreed-upon limits. The temptation arises to exhale. You have a powerful AGI aAtS. We have a powerful AGI aAtS. No one has a monopoly anymore. Why not achieve stable parity?

With nuclear weapons, at least the basic mechanism of threat is clear: strike, response, destruction. Parties assess arsenals, delivery systems, probable targets, and consequences. Assessments can be wrong. But for mutual deterrence, you don't need to agree on who is right. You need grounds to expect that after your strike, you will receive a response and may lose everything you intended to protect.

AGIs with broad authority have capabilities that are not limited to one pre-known method of influence. They find their own ways, build strategies, and react to others' decisions. Nuclear weapon owners also do all this. But here, we delegate part of the strategy search and execution to the machine — within the scope of a specific owner's task.

The mere existence of a "similar AGI" in a rival's hands doesn't inform what actions it will find, what response will follow our move, and where the system is obliged to stop. Similarity in capabilities alone is not enough to explain the mechanism of mutual deterrence. Recognizing the right to have a system doesn't mean pre-agreeing on every application or learning to resolve new conflicts. Especially if each of these intelligences is primarily obliged to protect its owner.

A telling picture is needed here.

One might object: but sufficiently intelligent systems will be able to negotiate. They will understand the facts, see the consequences, and find a way that humans missed. Imagine: parties have recognized each other's right to use AGI for defense. But both claim the same territory. Each considers it theirs and tasks their system with ensuring exclusive control. A general permission for defensive use has not resolved the territorial dispute. Each side calls its own actions defense and the other's a violation of the agreement. Concessions are forbidden. There is no common arbiter whose decision both sides are willing to accept on this dispute.

Both AGIs may perfectly understand the situation. Agree with the facts. Detect lies in their owners' statements. Even find a compromise that would allow avoiding conflict. But detecting a lie doesn't mean getting the right to abandon the assigned task. And finding a compromise doesn't mean getting permission to accept it. The AGI explains to the owner why the compromise is reasonable. The owner replies: great, now find a solution where they make concessions. But on the other side, it's the same.

One AGI says to another: "I understand why you need this solution. But mine require something else." The second replies: "I understand too. Mine do not allow concessions." Humans seem to have withdrawn from negotiations. But they remain within the goals, prohibitions, and priorities of their machines. And they continue to steer: national security, our interests, this position must not be surrendered, this advantage must be preserved.

We have automated the negotiators. The contradiction between the clients remains. In our example, it's impossible to satisfy both requirements simultaneously. Additional intelligence might help establish this more precisely. But the proof of incompatibility itself doesn't solve whose order ceases to be valid.

What should the system do now?

It can stop. Request new authority. Suggest changing the requirement. Owners might allow a compromise. But suppose the owner replies: the task remains. Concessions are forbidden. I do not permit ceasing task execution. Force is permitted. Then the search continues — now among methods of coercion.

Arguments have ended. Authority has not.

The machine can calmly determine that the other side will not agree voluntarily and look for a way to force them. The second system might reach the same conclusion. This doesn't necessarily mean both will open fire. The threat of retaliation might deter them. They might find another move or convince their owners to reconsider prohibitions again. But it's precisely these constraints and capabilities that need to be shown. "They are smart enough" is not enough.

The logic here can remain sound. Each system follows its owner's instructions and undertakes actions permitted by them for its goal. Obedience doesn't guarantee success — neither for it nor for the rival. However, attempts to achieve success can cause quite real harm. Therefore, the question begins even before transferring power to machines: who resolves disputes about the limits of authority? What must systems do when they cannot agree within the scope of their given tasks? Who has the right to change these tasks? Can a system cease actions against the owner's demand to continue? If an agreement is reached, who is authorized to accept it? Who will verify its execution? What will happen when the owner again needs an exception for national security reasons?

We don't have to agree on everything. But we need a process that allows for disputes, acceptance of limitations, and adherence to agreements. Otherwise, we are transferring our conflicts to AGI along with the authority to continue them — and hoping that intelligence itself will produce the agreement that the owners failed to reach. Three "yeses" are the beginning of this conversation. Not a certificate of its successful completion.

What Exactly Does the Shotgun Test?

This is a checklist for an AGI owner who claims the right to transfer real power to it.

Do you trust your system? Show the grounds and limits of your trust.

Have others recognized your right? Show where your internal permission ends and the agreement with them begins.

Are you ready to recognize the same right for others? Show that your requirements withstand a change of owner.

And then check what happens when instructions clash. Each side may permit its machine what the other considers unacceptable. Who has the right to change tasks when they conflict? The system's intelligence itself doesn't give its owner the right to decide for others. A national permit doesn't become a global mandate. The reliability of individual systems doesn't guarantee the safety of their interaction.

Therefore, next time you hear "our AGI is safe and serves humanity," ask:

Whose interests is it tasked with protecting?

Who will have to live with the consequences?

Who can tell the owner "no"?

Perhaps there will be good answers. But if, instead of answers, you are shown a presentation about values again, remember the shotgun. One of the most unpleasant possibilities is that the AGI will act strictly according to the owner's instructions. It is not obliged to work for you. But what obliges its owner to consider the harm the machine might cause you?

If there is no answer to this, the machine's impeccable obedience is cold comfort.
