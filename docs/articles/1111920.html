My name is Petr Voronin, and I own three Oh My Geek stores in Astrakhan with a monthly revenue of 3-4 million rubles. Until recently, I knew nothing about programming. But with the help of AI, I learned to create programs myself that help manage my business. I'll tell you how I managed it and what tasks I now solve without developers.

Opened a printing house instead of becoming a doctor

I am a doctor by education. I graduated from medical academy, but I never wanted to work in the specialty. My parents are doctors, so it was assumed that I would follow in their footsteps. But I wanted to be in business.

At 24, I opened a printing house. My father suggested the idea: the hospital where he worked constantly needed printed forms. My mother lent me money for the first risograph machine, which cost 150,000 rubles. Some acquaintances also invested funds.

I knew nothing about printing at the time. I didn't consider that I would also need a paper cutter for the work. At first, I had to secretly carry paper to the printing house at the medical academy to have it cut.

Gradually, I expanded the printing house: in addition to risograph printing, we started doing offset and large-format printing, and design. Eventually, the staff grew to 30 people.

How a hobby with board games turned into a business

In parallel, I became interested in board games. It all started with "Settlers of Catan," which I bought at a bookstore one day. My wife and I started playing a lot, then I began gathering other board game enthusiasts.

First, we held free game nights in cafes, then came festivals and championships. Some events attracted participants from other cities. We even held qualifying tournaments for Magic: The Gathering, the winners of which went to international competitions.

Gradually, this hobby grew into a board game club, and a small store appeared alongside it. At first, it occupied only 5-10 square meters. Later, the store expanded to 50 square meters, and I named it "IgroSlon" (Game Elephant). We sold mainly board games and comics.

Around 2011-2012, I started using MoySklad (My Warehouse). A business acquaintance from Arkhangelsk, who also had a board game store, recommended the service to me. I was looking for a product accounting program that would be simpler than 1C. I tried MoySklad, I liked it, and we've been using it for accounting ever since.

The problem was that I perceived the store more as a hobby than a business. I enjoyed organizing events and gathering people, but I didn't create a financial model.

Sales were low. At one point, my credit card debt reached almost 200,000 rubles. The store brought in little money, and I had to support my family.

Over time, my father got involved in the printing house's work, and I gradually stepped away from it. Selling board games became my main occupation, even though the store brought in much less money.

Oh My Geek

Around 2016-2017, I realized that I couldn't earn much from just board games and comics. I needed to expand the assortment and attract new customers.

I had almost no personal funds for development, so I started looking for partners. I found people who liked the idea of opening a larger store for fans of games, comics, and other geek hobbies. One of the partners still works with me.

We opened a store of about 70 square meters in one of Astrakhan's shopping centers. We named it OMG — Oh My Geek.

I brought the inventory from my "IgroSlon" store, and the partners added theirs. At first, each sold goods on separate shelves and received profit from their own sales. But over time, we abandoned this scheme and started working with a common inventory.

The store opening didn't require large investments. The main expenses were for renovating the premises and the first rent payment. We got a spot in the back of the shopping center that wasn't in demand by other tenants.

Customers gradually moved from "IgroSlon" to OMG. The old store became unprofitable, so I closed it.

In 2018, we opened a second location in another shopping center. For a long time, we had two stores, and in 2026, a third one appeared.

The assortment also changed. Now we sell not only board games and comics but also collectible character figures, mugs, socks, unusual products, and other gifts. For example, we have a lot of Asian snacks, sweets, and drinks.

More than half of our sales now come from food products. Board games remain part of the assortment, but it has become difficult to earn solely from them.

How we entered marketplaces and why we left

During the pandemic, our stores were closed for several months, and we were left with almost no sales. That's when I realized that I couldn't rely only on physical stores and decided to develop sales on marketplaces.

To do this, we organized a separate warehouse and set up order fulfillment and delivery. Drivers picked up goods from the stores and took them to the warehouse.

Sales were growing. In 2024, the business turnover reached approximately 50 million rubles, with over 60% coming from marketplaces.

It seemed like we had found a good way to scale. But then problems began.

First, manufacturers started entering marketplaces independently. Sometimes they sold goods cheaper than we could buy them wholesale. It was impossible to compete with such prices.

Second, I made a serious mistake myself: I didn't calculate the unit economics carefully enough.

I saw the growing turnover and thought the business was profitable. But I didn't account for all the expenses that arise when selling through marketplaces. As a result, sales grew, but the business didn't become more profitable.

In December 2024, I decided to completely abandon marketplaces. I had to part with the employees who worked in this area and focus again on local retail.

This experience made me look at business management differently. I realized that I couldn't focus solely on revenue.

How I re-learned business economics

Before that, I spent a lot of time on other projects: organizing festivals, giving lectures, and engaging in public initiatives. The stores continued to operate, but I didn't always delve deeply enough into their economics.

After leaving marketplaces, I decided to change that.

First, I created a financial model. I calculated expenses for rent, salaries, purchases, and other items. I determined the necessary margin, average check, conversion rate, and the number of sales required for the stores to be profitable.

Then I focused on the assortment. Using MoySklad data, I started analyzing sales and inventory, figuring out which products were profitable and which sat on the shelves for a long time.

Gradually, I realized that I needed additional analytical tools that considered the specifics of our stores. Later, I started creating them using AI.

How I learned to program with AI

I started getting acquainted with large language models about two years ago.

I was living in Tbilisi then and learned about programming and LLM courses there. I decided to try, even though I had always considered myself more of a humanities person.

In the classes, we studied mathematics, programming fundamentals, and how language models work. Many participants already knew how to write code, while I had to learn from scratch.

Gradually, I realized that with AI, I could create programs for business myself. I don't have to write every line of code myself. I can explain the task to the model, get a solution, check how it works, and refine it if necessary.

I wrote my first full-fledged program in January 2026. Since then, I've been coding almost every day: creating something new or modifying existing programs.

Now I use various models, including Claude and Codex.

For me, this was a real discovery. Previously, if I needed a program for a specific task, I would have had to find a developer and explain what I wanted. Now, I can solve many of these tasks myself.

Connected AI to MoySklad

When I started programming with AI, I thought about how to use it with MoySklad. The system already stored data about products, sales, and inventory. I wanted the neural network to be able to work with this data directly: analyze information, create documents, and perform other tasks.

For this, I needed an MCP server – a tool that allows AI to interact with other programs.

I found MoySklad's integration with an MCP server. I already knew its developer, Yuri: he had previously helped us automate our work on marketplaces.

Around May 2026, I connected the integration and started experimenting.

At first, I just wanted to see what would happen. But gradually, I started finding more and more tasks that could be automated.

How AI helps control expiration dates

The first task was controlling expiration dates.

When we expanded our food product assortment, a new problem arose: snacks, sweets, and drinks have expiration dates. We constantly need to check which products will soon have to be written off.

Previously, employees monitored this themselves.

I started with a notification system that alerted about approaching expiration dates in advance.

Then I expanded its capabilities. Now the program considers not only dates but also sales. For example, it can determine that a product's expiration date is still far off, but it sells so slowly that it's unlikely to be sold by the target date.

For such products, I created a separate "anti-top" list. It helps identify problem items and make timely decisions: change the price, move the product, or adjust purchases.

This is especially important for us because food products now account for more than half of our sales.

How I automated transfers and employee work

We have three retail locations, and the same product can sell at different rates in each. In one store, it runs out, while in another, it sits idle.

I developed a program that analyzes sales and inventory, considers the specifics of each store, and calculates which products should be transferred.

For this, I use MoySklad data and my own algorithms.

I also created electronic checklists for employees.

When a salesperson comes to work, they receive a list of tasks: check certain products, look at expiration dates, rearrange products on shelves, and perform other actions.

The employee marks the completion of tasks, and the information goes to a Telegram bot. The system also has a timer so that tasks are not delayed.

This way, I can monitor the daily operations of the stores without constant calls and messages.

Report in 30 minutes instead of half a workday

We have bonuses for the best employees of the week and month. To determine the winners, I analyze sales and other performance indicators.

Previously, I had to export data from MoySklad, transfer it to spreadsheets, consolidate the figures, and calculate the results. Preparing the report took about half a workday.

Now, the automated system does most of the work. It retrieves the necessary data from MoySklad, incorporates information from employees' electronic logs, and generates the report.

All I have to do is check the result. This takes about half an hour.

The calculations themselves are performed according to predefined algorithms. I don't ask the language model to arbitrarily determine who worked best.

How AI adds products to MoySklad and the website

Another time-consuming task is processing new deliveries.

When a supplier sends an invoice, the product information needs to be entered into the accounting system. If there are many items, manual processing can take three to four hours.

Adding product cards to the website is a separate task. Previously, we hired an employee to manually fill in product information.

Now the process is different.

After receiving the invoice, automation is launched. It processes the document, creates the necessary data in MoySklad, and adds products and images.

Then, the information is transferred to the website through integration with InSales.

Simultaneously, the system can suggest how to inform customers about new arrivals: what to post on Telegram or VKontakte, which products to highlight in advertising, what idea to use for videos.

It turns out that one operation helps solve several tasks at once: from accounting for a delivery to preparing promotional materials.

Previously, an employee could manually add about 40-80 product cards to the website per day. Now, automation can process about 200 items per hour.

The difference in speed is huge, although the results still need to be checked.

What else I do with AI

I haven't limited myself to automating daily operations.

For example, I developed my own ABC/XYZ analysis tool. It helps evaluate the assortment and make purchasing decisions, considering the specifics of our stores and suppliers.

I can ask the system to analyze a specific product category, look at sales, calculate metrics, and suggest changes.

Recently, I needed to change the names of products in an entire category. Instead of editing each card manually, I gave the task to an assistant, and they made the changes through MoySklad.

I also use AI for website work, creating videos, analyzing documents, and preparing business correspondence.

For example, when a shopping center sends a lease agreement, you can upload it to the model and ask it to analyze the terms. This doesn't eliminate the need to check the result yourself, but it allows you to understand the document faster.

However, I haven't gained more free time because of this. On the contrary, I constantly come up with new ideas for improvement or automation.

Why I don't trust AI with everything

Despite all its capabilities, I don't believe AI can be left completely unsupervised.

Language models make mistakes. They can misinterpret a task, suggest incorrect code, or draw an erroneous conclusion.

Therefore, I double-check important results. For financial calculations, I use formulas and algorithms, and sometimes I additionally verify conclusions with another model.

I also limit access rights. For example, I'm not yet ready to allow an assistant to independently delete invoices in the accounting system.

My approach is: first, understand how the automation works, test it in practice, and only then gradually expand its capabilities.

How AI helps save money

A month ago, I realized that I no longer needed a separate office employee: AI now performs their tasks. We transferred the employee to work in the store, where people are actually needed. Their salary was 40,000 rubles per month, and with taxes, it was over 50,000. On this one position alone, we can save more than half a million rubles a year.

It's still difficult to assess the economic effect of all the automation: I come up with and implement something new almost every day. But the business is growing — this fall, the monthly revenue is about a million rubles higher than a year ago.

How much we earn now

Now we have three stores in Astrakhan and our own website.

In a typical month, revenue is 3-4 million rubles.

Rent for three premises, salaries, marketing, taxes, and other current expenses amount to approximately 1.3-1.4 million rubles. We spend a comparable amount on purchasing goods.

On average, net profit is about 10% of revenue; in good months, it can be higher.

The business is seasonal. The main sales occur from late December to March 8, when people actively buy gifts. During this period, revenue can be about three times higher than in a normal month.

Therefore, when planning purchases, I have to consider seasonality. If I order too much stock, some money will remain frozen in inventory after the holidays.

Now I try to make such decisions based on calculations, not intuition.

What's next

I want to develop OMG not just as a chain of stores but as a space for teenagers and young people where they can find unusual goods, try new hobbies, play, and spend time.

In the future, I envision large stores with various themed zones. But the immediate task is more practical: to increase the area of one of the existing stores or open a new, larger location.

According to my calculations, one store of 300-500 square meters might be more profitable than several small ones due to rent costs.

I have a detailed business development plan until 2035, and with AI, I've even outlined a strategy up to 2070. At some point, I started thinking about what would happen to the company after I'm gone, and decided to plan its development for decades ahead.

Such a strategy helps in making decisions now. For example, if I'm offered to launch a franchise, but I plan to develop my own products, it will be easier for me to refuse. I understand what I want to achieve and can evaluate new opportunities with this goal in mind.

To implement these plans, I need to build processes that will work not only in three stores but also with further business expansion.
