Hello. My name is Alexander Bogdanov, I am the founder of AGIMA. In 2020, we relaunched the AGIMA website: the work took two years. In 2026, we decided on a redesign again. But this will not be a boring text about how we remade the logo 50 times. Instead, I will tell you how we completely handed over the website management to agents.

Where it all began

The problem with the old site was not in the launch itself, but in its operation. The company was changing faster than the showcase of our products: changes were slow and did not satisfy the business in terms of either quality or deadlines. At the same time, a significant budget was spent annually on support — taking into account the full cost of a cross-functional team: frontend, backend, testing, design, motion design, copywriting, management, and SEO. On the new site, stakeholders participate in development directly: a shorter chain between the task and the result, more automated checks, and transparent rules.

In addition, this time we took on a scope that we wouldn't even have planned before: to update more than 800 materials (a 10-year library) according to the new editorial policy, prepare them for AI system search and answers, re-illustrate them, rework cases and services, integrate analytics, and open up website management to business customers.

Externally, many operations now start with a single message in Mattermost.

Hidden text

Mattermost is a proven open-source messenger for internal communications. Threads and data access allow employees and agents to be united in one workspace: the request remains within the context of the discussion, and the result can be checked and developed there.

Internally, there is a knowledge base, skills, typed tools, version control, SecGuard, and CI with automatic review.

Why we took on such a volume of work

As of October 5, 2026, the content platform had 834 articles and glossary materials, 157 cases, 41 services, 297 events, and 96 employee pages.

With the classic approach, materials would have to be distributed among authors, editors, SEO specialists, and designers, hundreds of tasks would be assigned, priorities would be agreed upon, development would be awaited, and the result would be checked. Each subsequent material would add work for almost all participants in the process.

Agents and automation have changed this algorithm. One request triggers a full production process:

  1. Find the material and determine its type.

  2. Check the source, relevance, and publication rights.

  3. Clean the text from service blocks and old formatting.

  4. Apply the new editorial policy.

  5. Update the structure, metadata, and internal links.

  6. Prepare an illustration according to the new design system rules.

  7. Conduct technical and semantic review.

  8. Save the change with revision control.

  9. Publish or send to a person for review.

That is, the prompt here is an interface to the platform, not its replacement.

One prompt outside, a platform inside

We deliberately did not give the agent direct access to the database and did not allow it to generate an arbitrary interface. The non-deterministic model is surrounded by deterministic components.

We called this architectural wrapper AGIMA SiteOS. It is not a single chat or a page generator, but an operating system for a corporate website: data, design, content loop, agents, analytics, publication rules, and checks work as one product.

The site has a public AI assistant. In the right column, it comments on the page content, provides contextual tips and interactive widgets, and in a dialogue, allows you to delve deeper into the topic and ask clarifying questions. The assistant is connected not only to the content hub but also to the internal ontology and external materials about AGIMA — which is why it can explain more than what fits on one page. The next level is hyper-personalization: recommendations will take into account already viewed pages, actions, and the visitor's traffic source.

Every change undergoes type and content rule checks. The agent works with a file copy in Git: if the original SHA is outdated, the change does not proceed on top of the new one but is returned for rebuilding. Before completing the task, the agent checks the diff and the ready preview.

The public site assistant and the internal editorial agent are separated by permissions. The internal agent prepares changes in Git and runs them through checks. The public agent only receives published context and has no write tools: drafts and production releases are unavailable to it.

Business no longer passes feedback through a human proxy

In the previous model, a service manager or marketer did not manage the site directly. They wrote feedback to the manager, the manager turned it into a task, clarified details, looked for a free editor or developer, and returned with the result after several weeks or months. Then came feedback collection and a new round.

Now, the business customer works with the site in Mattermost. They can request a service description, landing page creation, case development, article writing, or job posting assembly. The agent finds the necessary entity, asks for missing information, applies rules, and returns a link to the finished result.

This does not mean that editors, designers, and developers have disappeared from the process. The role of the person who manually transfers feedback between participants is disappearing. Professionals design rules, components, and checks, and the business uses them directly.

We stopped storing knowledge only in regulations

A regulation is useful as long as a person remembers it and applies it to a specific task. A skill makes professional knowledge executable: it describes input data, workflow, quality criteria, blockers, and output format.

For example, our article review skill evaluates material based on seven criteria: expertise and technical depth, practical benefit, quality and credibility of facts, editorial policy and clarity, alignment with company strategy, original value, and SEO/AEO/GEO.

In addition to scores, the check has blockers. For instance, obvious self-promotion, unconfirmed facts, or uncoordinated client mentions cannot be compensated for by good style.

Thus, the editorial policy ceases to be a PDF file that is opened once a year. It is used in the production of every material.

Design system and illustrations

Before the final decision on the new site's appearance, we went through five iterations: we started with brand strategy and positioning, checked how they read in interfaces, and only then fixed the visual rules.

First, we assembled an extensible design system and coded its elements, and only then did we start working on the site pages. Therefore, the agent does not choose a "similar" style and does not invent the interface anew; instead, it consults the UI kit, uses allowed components, their states, and composition rules.

The same principle applies to illustrations. For covers, it's enough to prompt "create in AGIMA style": the system selects a reference from a prepared set, considers the material's theme, palette, and recent publications to avoid filling the feed with similar images.

The finished file undergoes technical checks for proportions, brightness, and preview readability. Then, a separate multimodal model searches for semantic defects: invented brands, incorrect text language, empty device screens, implausible objects, or inappropriate human faces. The check may request up to two corrective generations. This is how we ensure a reproducible process — agents do not make mistakes from one time to the next but follow a path strictly limited for them by our designers.

Release loop: analytics, experiments, and security

Reducing production costs is not enough. Changes must be measurable, verifiable, and safe. Therefore, PostHog, CI, and SecGuard work as a single release loop.

PostHog closed the loop from release to decision

We integrated the PostHog analytics system into SiteOS. Product Analytics and Web Analytics show what's happening in the funnel, Session Replay helps identify where a user stopped, and Error Tracking links a problem to a specific scenario. Data is collected from the first day of release.

Feature Flags and Experiments operate within the same loop. The agent assembles a variant from design system components and connects predefined events, while the team sets a hypothesis, audience, and success metric. As a result, product launch, its analytics, and experiments form a single production cycle.

Unified control loop for code and content

The agent can change more than just text. A new feature affects components, integrations, and code, so a single editorial review is insufficient.

Site changes undergo tests, typing, and CI. For security review, we developed SecGuard — a pluggable gate on top of Semgrep, Gitleaks, Trivy, Checkov, and OWASP ZAP, supplemented by our own set of rules for AI code. It consolidates the diverse output of scanners into a unified model of findings, applies the project's versioned policy, and returns a clear result to CI: pass or fail.

On a merge request, SecGuard compares findings with a fixed baseline and limits the gate to files modified in that MR. On the main branch, a separate run collects a complete picture of code, secrets, dependencies, and infrastructure configurations. It does not block anything: its task is to keep records, not to stop the release. This also includes SBOMs in CycloneDX format signed with cosign.

After deployment, separate tasks run checks for open ports, TLS versions, and certificate expiration, as well as dynamic analysis of the running application.

LLM triage does not make decisions. It can flag a potential false positive and reduce noise, but the verdict is made by the deterministic engine based on thresholds from secguard.yml.

A few words about vibe coding

Claude Code or another AI harness can quickly assemble the first screen. But it does not inherently create verifiable data, access rights, editorial rules, widget contracts, revision history, analytics, tests, and a stable change process. This is precisely what SiteOS is needed for — a limited, observable, and testable platform. The generative model works within it and provides speed, while SiteOS maintains site stability.

Vibe coding optimizes the initial result well. You can quickly assemble a page, show a prototype, and test an idea. Problems arise later, when the product needs to be developed by several people without breaking previous solutions.

The platform's foundation is a Git repository with a typed set of components. Articles, cases, services, and pages are stored as versioned files, and Astro assembles a static release from them according to the design system rules.

The agent modifies a working copy, selects allowed components, and passes parameters that undergo validation. A new component type still requires a team decision: contract, implementation, and tests.

This is how we use the model where it is strong: understanding the task, choosing from available options, and preparing changes. Predictability is ensured not by prompt instructions, but by Git, types, validators, tests, and Astro assembly.

After checks, the pipeline builds an immutable release candidate linked to the commit SHA and displays it for preview. If the business confirms the result, the same artifact is promoted to production — without reassembly. If a new commit appears during the approval period, the old confirmation will not work.

A working agent platform requires initial investment:

  • gather company context;

  • develop a design concept and design system;

  • define allowed components;

  • describe roles and permissions;

  • formalize specialist skills;

  • build typed tools;

  • set up revisions, audit, and rollback;

  • connect CI and security;

  • define analytics events;

  • create sets of checks and reference examples.

This is more expensive than a single landing page and more complex than a large prompt. However, each new material uses a ready-made foundation, and each new skill increases the capabilities of the entire system.

How website economics are changing

In a project model, costs increase with the number of changes. A new landing page is needed — a new mini-project is launched. Ten experiments are needed — designers, developers, and testers are occupied ten times.

In a platform model, a significant portion of costs is shifted to the beginning: design system, context, tools, security, and quality rules. After that, each repetitive change requires less manual work.

This model is not suitable for everyone. If a company releases one unique landing page per year, classic development may be cheaper. The platform begins to pay off when there is:

  • a large content tail;

  • regular campaigns and special projects;

  • multiple business customers;

  • reusable pages and components;

  • continuous experiments;

  • requirements for audit, permissions, and security;

  • knowledge that is important not to tie to a single employee.

Instead of a conclusion

Six years ago, we realized that a corporate website should be managed as a product: assign an owner, formulate goals, allocate resources, and develop iteratively.

Now, a new layer has been added to this. A modern website should be accessible not only to visitors and editors but also to agents — through structured context, limited tools, measurable quality rules, and a secure publication loop.

Agents have lowered the barrier to accessing the site. Business has gained the ability to manage materials and experiments directly. But quality did not arise from a prompt: it had to be designed, formalized, and integrated into the platform.
