A Few Words About Tools

There are these all-in-one artifacts: Claude Design, Open Design, and Figma Make – oh, there are many of them, too many to count. Now you open any of them, and you have everything you could wish for: presentations, mobile app designs, code base, brand code, and design systems. When I first started tinkering with them, everything was much simpler – a design system, prototypes, and maybe slides. That was it.

The approach is more or less the same everywhere, there are nuances, but the essence is the same. Therefore, I will focus more on approaches than on buttons. I have gathered three design work options – that's what we'll go with.

Option 1. A Service Where Prototypes Are Already Assembled by a Neural Network

To recap from the previous article: our entire service was built into prototypes. Gray, impersonal, but with all the nuances and details inside. And now came the design stage.

On hand – literally two design mockups in Figma. This is the starting point – what we began with. The project is already deployed in Claude, so I just say: "Let's start the next part of the epic, here's the visual direction – let's create design mockups.".

Let me remind you about the two directions in the project: the personal account (internal part of the service, for users) and the public part (the attractive one, for clients).

The personal account rolled out easily. We simply applied the visual brand of the service: gave it the UI kit, colors, fonts, sizes, a couple of behavioral patterns – and that's it. The system was already built during the prototyping stage, so the neural network quickly colored everything. Beautiful!

The public part turned out to be "demanding". We didn't put its visuals into the prototypes. The prototypes are beautifully drawn in Figma by the hands of our wonderful creators, and the design – again, two design mockups for the direction + a UI kit for the poor. And to roll out the design, you first need to extract all prototypes from Figma into the neural network.

I'll repeat the thought from the previous article: a neural network is better at coding what it came up with itself. Give it access to Figma via MCP or API, throw it a link to a frame – "go code it." It transfers. Currently, by the way, it does this much better than three months ago, even from PNG – I'm sure of it: back then, I had to persuade it like a schoolgirl at a prom.

The scheme is the same as with prototypes. First, the limiting conditions: "Here's a 1920 mockup, code it one-to-one, take sizes, indents, fonts, and grid from Figma." First, we upload all prototypes and check that they are coded according to the mockups, and only then do we give the visual direction: "Here are two mockups, they correspond to the prototypes, assemble them and 'apply' this design to the rest of the prototypes. And yes, take the UI kit too.".

We laid the development environment and stack during the design mockup rollout stage: configured the neural network for Vue, loaded the necessary libraries, fonts, and icons – everything as in the original, the designer found it beforehand. Then the neural network started assembling. It turned out quite well: there were edits, but minimal.

And now – the pitfalls, which I want you to look at and not do the same.

We started developing pages based on design complexity. The main service page – the dashboard, visually complex (for a designer). And then it turned out that the neural network simply replaced some images with SVGs (thanks… idiot). But we need a live system – we are laying down coding patterns from the start.

The developer joined and put everything in its place: from a coding perspective, the dashboard is not the most difficult page. The most difficult parts are tables with filters and data fetching, modals, states, and roles. That is, what is complex for design is different from what is complex for development. A beautiful dashboard image can be trivial to code, while a modest table can be a branch of hell on earth.

Conclusion: be friends with development, don't do nasty things, and don't say bad things. Amen! Deciding in advance which pages to do first is the surest path to success. Everything we coded, we immediately uploaded to GitHub – so development could immediately check the code's adequacy and deploy it to our production.

In essence, during the design rollout to prototypes, we were already laying down coding rules and constraints, and giving the development team a ready-made shell – they only had to finish the nuances and connect the backend. We reduced the development stage by sixty percent or more. The remaining forty percent is what's easier to finish manually, small things like moving a button, which would have taken as many tokens as time – a lot.

Option 2. No Prototypes, Only a Concept, or No Goal, Only Obstacles

Second project: no prototypes, no analytics – just "here's a concept, go and scale it.".

The first thing I went for was Claude Design (this happened chronologically BEFORE option 1). It suited us in all respects: it creates pages, it creates prototypes, and you can generate something. It's similar to Figma Make, but Figma Make was slow and didn't work well with local folders (at least back then). It would create a page, export it, and you could drag it as a mockup – but there was no systematic work. And Claude Design then existed as a separate entity from Claude Code.

I tried to develop a few pages – and it still looked more like a prototype than a design. We got some ideas, especially for the personal account, the cart, layout, and mobile adaptation. But to take it as creative work – God forbid!

As a result, we moved away from Claude Design: it spawned separate entities within the service, while Claude Code is more systematic. For all the worriers, my recommendation. Separate pages, its own system, readme, ".md" with change history – that is, context that the neural network always relies on, like a knowledge base within the project. This is much more convenient. And it does more or less the same as Claude Design, so I see no point in switching to a separate service. Unless you are building a landing page or a presentation – then maybe.

Now I can say for sure: Claude Code handles creatives well – if you connect neural network models (GPT, Kling for videos, Nana Banana) to it via API. Then you can generate images right there. Claude Code itself doesn't generate images – if you make a page, it will still look like a prototype.

Codex – more or less the same story, only within GPT. A bit more creative, also a local folder, also GitHub, through which the whole team can connect. Models compete with each other: Fable in Claude Code and Astra in Codex. A new version comes out – everyone, of course, rushes to it like for the latest iPhone, new features appear, but essentially not much changes.

Therefore, it's easier to use a strong model like Sonnet/Opus in Claude Code (or Sol in Codex) – you are understood, they do it well, and they don't consume a cosmic amount of tokens. And if you need super creativity, you can connect a top model on a separate page – but keep in mind: in a couple of sessions, it will eat up everything, possibly your soul a little too. I, for example, used Astra in Codex for a presentation – it handled it perfectly, by the way, my compliments. But presentations, for some reason, are done much worse than landing pages, I don't know why.

Option 3. Nothing: Assembling a Service from Scratch – From Logo to Interface

The third case is our internal product. I had an idea and functionality, I supplemented some of it with my own thinking: a neural network is great at generating ideas for what else can be added, but it doesn't think like a human and behaves like a novice. This is a separate story – I'll tell you about it in the next article.

Imagine the starting point: no concept, nothing at all, a non-working prototype with flawed logic that needs to be rebuilt. The request is "make it beautiful, make it sexy," and on your screen, something Soviet-era from 2004 waves at you. What to do, what to doooo?

I, strangely enough, used this scheme (as I am, so is my approach, with a twist).

Preliminary


I provided the source of what I inherited. The structure was somewhat assembled, the excess removed, but the visuals were not okay, not sexy, at best a C-. I say: "Bro, make it beautiful." No, that DOESN'T WORK!

It Worked From the Logo


Can you imagine?! Well, really, if you just try to make it "beautiful" without any basis, you'll get bland nonsense.


GPT is more creative, and I have it paid for. Basic Go wasn't enough for the service, so I bought Plus – and it's more than enough: plenty of creativity, enough tokens for anything. GPT is generally good for visuals, especially for interfaces, for some reason cosmic ones, better than Nano Banana. One point: it likes to add details, so you have to explicitly tell it "remove the excess, it's too noisy here." But it's convenient to edit pointwise – you can put comments directly on specific parts of the image, and it will refine them. A brilliant thing, I must say.

So, it drew me a logo, I read what meanings it put into it (I had already described the ideology to it), got six options, chose one, and said: "Create a visual brand based on it." It turned out to be similar to something else – "didn't steal, but was inspired." GPT retained the context – the logo, description, everything I fed into it – and it produced super colorful images. Just wow.

Next – the landing page. "Based on the brand, create a service landing page for me, just as vibrant, you know, like you can, but rarely do" – a hook with a button to a video and a free demo. It made it so that everything was whistling and farting. And I didn't have to polish it to the "as if by hand" state – the service is all about generation by neural networks, the main thing for me is that it's visible that it's AI, but done well and creatively. Everything in brand colors, in style and meaning. It suited me more than enough.

Then – the interface. "Now, based on the brand and landing page, assemble a beautiful, vibrant interface for the main service page." With references and constraints: clean, beautiful, so that the creativity from the landing page smoothly transitions into the service interface. Literally, by the second iteration, I got exactly the visual I wanted.

I assembled everything in Claude Code. Started a project, broke it down into sessions: one for the landing page and one for the service. Applied the visualization from ChatGPT – Claude adapted and rolled it out – i-de-al!

Separately about the landing page and animations. GPT and I made mockups for 1920 and 360, I sent them to Claude Code – "code it." It coded it in minutes, and in about fifteen minutes, we added a few animations. Animations are also simple: I found a video I liked on Pinterest, and said: "Break it down frame by frame and generate images in the theme, then assemble it into a video." I have image generation agents connected to Claude Code via API – it provided frames in the right style, and we assembled the animation. The landing page came alive as a fully-fledged child.

The logical question is: why didn't I do everything in Codex, why jump to Claude Code? Simply because Claude Code is more convenient for me. The essence is roughly the same, but I like that ChatGPT generates creatives separately, and Claude Code assembles everything separately – I do it simultaneously, I don't wait and don't burn a ton of tokens. Yes, I pay for two neural networks, and that's okay with me: I need exactly two. You are free to work in whatever way is most convenient for you.
