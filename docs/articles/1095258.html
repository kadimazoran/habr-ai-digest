The Man Who Made the Invisible Visible

Wujec's biography reads like a long test of his own ideas in practice. He started in 1983 as a scriptwriter and lecturer at the McLaughlin Planetarium in Toronto. The planetarium's task is simple and unrewarding: to show the universe that none of the viewers will ever see with their own eyes. Then, for over ten years, Wujec was the creative director at the Royal Ontario Museum. His team's interactive exhibitions were shown at the Louvre and the Smithsonian Institution.

The sticker wall appeared at the museum. In work meetings, everyone had their own picture in mind of what was important, and discussions went in circles. Wujec began posting quick sketches on the wall of what people were talking about. An argument about positions turned into work on a common diagram before their eyes. His entire method would later grow out of this observation.

Then Wujec moved into software development. At Alias, he launched Maya, a 3D animation package that received an Oscar for technical achievement. SketchBook also emerged there: from a professional graphics package costing $40,000, Wujec's team "distilled" a quick sketching program for $200, and then they made it free. According to him, it was used by about 40 million people. A tool that was only available to car manufacturers ended up in a schoolchild's phone.

When Alias became part of Autodesk, Wujec was awarded the title of Fellow – the highest level of expert career in technology companies. He was responsible for long-term strategy and drew it on one huge wall. He spoke at TED six times, and was twice invited as an official artist: for several days in a row, he captured everything the speakers said on the wall. A rare case where a person goes to an ideas conference and the first thing they check is whether there are enough markers.

Stars, museum halls, 3D models, corporate strategy – the material changed, but the task remained the same. The idea needed to be moved from the head to a place where it could be seen, touched, and discussed with a neighbor. Three talks grew out of this task, which later spread to training sessions: about how the brain extracts meaning from images, why the marshmallow falls, and why we draw toast.

How the Brain Creates Meaning

Wujec presented the first of the three talks at TED in 2009, titled '3 ways the brain creates meaning'. The impetus was an experiment in 2008 where artists David Sibbet and Kevin Richards drew throughout the conference: for each presentation, they made a sketch capturing its essence. They accumulated 650 pages, and according to Wujec, it worked.

He asked himself: why do pictures work at all?

His answer begins with an unpleasant fact: the brain does not see the world as it is. It builds a model, and several systems do this simultaneously. Wujec identifies three:

  • Ventral stream, the 'what' detector: recognizes objects and associates them with words.

  • Dorsal stream, the 'where' detector: arranges objects in space and builds a mental map.

  • Limbic system, 'the part that feels': assigns an attitude to what is seen before any reasoning.

The first two pathways were described by neurophysiologists Leslie Ungerleider and Mortimer Mishkin back in 1982, so Wujec has a perfectly academic basis from which he draws a simple conclusion. 

Text loads the ventral stream, while graphical formats activate all systems at once: you can name the elements, see how they are connected, and feel where the dangerous spot is.

Hence, graphics have three cool and useful qualities:

  • Clarity — an image clarifies what we are trying to say.

  • Interactivity — you can work with an image (move, add to, rearrange).

  • Persistence — the picture stays in front of your eyes and serves as external memory.

Wujec took his example from his experience with Autodesk. Executives spent two days drawing a strategic plan on one huge wall, and at the end, the team had a shared model that everyone agreed on.

Drawing in meetings started before Wujec. David Sibbet himself, one of the TED artists, has been doing visual facilitation since 1977. Wujec added an explanation to this practice: why a picture works, through which brain systems, under what conditions. His talk, which garnered millions of views, brought the idea from a narrow circle of facilitators to the masses. Thus, this technique found its way into strategic sessions, process maps, online whiteboards, and presentations with infographics.

Over time, everyone learned to draw Wujec-style diagrams, and it became fashionable. Arrows, boxes, circles with labels appeared in every second presentation, at every strategic session, and in every project report. A diagram became a sign of a serious conversation: if it's drawn, it means someone thought about it.

Only, people didn't always think. Such a diagram retells in pictures what was already said in words. It's drawn to show, not to work with: no one rearranges anything on it, no one returns to it. Of Wujec's three principles, only the appearance of clarity remains, while interactivity and persistence disappear along with the question that made drawing worthwhile.

You can check whether it's a method or a ritual with one question: what changed in the solution after the diagram was drawn? If nothing, you have decoration. Wujec drew to help the team see a discrepancy, a conflict, or a gap and do something about it. A picture that doesn't change anyone's mind has nothing to do with his method.

How Preschoolers Beat Business School Graduates

In 2010, Wujec gave a TED talk titled 'Build a Tower, Build a Team'. He talks about an exercise by Peter Skillman, who led design teams at IDEO, Palm, Nokia, and Microsoft. Wujec conducted this game at about seventy workshops, recorded the results, and compared the groups. 

The average team builds a tower about half a meter high. Business school graduates build them twice as low, and, in Wujec's words, their structures look 'pretty pathetic.' Lawyers and CEOs do slightly better than average. Preschoolers outperform most adults. Architects and engineers do best: the tallest tower Wujec saw was 99 centimeters. It was built by a team that understood triangles and self-reinforcing structures.

Wujec's explanation is worth quoting almost verbatim. Business school students are taught to find the one correct plan and execute it. The marshmallow at the end breaks the plan, and a crisis ensues. Children start with the marshmallow: they immediately put it on top and build one trial version after another, learning each time what holds and what doesn't. And none of them, Wujec adds with evident pleasure, waste time fighting for the position of CEO of 'Spaghetti Inc.'

Two findings from the talk are usually missed.

  • First: if you add the assistants of CEOs to the team, the result significantly improves. The assistant brings process management skills: they keep track of time, distribute work, and notice where the team is stuck. Success, according to Wujec, comes from a combination of special skills and process management skills. 

  • Second: one day he promised design students Autodesk software worth $10,000 for winning — and not a single tower stood. The same students, without the prize, were among the best. High stakes paralyze, and the skill of trial versions is learned quickly.

According to Wujec, in real tasks, the marshmallow can be anything: an unforeseen load, capricious users — in fact, anything at all.

The marshmallow exercise eventually became an almost mandatory part of Agile and Design Thinking training. And its core idea is reflected in the methodology of creating products and services:

  • Minimum Viable Product (MVP) — put the marshmallow on top from the first minute.

  • Pilot Projects — test the main assumption on a small scale before a large launch.

  • Short Iterations — build the tower floor by floor, checking each one.

The role of the assistant is taken by the facilitator and scrum master — people who get bananas are responsible for the process while others are busy with spaghetti.

Solving 'Wicked Problems' with Toast

The third talk, from 2013, is titled 'Have a Wicked Problem? Start by Telling Me How You Make Toast.'

The term 'wicked problems' was coined in the early seventies by Horst Rittel and Melvin Webber, researchers from Berkeley. It refers to tasks without a clear formulation and unambiguous solution. Attempts to solve them change the problem itself, and each participant has their own idea of what it consists of. Reforming urban transport, merging two companies, implementing a new system in a factory where people are used to working the old way. Wujec always clarifies: 'Such a problem cannot be solved. It can only be tamed.'

Taming begins with toast and proceeds in three steps.

Participants are given a blank sheet of paper, a marker, and the task of drawing how they make toast. The drawings turn out very different. Some draw how the bread changes. Others draw the toaster's mechanism, and here Wujec notes that engineers love to draw its mechanics. Still others unfold the supply chain: store, field, wheat — and so on back to the Big Bang. 

Americans draw a toaster, Europeans draw a frying pan, students draw a campfire. But the structure of all drawings is the same: nodes, i.e., objects and people, and the connections between them. A mental model, which previously lived in the head and was invisible to anyone, appears on paper. The optimal complexity, according to Wujec's observations, is from five to thirteen nodes. If there are more, attention ceases to focus, and the diagram only confuses the explanation.

Then the work on cards begins. Nodes are distributed onto sticky notes, and the logic of connections emerges. People rearrange the cards and notice what's missing. Wujec here refers to the observation that the easier it is to change the representation of a task, the more willingly people improve it.

At the end, the group assembles a common model. At first, everything seems chaotic, but then the best nodes come to the fore — and the model becomes clear. A group diagram can easily handle twenty nodes or more: each participant built it and therefore doesn't get lost in it. 

Groups working in complete silence perform better and faster. 'Conversations are distracting,' Wujec says briefly.

Nodes and connections are the language in which business describes almost everything today. Process maps and formal notations like BPMN, customer journey maps, value stream maps, system maps in strategic sessions. Wujec's toast example is the shortest explanation of why they are needed: as long as the model is in the head, everyone is sure they are talking about the same thing as everyone else.

However, in real tasks, usually only the last step is preserved from the exercise: a beautiful final diagram. It's drawn by one analyst — for documentation, directly in notation, and immediately with forty nodes, to the complete shock of the readers. The first two steps are lost, and with them, the meaning is lost: no one saw that one person was thinking about the toaster, another about the wheat, and a third about the frying pan. The group session, meanwhile, proceeds with conversation, and the diagram is ultimately dictated by the loudest participant.

How to Solve 'Wicked Problems' 

In recent years, Wujec has gathered all his ideas into one system, Wicked Problem Solving.

It is based on three principles: starting with a question, making ideas visible, and using simple actions — sticking, drawing, sorting, rearranging, voting. Wujec calls the basic technique a 'play,' like in team sports, and breaks it down into five steps under the acronym SPACE.

  1. Stage — prepare the space and formulate the question.

  2. Populate — unload everything related to it from your head, one thought per sticky note.

  3. Arrange — arrange it in a suitable structure: a timeline, an 'urgent-important' matrix, a cause-and-effect diagram, a system model.

  4. Choose — select what is truly relevant.

  5. Exit — turn the result into a to-do list. 'Complexity is the enemy of execution,' says Wujec.

He separately warns about the trap that people fall into most often. People think they are solving a problem, but in reality, they are justifying a decision made in advance. The first idea that is voiced attracts all the discussion — this is the anchoring effect. 

Wujec admits that he is not a great artist, and according to him, the language of simple diagrams can be learned in one day. This is both the strength of the method and its vulnerability. Sticky notes and arrows spread easily through corporate sessions, but the habit of asking questions, seeking discrepancies, and bringing solutions to a ready list of tasks takes longer.

Engineering Creativity vs. Cargo Cult Around AI

In November 2023, Wujec gave a lecture titled 'Making Ideas Visible' at NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center. He directly proposed applying his tools to AI implementation. Wujec classifies AI as a wicked problem. It's impossible to formulate the task once and for all: the technology changes faster than we can agree on what to do with it, and each participant understands it differently. And Wujec offers his favorite advice: grab a pen and paper.

While working at Autodesk, Wujec talked a lot about generative design. An engineer sets the conditions: loads, fasteners, material, weight — and the computer iterates through shapes and offers dozens of options.

His most striking example is the swingarm of the Lightning electric superbike. This is a motorcycle part that the algorithm created in the shape of a fox's pelvis: in hours, it found what evolution had sought for millions of years. The first version, resembling a bone, turned out to be too expensive, but its adaptation for a standard milling machine went into production three years later. The machine found the shape itself, but people had to refine it and calculate production costs. 

At the same time, Wujec said that a neural network is not always needed for generative design; often, a simple iteration of options is sufficient. It doesn't matter what technologies are used; what's much more important is who sets the conditions for the machine and who chooses from the proposed alternatives.

It's similar with language models, only instead of an aluminum part, it's code, texts, layouts, and calculations. And each of Wujec's three ideas, if not turned into a ritual for getting a banana, will help overcome the challenges we face due to the rapid development of AI. 

Visualize Meanings

We give the model text, and it responds with text — sometimes excellent, spanning several screens. Reading it, we use only the 'what' detector. The structure of the task remains unique in everyone's head, and the limbic system has nothing to feel without visual representation. 

You can activate the other two detectors again using graphics:

  • Ask the model for a diagram along with the text: nodes, connections, forks. Models can easily write diagrams in Mermaid or PlantUML.

  • Transfer the diagram to a place where you can move it with your hands: onto a board, onto sticky notes, onto paper.

  • Models don't support long conversations well, but you can give them a diagram that will act as a meeting room wall.

In the article on IT as a Humanities Science, I already showed that today's problems with AI are more of a communication crisis than a computational one. Wujec offers a simple solution to this problem — a picture that everyone looks at.

Start with the Marshmallow

Spaghetti is everything the model outputs quickly and in any quantity. The temptation arises to build higher immediately and postpone verification. Code written by the model overnight works great on examples and fails on live data. A neat report turns out to be based on a single incorrect figure from a source that no one opened. Before building big, find the most critical assumption and test it with the thinnest version that already touches reality. 

You can recall the assistant from the same talk: we have plenty of special skills and different agents, but they lack the ability to maintain a common goal, stop work that has gone off track, and place the marshmallow in time. This role is still performed by humans.

Draw Toast Before the Prompt

Ask the model to describe how toast is made, and it will draw a regular slice of bread and almost certainly with a toaster. It has no way of knowing that you use a frying pan. The same happens with any more complex task: the model fills in the gaps with the most plausible version of the world, and it coincides with yours only as much as you have described it.

  • Five to thirteen nodes and the connections between them describe the task better than three paragraphs of wishes.

  • Keep the diagram in the form of cards: you don't want to edit a smooth, finished text from the model, but a list of nodes remains dynamic.

  • Let the model be another participant. Everyone draws their own toast, the model offers its own, and people assemble the common wall.

    I discussed a similar idea using the example of an AI character: the structure defined before the prompt acts as a framework that the model cannot help but rely on.

    Cargo cults have already sprung up around AI. The feed is full of headlines like 'Best Prompt for...', 'Ten Prompts That Will Change Your Work,' or 'Add These Punctuation Marks and the Model Will Become Smarter.' Some promise the model tips, others threaten it.

    In 2025, researchers from the Wharton School of Business tested such techniques on five models and complex scientific tests. There was no significant effect. On individual questions, the results varied by thirty points in both directions, and it was impossible to predict where a technique would help and where it would harm. The authors' advice sounds very Wujec-like: give simple and clear instructions. I suggested replacing complex prompting courses with a bedtime story — for the same reason.

    Prompt templates are copied just as stickers and spaghetti towers were copied — by form, without understanding why each step is necessary. Wujec worked with ideas, and his exercises were a way to get to the core of the problem. A prompt template will only work for someone who understands which marshmallow they are testing and whose toast they are drawing.

    When the Wall Meets the Cognitive Prosthesis

    The funniest thing about Wujec's legacy is that AI has done half his work for him. He spent years persuading teams to create trial versions earlier, and now a trial version costs one prompt. He promised to teach the language of diagrams in a day, and today a model will draw a diagram in a couple of minutes. Different solution options used to have to be painstakingly extracted from the team, but now they can be generated in any quantity.

    But along with accessibility came the familiar dread. Wujec's exercises became frameworks, frameworks became rituals, and now working with AI is also turning into rituals. And in their new form, they are even more dangerous. Previously, each meeting participant had their own mental model, and Wujec brought them together on one wall. Now, each person also has their own conversation with the model, and each has drawn their own toast, confidently and smoothly. Mental models within the team diverge more than in the era of sticky notes, and it's harder to notice: everyone thinks they've already discussed everything.

    The most difficult question is asked by Wujec himself. His entire work consisted of bringing ideas out of the head: onto paper, onto a sticky note, onto a wall. It seems that AI maximally implements this technique. But there is a difference between a wall and an LLM. On the wall hangs your thought, made visible: you can touch it, move it, dispute it, and it remains yours. The model outputs a thought that you did not think.

    Wujec works with several models every day and asks himself if they are becoming 'cognitive prostheses.' Is he handing over the most human thing — the ability to imagine and see what is not yet there?

    And here, the ready answer is not from the author himself, but from his method.

    A thought from a chatbot window, which the team has posted on a shared board, rearranged, compared with others, and tested on live data, becomes a collective work, visible to all. A thought that is immediately taken into work from a chatbot window remains unmanifested. 

    Taking and mindlessly working with neural network responses is the same cargo cult, only cognitive. 

    'Make ideas visible, tangible, and meaningful,' Wujec says at the end of the talk about toast. When there are a great many ideas, this advice sounds like a commandment for the sake of preserving one's own thinking. And as you already know, spaghetti can wait while you place that one special marshmallow on top.
