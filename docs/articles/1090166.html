Reproducing grokking and verifying saved models

We reproduced grokking on three seeds: the neural network quickly memorizes training examples but starts generalizing significantly later. We show the results, verification boundaries, and open artifacts of the experiment. Read more

A neural network can perform almost perfectly on the examples it was trained on, yet fail on new ones. This simple distinction – “memorized ≠ generalized” – is a convenient entry point into the phenomenon of grokking: the model first memorizes the training set, and the ability to generalize appears much later.

In Grokking Lab, I wanted not just to get a beautiful learning curve, but to assemble a reproducible experiment: to fix the configuration, seed, milestones, checkpoints, and measured metrics, and then verify the saved states with a repeated forward pass. Therefore, the main principle of the project is evidence first: first the measurement and verifiable artifact, then the interpretation.

Experiment

The task is intentionally simple: (a + b) mod 113. The model receives two numbers and must predict the result of addition modulo 113. This is convenient for research: the correct answer is unambiguous, and the train and validation sets can be separated in such a way as to distinguish memorization of specific pairs from the application of a general rule.

Basic configuration: one Transformer layer, d_model=128, 4 attention heads, d_mlp=512, ReLU, no normalization or dropout; full-batch AdamW, learning rate 0.001, weight decay 1.0; 30% of the table for training and 70% for validation; 40,000 optimizer steps. A frozen package of seed 42 and full archives of seeds 43 and 44 are available in the public repository; direct links are provided at the end of the article.

What happened

Seed 42 reached the memorization threshold at step 200. The generalization candidate was recorded at step 25,600, and the stable plateau at 26,500. The final train and validation accuracy after 40,000 steps are 1.0000. There are over 25,000 training steps between rapid memorization and late generalization.

Additional confirmed runs of the same setup yielded the same qualitative picture, but with different transition times: seed 43 – 302 / 32,300 / 33,200; seed 44 – 416 / 23,000 / 23,900 for memorization / grokking candidate / plateau, respectively. All three runs completed with train accuracy 1.0000 and validation accuracy 1.0000.

The criteria were set in advance: memorization means train accuracy ≥0.98, generalization candidate is the first measured achievement of validation accuracy ≥0.985. A plateau is recorded after 10 consecutive checks at or above the 0.985 threshold. With a 100-step interval, this is 900 steps between the first and tenth checks.

Measurement resolution varies: for the historical seed 42, the memorization step was determined by the logging grid every 100 steps; for seeds 43 and 44, it was checked after each optimizer step. Generalization was checked every 100 steps, so the candidate is the first recorded point above the threshold, not necessarily the exact step of crossing it.

This is important: it is not the exact moment of transition that is reproduced, but the scenario itself: “rapid memorization → long delay → generalization”. The spread between seeds shows that one impressive curve is not enough and that the transition time itself is a subject of research.

A graph can be built from an incorrect file, results from different runs can be mixed, or the link between a checkpoint and its configuration can be lost. Therefore, Grokking Lab saves the original metrics and model states. The seed 42 package contains 401 measured records from step 0 to 40,000, five PyTorch state_dict checkpoints with SHA-256, a manifest, timeseries, event detection, and checkpoint replay. For seeds 43 and 44, 403 records each are saved: the same regular grid and two adjacent points around the memorization threshold.

The reproducibility boundary remains explicit. The historical seed 42 did not save the original source-code hash and a separate split indices file, so byte-identical reproduction of the internal script is not claimed. Seed 44 additionally contains dataset_split.json, environment_lock.json, and source_hash_manifest.json. However, Git dirty-state detection was unavailable for it: the presence of source hashes does not confirm a clean Git state.

However, functional verification of the saved states is performed by a repeated forward pass. For seed 44, the replay particularly clearly shows the phase gap: at step 416, train accuracy = 0.9802, and validation accuracy = 0.0016; at step 23,000 – 1.0000 and 0.9851; at the plateau of 23,900, validation accuracy = 0.9998; in the end, both accuracies are 1.0000. The recorded replay_max_abs_diff = 0.0.

During publication preparation, the full archives of seeds 43 and 44 were checked: ZIP integrity, grid and uniqueness of records, order of timestamps, milestone values, and SHA-256 of all five checkpoints for each run. The metrics from the saved replay reports matched the corresponding lines in the timeseries with a difference of 0.0. The replay itself was performed by the original PyTorch pipeline; this check compared artifacts and did not initiate a new independent forward pass or retraining.

Verifiable Evidence

The approach can be summarized in a short chain: Experiment → measured metrics → checkpoints → manifest/hashes → replay → verify → interpret. An LLM in this pipeline does not create metrics or decide what the result should be; it can only interpret already measured data. This is useful far beyond grokking: the experiment becomes not a story about the result, but a package of verifiable evidence.

What these results do not prove

Three runs do not prove a universal grokking mechanism. From them, one cannot conclude that the effect will occur with any architecture, task, or set of hyperparameters; one cannot directly transfer modular addition to large applied models. In these runs, the seed determines both initialization and data splitting, so their influence on the transition time is not separated. The correct conclusion is: in one fixed setup, delayed generalization was reproduced on seeds 42, 43, and 44, and the transition moment varied noticeably.

What's next

The next logical step is controlled experiments where one factor is changed at a time: weight decay, learning rate, train split, model size, normalization, or architecture. Of interest is not only the final accuracy, but also whether delayed generalization occurs, when the transition begins, and how stable it is between seeds.

Collaboration with labs

I am open to collaboration with research groups interested in grokking, delayed generalization, mechanistic interpretability, and reproducibility of ML experiments. The format of independent reproduction and verification of alternative hypotheses on the same evidence-first pipeline is particularly useful. A good next experiment is not one that confirms our interpretation, but one that can refute it.

Conclusion

Grokking clearly demonstrates a simple fact: a perfect result on training data does not yet mean that the model has mastered the underlying pattern. In our three runs, memorization occurred at steps 200–416, and the generalization candidate only at 23,000–32,300. Therefore, the main question now is not “can grokking be observed?”, but “what controls the transition moment from memorization to generalization?”

The Grokking Lab project contains an executable PyTorch core, frozen research artifacts, provenance and integrity checks, as well as checkpoint replay. The research results should be distinguished from the readiness of the experimental MVP for applied use.
