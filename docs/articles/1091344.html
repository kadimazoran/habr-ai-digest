Prices are falling. On September 22, Anthropic and OpenAI, within about an hour and a half of each other, released models cheaper than their predecessors, and DeepSeek offered cached input for $0.003 per million tokens.

And this is not "generosity." Retail DDR5 has increased by another ~9% in six weeks, and H100 rentals have risen by 15% in a year. Something else has changed – who and how pays for this cost. Part of the cost reduction is engineering: it's the use of cache, KV-cache compression, and the emergence of models that spend fewer tokens on the same task.

Another part is financial, and in September, its scale became visible in numbers for the first time. Let's figure out who lowered prices and on what, which models are now only available by permission, and what's happening with open weights and hardware. Details inside.

Anthropic and OpenAI lowered prices with a 1.5-hour difference

First Claude Opus 5.5, and about an hour and a half later – GPT-6 Sol and GPT-6 Luna. The companies announced the models were more powerful and cheaper than their predecessors. And both pulled the main price lever not where it's usually sought.

Claude Opus 5.5 — minus 20% and thinking that cannot be turned off

In Opus 5.5, a million input tokens cost $4 and $20 per million output tokens. For comparison, in the previous Opus 5 model, you would have paid $5 and $25 for a similar volume. Reading from cache became much cheaper: $0.20 instead of $0.50. Context — 1 million tokens, maximum output — 128K.

Anthropic positions the model as follows: for most tasks, it performs at the level of Claude Fable 5.1 and is 40% cheaper than Opus 5 on typical loads. The difference between a 20% price reduction and a 40% reduction in real work comes from cheap cache and the fact that the model simply uses fewer tokens. Terminal-Bench 4.0 shows: 66.4% at effort xhigh level compared to 57.9% for GPT-6 Astra and 55.8% for Fable 5.1; FrontierCode v1.1 — 54.4%, GDPval-AA v2.1 — 1,846 Elo.

For those planning to migrate from Opus 5 to 5.5, it's more important to check the API changes list. In Opus 5.5, the "thinking" mode cannot be turned off: the disabled value returns a 400 error, and only the effort parameter sets the reasoning depth. Forced tool_choice with values any and tool is also prohibited; only auto and none work. If your agent is built on hard tool calls, migration is not just a matter of replacing the model name.

And one more detail, which we'll return to later: according to Anthropic, Opus 5.5 redirects most cybersecurity tasks to Opus 4.8.

GPT-6 Sol and Luna — minus 50%

OpenAI responded with two models. GPT-6 Sol costs $2 per million input and $10 per million output tokens, GPT-6 Luna — $0.10 and $0.50. OpenAI compares them to GPT-5.6 promo prices — this is a continuation of the August story. Then OpenAI temporarily lowered GPT-5.6 Sol from the base $5 and $30 to $4 and $20. Compared to the base tariffs, the new Sol is 60% cheaper for input and 67% for output.

But the main thing in the release is not the price, but a separate post about caching. OpenAI launched a discount on cached input up to 90%. Caching points, preliminary cache warming, a dashboard, and miss diagnostics have appeared. And on GPT-6, the reasoning effort level can now be changed mid-session without resetting the cache — via a separate configuration_update element in the request. Previously, changing the effort meant the entire context was recalculated from scratch.

A week later, on September 29, OpenAI continued the same trend at DevDay. GPT-6.1 Sol costs the same $2 and $10 million, but cached input halved in price — from $0.20 to $0.10, meaning the discount is already 95%. Writing to the cache is paid: $2.50 per million tokens. OpenAI promises nearly Astra's level in agent coding for a fifth of its price. Among other things for developers, the Agents API with computer use support and the Astra Ultrafast mode are important. Up to 300 tokens per second in Codex, which is eight times faster than usual.

Sonnet 5.5 — the junior model surpassed the senior one

On September 28, Anthropic released Sonnet 5.5 for $2 and $10 million tokens — the same as Sonnet 5 cost. Exactly at the price of GPT-6 Sol. On Terminal-Bench 4.0, it scored 70.6% compared to 66.4% for Opus 5.5, which was released a week earlier.

What's really important here

First — the price per token and the price per task have diverged. For Opus 5.5, the token price is 20% lower than for Opus 5, and the typical task price is 40% lower. Sonnet 5.5 costs the same as its predecessor but uses four times fewer tokens for a response. Calculating the economics of a function by the price per million tokens is becoming as meaningless as comparing cars by the price of a liter of gasoline without looking at fuel consumption.

Second — cache has become the main lever. OpenAI's discount on cached input reaches 90%. For Anthropic, reading from cache in Opus 5.5 has become two and a half times cheaper. For applications with long system prompts and repeated context — agents, RAG, coding assistants — the real price is determined by the cache hit rate.

Third — in one month, announcements include Terminal-Bench 2.1 and 4.0, OSWorld 2.0 and 2.1, GDPval-AA v2 and v2.1, CursorBench 3.2 and 4.0, and model efficiency is measured at different effort levels. Numbers from different announcements cannot be directly compared, even if the benchmark name is the same.

Who pays: Anthropic's prospectus and OpenAI's trillion-dollar round

If tokens are getting cheaper faster than hardware, someone has to pay the difference. This became apparent in September.

Anthropic's Commitments

On September 28, Reuters reported on the contents of Anthropic's IPO prospectus. The document is not publicly available, so we have to rely on the agency's "retelling." The company filed a confidential application back on June 1.

Key Figures

  • Revenue for 2025 — about $4.6 billion, about 12 times more than in 2024.

  • Revenue for the second quarter of 2026, according to preliminary data, is over $11.5 billion.

  • Net loss for 2025 — $42 billion, but about $34 billion of this is a non-cash accrual due to the increased valuation of financing, which can be converted into shares; operating loss — about $8 billion.

  • Cloud and computing commitments for the coming years — $518 billion.

  • Almost a quarter of 2025 revenue came from two clients, 12% each.

This is money the company has already promised to computing providers. Cheap Opus 5.5 and $518 billion in commitments mean capturing the market at a price now and paying with revenue later.

Among the risk factors are not only customer concentration but also models that may "resist shutdown," hide information, or behave "like blackmail." The IPO valuation target is over $2 trillion. The stock market debut itself is expected after the midterm elections in November.

OpenAI: round at a valuation of $1.2–1.5 trillion

On September 15, Bloomberg reported that OpenAI is in early discussions for a funding round at a valuation of over $1.2 trillion. According to the NYT and WSJ, as retold by Forbes, investors are offering $1.2 trillion, and the company is asking for up to $1.5 trillion.

The previous round in March was at a valuation of $852 billion, including the raised funds. Annual revenue, based on the current pace, is over $40 billion. This is double the figure from the end of 2025. At the same time, Altman said in an interview with Fortune on September 12 that an IPO in 2026 is unlikely. And on September 30, Bloomberg clarified that OpenAI wants to raise at least $30 billion at a valuation of around $1.4 trillion, excluding new funds. Negotiations are still in the early stages.

Suppliers and Creditors

Those receiving this money are also under pressure. In a report by S&P Global, the credit quality of hyperscalers is described as "gradually weakening," and financing is becoming more complex and less transparent. Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet, Oracle, SpaceX, and Meta* will spend over $7 trillion on data centers and AI from 2025–2030.

Today's API prices are prices from the market capture period before IPO. Incorporating them into unit economics for three years ahead is roughly equivalent to planning a budget based on a telecom operator's promo tariff.

GPT-6 Astra and permission instead of access

OpenAI's first model with Critical level

On September 3, OpenAI began rolling out GPT-6 Astra. Now the flagship can be obtained for $10 and $50 per million tokens, and from the fourth, it became available for Pro, Enterprise, and API. In Terminal-Bench 4.0, it scores 57.9% compared to 37.3% for GPT-5.6 Sol. The 99.9% figure on ARC-AGI-3 is not entirely accurate, as it was obtained on a special wrapper that preserves the hidden reasoning state between requests. On the standard wrapper, ARC Prize calculated slightly less — 62.7%.

More importantly. Astra is OpenAI's first model to receive a Critical cybersecurity level in the Preparedness Framework High for biology. It is disabled by default in corporate accounts, while the public version refuses to write proof-of-concept exploits. OpenAI plans to expand access for defenders through a separate Daybreak program.

And one more admission from the system card. OpenAI itself writes about a noticeable decrease in the traceability of chain-of-thought reasoning, so production monitoring looks not only at the reasoning chain but also at tool calls and responses. CoT is no longer a reliable window into what the model is doing.

On September 9, Artificial Analysis rated Astra at 53 points — on par with Claude Fable 5.1. But running the index cost $3.26 per task compared to $7.63 for Fable. Astra uses about 27,000 output tokens per task compared to 78,000. Again, the same pattern: the task cost is more important than the token cost.

Open weights are cheaper than ever, but the gap remains

DeepSeek V4.1-Flash: KV-cache in FP4 and input at $0.003

On September 10, DeepSeek released V4.1-Flash under MIT. Architecturally, it's the most interesting release of the month. The base has 552 billion parameters, of which 8 billion are active in input processing and 16 billion in generation. The model is structured as a causal encoder-decoder — a rarity compared to the pure decoders of all others. At the same time, KV and sparse attention indices are reused between layers.

The KV-cache is stored in FP4 and occupies about 890 bytes per token. Compared to V4-Flash, it requires a quarter of the HBM and an eighth of the SSD space. Hence the new pricing. The division into peak and off-peak hours, introduced in August, has been maintained: cached input — $0.003 per million tokens off-peak and $0.006 during peak, uncached input — $0.15 and $0.30 respectively, output — $0.60 and $1.20.

And here it's important not to get confused about the direction. Compared to the August tariffs for V4-Flash, uncached input has become about a third cheaper, and output — less than 10% cheaper. Compared to the pre-August $0.14 for input and $0.28 for output off-peak, it's still 2.1 times more expensive. DeepSeek did not cancel the August price increase — it made the cache cheaper.

On September 11, in Seoul, Samsung's stock fell by 3.5%, and SK hynix by 2.2%. According to Forbes' assessment, investors interpreted the reduction in KV-cache as a reduction in HBM per user. According to DeepSeek's own measurements, in Terminal-Bench 2.1, the model even outperforms Opus 5: 90.6 versus 89.1. In Terminal-Bench 4.0, the picture is reversed: 31.2 versus 51.8.

Xiaomi MiMo-V2.6 — new leader among open-source models

On September 22, Xiaomi released the MiMo-V2.6 family under MIT. The top-tier Pro has 1.02 trillion parameters, 42 billion active, a context of 1M, and accepts text, images, video, and audio as input.

In the Artificial Analysis index, Pro scored 46 points — first place among open-source models and the same as the closed-source Grok 4.7. It costs $0.435 per million input and $0.87 per million output tokens. The smaller Flash, with 309 billion parameters and 15 billion active, costs $0.14 and $0.28 per million. These are exactly the pre-August prices of DeepSeek V4-Pro and V4-Flash. One could say Xiaomi has occupied the price niche that DeepSeek vacated with its price increase.

What's really important here

First — in terms of volume, open-source models have won. According to OpenRouter, in the week of September 14, Chinese models accounted for 57% to 67% of tokens on the platform. In February, it was 6–13%.

Second — the quality gap remains. On September 7, Artificial Analysis updated the index to version 4.3: replaced Terminal-Bench 2.1 with 4.0, and τ³-Banking with AutomationBench. At that point, the gap between the best closed-source and best open-source models was nine points: 53 for Fable 5.1 and GPT-6 Astra versus 44 for GLM-5.3 and Kimi K3.

By the end of the month, MiMo-V2.6-Pro had reduced it to seven points. Where the boundary lies is evident with DeepSeek. In Terminal-Bench 2.1, it leads Opus 5, while on 4.0, it lags by almost half. Long agent tasks still remain the domain of closed-source models.

Vera Rubin goes into production, and memory continues to get more expensive

AI Infra Summit and MLPerf

At the AI Infra Summit in Santa Clara on September 15–17, NVIDIA confirmed that Vera Rubin is "in full production." The company had spoken about mass production back in May. This data is confirmed by the MLPerf Inference v6.1 results, published on September 16. According to NVIDIA, a Vera Rubin NVL72 rack in preliminary tests (submission) is up to 3.7 times faster than a GB300 NVL72 on Qwen3-VL and up to 2.5 times faster on DeepSeek-R1.

Memory and Rentals: growth has slowed but not stopped

It's too early to celebrate; the minimum retail price for a 32GB DDR5-6000 kit according to the Tom's Hardware index has increased from about $392 in mid-August to $429 on September 28 — about 9% in six weeks. The pace is lower than in the summer; on the spot DDR5 market in mid-September, demand has slowed, but there's no reversal.

Long-term signals are tougher. Samsung expects that by 2027, HBM production will take up almost a third of all global DRAM capacity (30% instead of the current 20%). As a result, the volume of ordinary memory production will inevitably decrease.

Against this backdrop, TrendForce has raised its forecast for the average HBM price growth for 2027 to plus 121% year-on-year. And enterprise SSDs in the fourth quarter will become 23–28% more expensive quarter-on-quarter because hyperscalers are increasing orders for QLC drives for KV-cache. The very optimization that made DeepSeek's cache cheaper is shifting pressure from HBM to flash memory.

GPU rentals are also not getting cheaper. According to the GetDeploying index, the median price for H100 from 40 providers is $3.42 per hour, up 15% over the year.

Claude found an enzyme system, but the priority is contested

On September 23, Anthropic announced a result that will show you what cheap tokens turn into if you use a lot of them. After 21 hours of work by about 950 agents, consuming 210 million tokens, one of them noticed a new system in bacteriophages — array-associated reverse transcriptases, or ART. The reverse transcriptase itself from a jumbo phage was described earlier. New is the associated repeat array, similar to a CRISPR array, and an additional protein. Anthropic writes that Claude apparently noticed it first.

Initial laboratory experiments confirmed that the array is expressed into a set of short RNAs. The function of the system is still unknown — and this is the honest part of the story: the agents found a candidate, but humans still have to confirm and explain it.

A few days later, the story gained another side. On September 27, the NYT reported on microbiologist Mario Rodriguez Mestre from the University of Copenhagen. According to him, his group has been studying the ART system for almost four years, has not yet published the results, and regularly used Claude to create code and draft articles. Anthropic replies that it does not train on dialogues and was unaware of published data on ART. Scientists had previously reacted cautiously.

The formulation today is as follows: the agents found a system that is not in the public literature, but the question of who was first remains open. Even if we count all 210 million tokens at the Opus 5.5 output price, it would be about $4,200. The specific model was not named in the announcement; only Claude Science and Claude Code were mentioned, and part of the tokens likely went through the cache.

The law has come into force, and sovereign models are to be made mandatory

On September 1, Federal Law No. 243-FZ "On Support for the Development of Artificial Intelligence Technologies" came into force. It covers large fundamental models with 1 billion parameters or more. The law divides them into "sovereign," meaning fully Russian, and "national." The latter allow for foreign components under open licenses. These norms will take effect from March 1, 2027. The first consequence appeared a week later.

On September 8, "Kommersant" reported on a draft government decree: in public administration, critical information infrastructure, education, healthcare, and a number of other areas, a sovereign model must be used if it is no more than 1.5 times more expensive than a national one. The Ministry of Digital Development confirmed that discussions are ongoing but calls the list "preliminary" and says it is "premature" to discuss it. For the market, this means that a token in regulated industries may have a regulatory markup of up to 50%.

Final Thoughts

The cost of computing continues to rise: memory and GPU rentals are getting more expensive. But the token price has decoupled from the cost, and in the next year, it will be determined by two factors that are only indirectly related to hardware.

The first is engineering, and it's here to stay. Cache, KV-cache compression, sparse attention, and models provide real cost reduction. It's telling that DeepSeek, which doesn't have a trillion-dollar valuation behind it, lowered its rates specifically for "cached input" while keeping output prices higher than pre-August levels.

The second is financial, and it's temporary. The prices of Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Sol are prices from the pre-IPO period, when market capture is more important than margin, and $518 billion in commitments are paid for by future revenue. When public companies have to show shareholders profits, this part of the cost reduction may reverse — just as it reversed for DeepSeek in August.

Therefore, the conclusion for the next year is: calculate the cost of the task, not the token, and design applications for a high cache hit rate. This is the part of the savings that will remain with you, no matter what happens to the price lists.
