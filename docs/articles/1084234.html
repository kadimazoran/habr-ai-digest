Just a few years ago, NVIDIA Tesla V100 was primarily associated with data centers and supercomputers. Today, these decommissioned 16GB V100 SXM2 accelerators are flooding the secondary market, and for local AI enthusiasts, they look surprisingly interesting. This is especially true for the 32GB version of the V100 SXM2.

Why NVIDIA Tesla V100 Specifically

I had a simple idea: why not build a server with two or four used server accelerators instead of one modern and expensive gaming graphics card? The result is 64 to 128 GB of video memory, high bandwidth, and the ability to use NVLink (in pairs). At the same time, V100 modules can now be found at a price incomparable to modern top-tier GPUs. For example, current offers for 16GB V100 SXM2 on marketplaces are around $100–150 per module, although the price heavily depends on the condition and year of manufacture.

An important caveat: V100 is based on the 2017 Volta architecture. Such a graphics card will not be faster than modern RTX cards in all tasks. Gaming performance is roughly equivalent to a Radeon 6800XT or 5060Ti. However, this graphics card lacks several new features, for example, it does not support ray tracing, nor Frame Generation (DLSS 3/4).

But in local LLM inference, the situation is more interesting: here, the amount and bandwidth of memory are of great importance, not just the generation of Tensor cores. Therefore, a modern server based on NVIDIA V100 SXM2 can indeed compete with much more expensive graphics cards in several neural network scenarios, while costing several times less. In some tasks, such a graphics card remains at the level of an RTX 3090 Ti. Of course, much depends on the specific configuration and board version (the same NVIDIA V100 SXM2 with 32 GB of memory will work more efficiently with small models like Qwen3.8–27B, which fit entirely into VRAM). And if you use four 32 GB V100s, you get a configuration with 128 GB of HBM2, which opens up the possibility of running models that simply don't fit on a single consumer graphics card, although the cost in this case starts to become significant.

What is V100 SXM2

NVIDIA Tesla V100 is built on the Volta architecture and is equipped with 5120 CUDA cores and 640 Tensor cores. The SXM2 version has 32 GB of HBM2 with a bandwidth of 900 GB/s. The claimed performance for Tensor operations reaches 125 TFLOPS, and the maximum power consumption is 300 W. NVLink with a bandwidth of up to 300 GB/s is used to connect multiple accelerators. For comparison, the GeForce RTX 3090 has 24 GB of GDDR6X and a memory bandwidth of about 936 GB/s, and the RTX 5070 has 12 GB of GDDR7 and 672 GB/s. The RTX 4070 Ti has 12 GB of GDDR6X. This means the V100 appears outdated in terms of architecture, but its 32 GB of HBM2 remains a very serious resource for local neural networks.

There were two main versions of the V100: PCIe and SXM2. For a home build, it makes more sense to look at the SXM2. When assembling and configuring, pay attention to driver and application installation. For example, in the current PyTorch branch starting from 2.11, pre-built CUDA 12.8/12.9 binaries no longer include support for Volta; for V100, it is recommended to use builds with CUDA 12.6 or compile PyTorch yourself with sm_70 support. This is one of the main drawbacks of the V100 in 2026: the hardware is cheap, but software compatibility already requires attention. Regarding the special mezzanine connector SXM2, the Tesla V100 has two of them: one is required for PCIe communication, and the second is used for connecting to other graphics cards via the bus. When choosing an adapter, pay attention to NVLink connectivity – not all such adapters have the second SXM2 connector soldered/routed to the edge of the board for connecting an NVLink Bridge. This is another important point to consider when selecting a configuration for a future AI server.

My AI Server Configuration

The SXM2 module itself does not fit into a standard PCIe slot. An adapter board is needed. Currently, there are quite a few such boards, available in configurations for one, two, or even four Tesla V100s. Typically, these boards connect to a standard PCIe slot via various risers and PCIe adapters. I should note that such adapters can "cut the bus" – I have encountered options not only for PCIe x16 but also for PCIe x8. Such configurations impose limitations on the motherboards used, so this point should not be overlooked either.

The simplest option, in my opinion, is to use adapters for NVIDIA Tesla V100 SXM2 for direct installation into a PCIe slot, for example, with air cooling. The photo shows exactly this option, with coolers from an RTX 4090. By the way, the photo also shows an installed NVLink bridge connecting the graphics cards. However, the cost of adapters can now be comparable to the price of the V100 itself.

Cooling - The Main Problem of a Home V100

In my opinion, air cooling is an extreme solution. Server V100s do not have a standard gaming cooler. NVIDIA intended these modules to be installed in servers with powerful heat exchangers/radiators and directed airflow. With a TDP of 300 W, one card generates as much heat as a high-performance gaming graphics card. Two V100s mean 600 W just for the GPUs. Four mean 1200 W. I also have a pair of Xeon E5-2680s with a TDP of 120 W each. Therefore, the "small heatsink and fan from a processor" approach is not always suitable. For effective cooling in such cases, massive copper or aluminum radiators with heat pipes and separate fans are used, with mounting dimensions similar to server solutions.

And if air cooling might be justifiable for a test build, then for a working build with two or four cards, the most reasonable cooling option is liquid cooling systems (LCS), especially if the server is intended to run 24/7 near people. The photo shows an example of an NVIDIA Tesla V100 with an LCS water block installed.

The V100 has a flat die surface, and water blocks designed for SXM2 are available. Here is an example of a test build with a typical two-section LCS and LGA 115x-style mounting. This solution is not only quieter than an air cooling "turbine" but also allows dissipating ~300 W of heat during operation.

Server with Two V100 SXM2

For home use, I would start with two accelerators. We get: 2 × 16 GB = 32 GB of VRAM for running local models like Qwen3.8–27B and Gemma4-26b. In the case of the older V100 version, we get 2 × 32 GB = 64 GB of VRAM and theoretically: 2 × 900 = 1800 GB/s of total memory bandwidth when using NVLink. Don't forget about the PC's RAM. And don't forget about power supply issues – the test build, as shown in the photo, consumes about 1 kW.

Qwen3.8 Tests Showed

Local LLMs like Qwen3.8–27B and Gemma4-26b (and similar ones) are of particular interest. For a 32GB V100, a result of about 36.6 tokens/s can be achieved with Q4_K_M. The model requires approximately 24 GB of VRAM and can be fully loaded onto a 32 GB V100. By the way, modern LLM frameworks can distribute models across multiple accelerators. NVLink significantly reduces the cost of data exchange between GPUs compared to standard PCIe. That is, if you don't need to run heavier models and Qwen3.8–27B/Gemma4-26b are sufficient for you, then you can stop at a server with a pair of 16 GB V100s. I use a Qwen3.8 + Ollama + OpenCode setup. Depending on the situation, MCP and the target application are then used in the chain. For example, you can generate 3D models using Blender MCP or create sketches in Arduino CLI.

Instead of a Conclusion

Looking at such a system from the perspective of a user who doesn't do industrial neural network training but runs local LLMs, retouching, and generative tasks for everyday issues, the impression is unusual. Two 32 GB V100s allow you not to worry about whether 12 or 16 GB of memory will be enough. The model can be loaded onto one GPU or distributed between two. And two 16 GB V100s remain the most budget-friendly option for a home AI server. It is worth noting several points that might deter users from such savings:

  • These cards are used, mostly from 2017–2018 (these are the cheaper versions; if they are newer, the cost quickly approaches that of modern graphics cards), so you could easily get a "dead" one and end up with repair costs.

  • These cards require a modified Nvidia driver to work. It's not always possible to set them up without conflicts and "workarounds." The situation is similar on Linux systems, by the way.

  • High power consumption: a system with four V100s can exceed 1.5 kW, and a system with two V100s approaches 1 kW.

  • Consequently, high heat dissipation. This leads to difficulties in installing cooling systems, high operating noise, and certain risks of system overheating.

  • Two 16 GB cards with NVLink are not the same as one 32 GB card. Accordingly, two 32 GB cards with NVLink are not the same as one 64 GB card. Modern neural networks support memory splitting: if the Qwen3.8–27B model does not fully fit into the video memory of your 16 GB V100 graphics card, Ollama will automatically load some layers into the VRAM of one graphics card and some into the VRAM of the second graphics card. The same applies to large models when other layers are loaded into system RAM. However, this affects generation speed. This also needs to be considered.

  • You can play games on these cards, but there's not much point – they are "tailored" specifically for AI: training and generation. And in general, RTX 5070, RTX 4070 Ti, RTX 4090, and RTX 3090 have advantages in various modern computational tasks.

However, the Tesla V100 offers enthusiasts something difficult to obtain cheaply: maximum GPU memory and neural network performance within a limited budget. It is one of the most interesting options on the secondary market. Several Tesla V100s + NVLink allow building a system that, in terms of GPU memory volume and multi-card capabilities, approaches much more expensive solutions. For a home AI station, this is already a quite interesting configuration: you can run models that require more than 24–32 GB of memory without buying a professional accelerator costing several thousand dollars. This is precisely why four V100s can be a significantly more powerful platform for large models, despite their older architecture. It's especially nice that after purchasing the basic platform, you can gradually expand the system: my plans include adding another one after testing a pair of V100s (making it the third 32 GB V100). Perhaps, with a suitable motherboard, it will be possible to move to a build of four such graphics cards.
