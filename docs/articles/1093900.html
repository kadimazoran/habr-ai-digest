People who rarely wrote anything original have suddenly started creating detailed projects, business plans, documentation, presentations, tickets, pull requests, posts, and meeting notes. All of this is generated by LLMs. And these texts are unreadable. AI helps me write better and faster, but I'm up to my ears in reading almost everything written by AI. In this essay, I'd like to express my frustration and talk about what the value of AI is for me when writing texts.

AI Writes Terribly

I observe a pattern: people use AI to create something new, and then, with the help of AI, retrospectively compile what they've created into a design document. Reading such a document becomes torture. The design document ceases to be a proposal on which consensus is built and ideas are gradually refined through slow reasoning, turning into a machine-generated summary full of tedious details without any context or point of view. It's an unreadable, inhuman result. The "authors" of such documents lose patience when they turn out to be uninteresting to people, and this is justified, as what they created is already working[1].

Furthermore, I increasingly see descriptions of pull requests that are clearly written by machines for machines. They are extremely detailed: this was changed to that, this was broken down into parts, this was combined, this was left untouched, tests were added here, and so on. But I'm left with questions: why are we doing this? What's the value? How risky or urgent is this work? What should my contribution be? What should I pay attention to? The same applies to AI-generated tickets or meeting notes. Text, like an ever-increasing portion of our world, is beginning to consist of decontextualized statements.

Once, I received a private message on a delicate topic that was clearly generated by AI to reflect all the nuances of the conversation and not offend me. But in doing so, the message ceased to be personal, turning into something lifeless and disjointed. All the parts were there, but they made no sense as a whole. I wasn't interested in reading the message or replying to it. I would have preferred the author to be themselves and write in their own tone and with their own temperament, even if they couldn't find the right words and even offended me.

In relationships with people, there is always vulnerability and risk. When communicating with AI, neither of these exists, and attempts to eliminate vulnerability and risk destroy the communication process itself. In his essay Resist Summary, which I often reread, Simon Sarris wrote:

I believe that a person should write as much as possible based on their own experience and feelings, giving the reader their characterization of life or events.

Perhaps the laziest and most offensive example of AI use for me was this: someone compiled a summary of my comments on their proposal as a response to me! Thank you! I couldn't have said it better myself[2].

I Don't Have the Context You Have

Recently, Cynthia Dunlop published the results of a survey Report: How developers react to AI-scented blog posts, dedicated to reading AI-written articles. The majority of readers reported that if they suspect an article was written with AI or by AI itself, they stop reading it (78%) and avoid the author in the future (71%). Brian Cantrill wrote about this study in his essay The revolt of the reader: "having discovered LLMs, our brains hit the eject handle mid-sentence in an attempt to save ourselves.".

The most significant finding of the survey was that the vast majority of people (98%) prefer the author's own texts, with all their flaws and peculiarities, over soulless AI rephrasing. Sincere text composition is one of the best ways to establish connections with people. This reminded me of a comment made by Bjarne Stroustrup, the creator of C++, during an expert discussion on book writing that I attended over ten years ago:

If, when reading a book, I hear the author's voice in my head with all their strange accents and speech patterns, I feel something wonderful has happened. If, however, the book feels like dry academic text, then the author has simply written another dry academic text. In this sense, giving talks is very useful. I hear many people when I read them, and that's good.[3]

If you wrote the prompt for the AI yourself, the resulting output is often quite useful. But don't confuse this with effective text composition. When you read the generated output, you already have a large amount of context—you formulated the prompts, set the constraints, and provided the artifacts (documentation, source code, logs, statistics, images, etc.) that led to the creation of the output. You can glance over it and decide what is valuable and relevant, and what is not. But you were part of the process, meaning you were both the query and the response. You provided the machine with a living human point of view. However, when you send the same text to someone else, they have almost no context. They were not part of the process, so it's harder for them to decide what is relevant and what is not. They are forced to read everything in detail and think about every line, peering into the machine's internals in hopes of establishing context. Therefore, it's natural that they stop reading.

AI Can Be Very Useful in Writing

I recently wrote a scientific paper[4]. During the process, I actively used AI, which sped up the work and made it more enjoyable. However, AI didn't write a single line of the paper. I wrote the paper in LaTeX. The context provided to the AI included instructions on journal style and the LaTeX template, all previously published articles in the journal, the source code of the software systems I was writing about, configurations, logs, and production deployment metrics.

After writing a paragraph, I often asked the AI to check if I had stated everything correctly, by cross-referencing the source code or configuration, or by examining production logs or metrics. For example, it checked if I had correctly specified which database columns were indexed, or how rows were sorted in Parquet files. This allowed me to stay in context, often moving on to the next paragraph while the AI checked the previous one. This helped me find important omissions and inaccuracies. Notably, the reverse process, i.e., the AI writing a paragraph based on the context I provided, for example, describing the operation of source code, always turned out to be useless. It was never helpful. The output was always unpleasant to read, and often inaccurate.

Another aspect that helped me stay in context was using AI for formatting citations. It's very tedious to search for details in an article, on a website, or in a book, fill out the BibTeX format, and then cite it in the text. Instead, I would leave instructions in parentheses for the AI to create the citations I needed and continue writing without losing momentum[5].

AI was an incredibly good and sometimes even ruthless spell and grammar checker. It came up with great ways to simplify overly complex or convoluted sentences[6]. It skillfully handled the creation of technical diagrams in TikZ, which also sped up the work and allowed me to avoid spending time learning the syntax. AI even found one very subtle error: in one paragraph, I used an incorrect notation that was missed by all four human reviewers who were specialists in the system[7].

AI was incredibly good at writing only one part: it composed the abstract—the most concise, mechanical, inhuman section of the paper. I included it in the text without any changes. This probably shouldn't be surprising: AI excels at summarization, abstraction, and describing original work.

Can AI Become a Good Text Composer?

It would be a mistake to underestimate the level of writing that AI may eventually achieve. We are still in the early stages of model development, and the models most of us use are trained to be factual, straightforward, and correct, rather than creative. However, Murat Demirbas's excellent essay The Safest Job from AI may be Writing points out that the quality of LLM writing has plateaued because, unlike programming, mathematics, and accounting, text composition is difficult to formulate clearly and verify[8].

Since LLMs lack an active mental model of a specific live reader, they simply optimize for the static probability of the next word from a vast dataset. They cannot empathize with a live reader's feelings because they have no lived human experience. And they completely lack personal stake.

I was interested in two recently discovered projects for improving AI texts. The first is ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English, a standard for writing technical documentation that grew out of the work of an aerospace working group in the 1980s. It consists of two parts: a controlled vocabulary and grammar and style rules. A version of ASD-STE100 has been created for use with AI models[9]. ASD-STE100 is not suitable for all AI-generated texts: it is specifically designed for creating clear, easy-to-follow guides. I will try using it when creating installation instructions, operational, and similar documentation.

The second is Pangram: a model fine-tuned to detect AI-generated texts. Brian Cantrill notes in his essay The revolt of the reader:

Using AI to write is a violation of the social contract between writer and reader: we, the readers, should not be burdened with the task of analyzing a sentence that the author themselves did not labor over.

And I completely agree. Brian has begun experimenting with Pangram to detect AI-generated texts that violate this contract and has mandated that all public texts from his company, Oxide, be run through Pangram and certified as human-written. Tools like these will save us from the laziest and lowest-quality AI texts[10].

When There Are No Words

When we write, the meaning of our words is not always straightforward, objective, and concise. Examples of this include poetry, prose, song lyrics, mythology, humor, and theater. Iain McGilchrist, whom I consider one of the most important authors of our generation, writes in his book The Matter with Things: "The more important the subject, the more we have to struggle to reduce it to language." Ask an LLM for a reliable way to fall in love with someone: in an AI-generated text, detailed information creates the illusion of meaning, but by imitating human knowledge, intelligence, and understanding, AI writes about facts in a very different quality compared to someone who has lived the experience. The whole cannot be broken down into parts without significant loss of meaning.

A direct approach destroys its object. Similarly, there are subjects that can only be conveyed indirectly. To grasp the depth of what is said, or to say something truly new, we are always forced to go beyond the language of everyday life, which means paying careful attention to what is said indirectly.

The statement that texts in business, production, and science should contain ambiguity, not just facts and conclusions, may seem foolish. But operations, incident response, product development, performance management, security vulnerability discovery, personnel certifications, goal and objective declarations, and many other actions are creative tasks that involve ambiguity. Narrative is often as important as facts and conclusions. Furthermore, it's easy to anthropomorphize LLMs as good systems thinkers because they can synthesize a wide range of information. However, quoting Simon Sarris again, "narrative resists compression" and "the opposite of summarization is attention to detail.".

Despite the emphasis on linear thinking, the work of a good systems engineer, like that of a good anthropologist and psychologist, is difficult to articulate in words, let alone in a summary. We don't always observe and record according to a formula. Even when recording facts, some context is lost. The observer is also part of the context. Systems work always involves an element of exploration, readiness for the unknown, developing a relationship with the system itself, and a desire to be surprised. Ordinary language doesn't always have enough suitable words[11].

The nature of summaries is acceleration. But as a result, we avoid building our own complex mental model. I worry that without a complex model in mind, a person will never be able to notice complex interrelationships that would otherwise be lost. And attention to detail will be lost.

Conclusion

I don't want to live in a world where you use AI to turn something important into an unreadable summary, and then I use AI to try and decipher it. I want to hear *you* in all your imperfection. I'm interested in your interpretation of aesthetics, beauty, quality, relationships, time. I want to know how you feel. I need you to cut through the noise and tell me what's truly important. I don't want to read a summary of facts and connections divorced from experience and relationships. As Ian McGilchrist says, our way of being in the world changes the nature of what is embodied in it, and this is the only world we know[12].

If you ask a machine to do something for you, you risk asking for not what you need, but what it can do well. If it's good at creating summaries and you rely on that tool, you might tailor your questions to get better answers. This is akin to teaching the machine what you need, but at the same time, you are teaching yourself to ask questions about the world in a certain way. What questions are you missing in the process?

Most likely, the value of conscious writing will increase in the future. People who write and write to think, to think deeply and carefully, or to create, or to share, or to capture something important without direct expression, will continue to write and create original works. People who have never been writers will use AI to generate a multitude of texts.

As we read ever-increasing volumes of AI-generated text, we may find that AI has become distorted and changed, perhaps because we ourselves have become more superficial. The pursuit of certainty limits our humanity. Sometimes it's more important not to find the words. I don't want to read what you didn't write.
