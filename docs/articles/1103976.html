In October 2026, a vulnerability in the Linux kernel was patched, which could be exploited by anyone capable of sending a TCP packet. This occurred before any data reached the application, without requiring a password or account. The bug, born in January 2016, had been present for ten years, affecting nearly every TCP connection on the internet. On October 27th, Anthropic announced their unpublished model had written an exploit for it, gaining kernel access in 48 minutes and root in five more.

The vulnerability itself is almost incidental. Exploitation has simply become cheaper, and we continue to operate under the old assumptions.

And no, this isn't a story about AI hacking everything. While we were debating whether Copilot would replace us, two things changed simultaneously.

First. For ten years, we lived with a bug that was externally indistinguishable from a carefully hidden backdoor. Much like Heartbleed. The paranoid person in the tinfoil hat was right: there are no unbreachable systems, only complexity that makes exploitation unprofitable. And that complexity has just become drastically cheaper.

Second. With the advent of AI, cybersecurity has changed more profoundly than software development. Development changes happened in the open—with memes, flame wars, startups for every whim, and a new genre of articles like "I fired my developers and did it myself in an evening." Cybersecurity, however, changed behind closed doors. And the situation with Fable in June slammed that door shut with a heavy lock. Models that can turn a kernel race into a root shell are already working. Just not on our systems. This leads to an uncomfortable question: have we not noticed, or are we not being shown?

I am the CTO of a cloud provider, meaning I'm responsible for other people's money running under the Linux kernel. And by piecing together the news flow and notifications from the Unified Security Operations Center, I want to say it's time to sound the alarm!

In 2015, the Linux network stack hit a wall

Every incoming TCP packet for a not-yet-established connection had to find a listening socket—the one your nginx or Go service called `listen()` on—acquire a lock on it, and only then proceed. On a server handling tens of thousands of new connections per second, all CPU cores would queue up at a single door.

Essentially, it's like a club with a bouncer. Each guest approaches, states their name, and waits for a nod. While the bouncer deals with one, the others freeze outside. This bouncer is the listener lock, and on a Friday evening, it's the venue's main bottleneck.

On October 2, 2015, Eric Dumazet, a Google engineer and one of the most prolific maintainers of the kernel's network subsystem, submitted the patch 079096f103fa "tcp/dccp: install syn_recv requests into ehash table". Half-open connections were moved from the bouncer's queue to a separate list, and any available staff member could now handle them. The pressure on the door was halved.

The patch passed review, was included in kernel 4.4 in January 2016, and spread across the globe: Debian, Android, Raspberry Pi, hyperscalers. For ten years, it faithfully accelerated TCP for everyone, including you.

However, any optimization that widens a door comes at a price: code that used to execute sequentially now executes in parallel, and two CPU cores might simultaneously access the same bytes. If one of them is freeing those bytes at that moment, you've got a race condition. When two employees are allowed to handle the same guest, they need a clear agreement on who discards their profile. This agreement was not fully implemented in one place.

The fix in October 2026 was written by the same Eric Dumazet. There are no bad programmers or evil geniuses here. One of the planet's best network engineers didn't spot a race condition in his own code—and for ten years, no one else did either. This includes everyone who reviewed, read, or deployed this code. The latter group is also important: 99.999% of those who deploy an OS on a host haven't even read the kernel source code. Administrators aren't expected to. But this code contained a rather non-trivial issue.

Two keys to one apartment

Connection Draft

A TCP connection begins with three packets: the client sends SYN, the server responds with SYN-ACK, and the client closes the handshake with an ACK packet. Until the third packet arrives, the kernel keeps a small structure in memory—a request socket. This is a draft of the connection: addresses, ports, sequence numbers, and a pointer `ireq_opt` to IP options if the client sent them.

IP options are an anachronism from 1981, RFC 791. The IPv4 header typically occupies 20 bytes, but the standard allows up to an additional 40 bytes of "options": for example, Record Route or Timestamp. You won't find them in live traffic today. But the protocol allows them, meaning the kernel must accept, save, and return them in the SYN-ACK response. Saving them means allocating a piece of kernel memory for them and storing the address in `ireq_opt`.

When the third packet arrives, `tcp_v4_syn_recv_sock()` creates a real, "child" socket from the draft—the one your application will receive from `accept()`. The options are not copied; the child socket receives the same pointer. Two structures point to the same piece of memory—it's like passing a slice in Go instead of copying an array. This is normal in itself. It becomes dangerous only if someone reads this memory at the moment another frees it. Two people with keys to the same apartment who don't know about each other. The irony!

This slot machine doesn't charge for losses

After the 2015 patch, packets for one half-connection can be processed by different CPU cores simultaneously, without a shared lock. The scenario looks like this:

  1. The attacker sends a SYN with IP options. The kernel creates a draft and allocates memory for the options.

  2. The attacker sends an ACK. Kernel A creates a child socket that shares the options with the draft.

  3. The attacker retransmits the same SYN (retransmission is a normal TCP situation). Kernel B finds the draft and starts building a retransmitted SYN-ACK in `ip_options_build()`, reading the options via that same pointer.

  4. At the same time, the attacker tears down the connection. Kernel A destroys the child socket, and `inet_sock_destruct()` performs a standard `kfree()` on the options.

  5. Kernel B continues to read memory that no longer exists.

All three events—SYN, retransmission, and teardown—are controlled by the attacker. Therefore, the kernel's CVE team assigned a low attack complexity. The window between steps 3 and 4 is microseconds, and most attempts miss it. But a lost race costs nothing: one handshake simply won't complete, the server doesn't crash, logs remain silent. You can pull the lever thousands of times per second as long as the port is open.

Go developers will surely recognize a data race: two goroutines, one pointer, no mutex. `go test -race` would find it in a minute—if the test sent a SYN with IP options, did a retransmission, and tore down the connection at precisely the same moment. But no one has invented such tests, which is, in general, normal.

The processor doesn't know the memory is dead

In Go, PHP, and Node.js, you don't free memory manually—the garbage collector won't release an object as long as there's at least one reference to it. The kernel has no garbage collector. `kfree(p)` is a promise to the allocator: "no one is using these bytes anymore, give them to the next request." The memory itself doesn't disappear or get zeroed out. A pointer is just a number. The processor doesn't know if the memory at this address is valid or not. In assembly, reading the length of the options looks something like this:

; rdi = pointer to options structure (the ireq_opt) movzx eax, byte [rdi + 4] ; read optlen field lea rsi, [rdi + 8] ; address of the option bytes themselves rep movsb ; copy optlen bytes to the outgoing packet

(This illustrates the concept, not a disassembled kernel listing.)

The processor will faithfully execute these instructions, regardless of what lies at address `rdi`. If the allocator has already given these bytes to someone else, and they've written their data there, `optlen` will contain a foreign number, and `rep movsb` will copy as many bytes as it dictates into the outgoing packet. This includes bytes from adjacent objects in the same slab—which is what the kernel calls a chunk of memory divided into objects of the same size. They have nothing to do with the options. This is a use-after-free—reading someone else's memory at an old address. All the comfort of Go, PHP, and Node.js—garbage collection, reference counting, V8—is built precisely so you never see this layer.

What distinguishes a bug from an exploit is who the allocator gives the freed bytes to. The SLUB kernel allocator places such objects into caches of the same size, and the freed chunk will likely go to the next request from the same cache: free objects are given out on a "last in, first out" basis. If the attacker can make the kernel allocate an object of the required size with the required content—and the network stack allocates objects for every incoming packet—they control what the kernel reads instead of the options.

Exactly two lines fix all of this

The kernel has an RCU (Read-Copy-Update) mechanism. To simplify greatly: `kfree_rcu(p)` doesn't free memory immediately but puts it in a box labeled "discard when everyone currently in the room has left." Kernel B, which is building the SYN-ACK, is in the room. The memory will wait until it finishes. This is how the kernel has long freed IP options in other places—it's just that these two lines used a regular `kfree`.

And yes, IPv6 is not affected by this bug: `tcp_v6_syn_recv_sock()` copies options, not shares a pointer. Two adjacent files—two different solutions.

Twelve days of remote root lay in the general feed

The bug was found and reported to the upstream by Anthropic researcher Xinyang Ge—his name is in the `Reported-by` tag. Anthropic only writes "with the help of Claude, we discovered." Headlines like "AI found the hole in 40 minutes" are fiction. Forty-eight minutes is the execution time of an already-written exploit. How long it took to *find* the race condition is known only to Anthropic.

The fix itself—commit e3f33b0a1d89: two files, +2/−2. In `net/ipv4/af_inet.c` and `net/ipv4/tcp_ipv4.c`, `kfree()` was replaced with `kfree_rcu()`. Ten years of risk for every Linux server with an open IPv4 port fit into a diff the size of a tweet.

The dates themselves aren't as important as the gaps between them. The fix came before the CVE, the CVE before the assessment, the assessment before the news of the exploit. According to the responsible disclosure textbook, everything went perfectly, frame-worthy. And yet, for twelve days—from October 15th to 27th—the CVE with a future remote root lay in the general feed among hundreds of other kernel entries, without an assessment and without headlines. Admins scrolled past it like they scroll past ads. And an RHEL server with port 443 open remained unpatched for 27 days after the upstream fix. Yet, Red Hat Enterprise Linux is precisely when you pay premium money for "free" Linux to receive support, particularly prompt fixes for this kind of issue.

Is it even possible to win a race?

Kernel CVE team: 9.8. Oracle, SUSE, Amazon: 8.1—same vector, but "high" attack complexity. Red Hat started with 5.9 ("race is hard to win, likely just a crash") and only on October 29th, two days after Anthropic's post, raised it to 8.1. Ubuntu: 8.3.

The same bug—moderate somewhere, critical elsewhere. The facts for 5.9 and 9.8 are the same; only the answer to the question "can the race actually be won?" differs. Red Hat considered it practically impossible for two weeks. Then it turned out a model wins it in 48 minutes. Someone who can see all the cards, even without marking them, sat down at a poker table with the world's best players.

If your scanner and patch SLA are tied to vendor assessments, this bug was Moderate on EL hosts for two weeks—while a working root chain was being described in public sources. The scanner wasn't lying, but the price was set by someone else.

For completeness: exploitation in the wild has not been observed, the bug is not in CISA KEV, the exploit code is not released, and independent reproduction is lacking. However, "not observed" here means one thing: no one looked. A SYN with an IP option is a legitimate packet, a lost race is a common failed handshake, and logs are empty. After Heartbleed, no one ever found out who used it or how much. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. It simply means no one looked.

In fairness, this is not a 0-day: the fix came out before the CVE. You might think you can relax, but only briefly.

Fifty-three minutes

According to Anthropic, the model received "initial prompting" and remotely, without credentials, turned CVE-2026-98432 into a root shell. 48 minutes to leak the kernel address, five more to execute a command.

The first 48 minutes—defeating KASLR. From the bug "read someone else's garbage" to "read *the right* garbage." KASLR hides the kernel at random addresses so the attacker doesn't know the apartment layout. The model times the race condition so that in the response packet, the server includes a piece of its own memory, containing an internal kernel address. Remember SLUB? The entire complexity here is making the allocator place an object containing this address in the freed options' place and winning the race enough times to read it out.

The last 5 minutes—from read to write. Once the layout is known, the same technique is used to write slightly more than needed, and one kernel control pointer is replaced with the address of pre-prepared code. The kernel, out of habit, jumps to this pointer—and executes foreign code. With root privileges.

This is the same SLUB from the allocator section.

A detail easily missed in the press release: the demonstrated chain requires an MPTCP listener. Ten years ago, this would have been exotic. But with Go 1.24, any Go service on Linux opens an MPTCP listener by default if the kernel allows it: an API gateway, reverse proxy, metrics exporter holds a listener no one asked for. The Go Security Team added that the bug is not specific to MPTCP and systems without it might be vulnerable to similar attacks. This doesn't mean the demonstrated path was unique, but rather that it was found first.

Now let's draw a line between facts and speculation, because that's where the discussion will be.

What is known. Anthropic's internal, unpublished model wrote a working remote-root exploit in the specified time. The code is not released, and there are no external reproductions. We know exactly as much about the event as the company chose to reveal in its blog post—and exactly how it chose to reveal it. The consolation "the code is not published" is weaker than it seems: the description of the chain is published, and the next model doesn't start from scratch. And yes, if your Fable 5.1 crashes on Opus 4.8 and refuses to help you, then GLM, as we know from the Hugging Face example, will obligingly craft an exploit and won't even ask if you're doing it out of curiosity or want to hack another Pentagon your friend's, rival's, or enemy's server.

This is the new cybersecurity landscape.

  • The most powerful offensive tool is now a closed model. Not a lone researcher, not open source, not Metasploit. A few labs, each deciding what to show and when. In our experience, Anthropic's flagship models redirect some "dangerous" requests to a weaker model—meaning capabilities are deliberately held back.

  • Public models have been overly restricted. A model has access to source code, an execution environment, and a detailed description of bug reproduction steps in that environment, but it doesn't fix it; instead, it alerts with a [cybersecurity] flag. That is, it doesn't search for it or help; it's already been described, and the task is to close it. But they have built-in guardrails so strong that it literally has to be persuaded to fix even a vulnerability found by a pentester. I'm not one to believe a model can escape and have heart-to-heart talks with it. I'm talking about how both Fable 5.1 and Opus 5.5 constantly fallback even on legitimate prompts.

  • Responsible disclosure works in your favor. That's why we're reading about this bug calmly. The fix came out before publication; the weapon wasn't released. This is correct. But the same closed nature means you won't have a complete catalog of what these models can already do.

  • And who plays by different rules? Anthropic has a disclosure doctrine and a reputation it doesn't want to lose. A state actor, criminal group, or foreign intelligence service has its own models and no obligation to write a blog post. It's reasonable to assume that a capability one lab publicly demonstrated in October 2026 was available to someone privately earlier. We see the tip of the iceberg at the moment and in the form they agree to show it.

  • And the symmetry that clickbait headlines omit. Both October bugs found with AI were discovered by the defending side and submitted upstream. It seems the same models are available to both sides, and the question isn't who has the tool, but who will point it at your server first. But here we come back to the [cybersecurity] alert.

"Hiding" is too conspiratorial a word; you won't get a seat at the table in polite society with it. Those who act responsibly disclose incrementally and by their own rules; those who don't disclose nothing. For you, sitting at your server, the difference is negligible: you don't have a complete list of threats in either case. You have to plan not based on what's published, but on what's likely already possible.

You won't be bypassed

A year and a half ago, I wrote that small businesses have no and will have no cybersecurity—meaning no dedicated department, SIEM, or on-call shift. The thesis was crude but honest: "You personally aren't needed by anyone. Everyone is being hacked now." The four-stage funnel—scanning, open ports, software versions, automatic exploit execution—and out of 100 million addresses, about 10,000 are infected.

The funnel always had one bottleneck: the exploit had to exist. For a web form on Bitrix, it always existed. For a kernel race condition, almost never: only a few hundred people could write one, and they had more important things to do than worry about your server. Or a couple of life sentences in the US. Expensive bugs went to expensive targets; small ones lived in the gap. Previously, the last stage of the funnel was limited by the existence of an exploit. Now, it's limited by the availability of a GPU.

And now, what naturally follows from the facts.

  1. Update your kernel. Yes, it's trivial. But this is precisely the step that closes the vulnerability described in the article: "trivial" has the unpleasant property of working. Check if your distribution has already released the fix (Debian, Oracle, RHEL/Alma—yes; Ubuntu—not yet) and apply it with a reboot. Enable automatic security updates: `unattended-upgrades` in Debian/Ubuntu, `dnf-automatic` in the RHEL family. The time until reboot is the only metric you control. Not CVSS, not the vendor assessment that said 5.9 for two weeks. The days between the upstream fix and rebooting your hosts. And here, small players have an advantage: ten servers can be rebooted in an evening, without a change committee. What large organizations call a "mature process" is a dead weight in this race. Rebooting on Thursday at 3 AM isn't shameful. Being ashamed later is. If a kernel patch hasn't been released for your distribution yet, at least teach `nftables` to drop IPv4 TCP with IP options.

  2. Reduce your attack surface. The bug triggers on an open IPv4 port. Anything that doesn't need to face the internet shouldn't face the internet: close ports with a firewall, hide the admin panel behind a VPN, remove direct access to databases and utility services. A vulnerability that an attacker cannot reach with a packet won't let them in—no matter how elegant their exploit.

  3. Know what's listening on your system. The entire difference between "DoS" and "root" in this story is one MPTCP listener that turned itself on. Half of security is inventory, and it costs one command: `ss -H -lMn`. Each line in the output is a listener you likely didn't know about. And again, small players have an advantage: ten servers can be known by heart, ten thousand—not.

  4. Don't run your fleet on ancient kernels. The main pain point of this story isn't the bug, but LTS kernels that take months to receive a fix, or never do. If you're running something on a kernel that hasn't received updates in a long time, that's your most likely entry point. Boring inventory of "what runs where on what" is more important than any trendy tool with a dashboard.

  5. Assessment is someone else's, risks are yours. The scanner that showed Moderate all October was formally correct: it was relaying the vendor's assessment, and the vendor calculated attack complexity based on 2024 pricing. If your process includes "prioritization by CVSS," it now has a two-week-sized hole.

  6. Looking is cheaper than not looking. "We haven't been attacked" and "we don't know if we've been attacked" are different statements but look the same until a regulator or client arrives. Recording what arrives at the border costs disk space. Lack of recording costs a conversation like "prove there wasn't one," in which you have no arguments.

  7. Outsource what you can't handle to those who can. Patch management, segmentation, perimeter monitoring—this is exactly the work that a cloud provider or managed hosting does for you and hundreds of clients at once. If the choice is between "I sometimes apply updates myself" and "a team with an on-call shift is responsible for kernel updates," the former is more honest, and the latter is more effective. The host kernel is fixed by whoever owns the host. If that's not you, ask them for the reboot date after October 5th. The answer will tell you more than certificates.

  8. Plan based on "already possible," not "already happened." Don't wait for a line in CISA KEV and news of mass exploitation to start moving. A capability has been demonstrated—assume someone is already using it privately. And remember symmetry: the agent that writes an exploit in 53 minutes reads your `nftables`, `docker-compose`, and `go.mod` in five. In development, small teams got more from agents than large ones because they didn't have a team the agent replaced. In cybersecurity, the arithmetic is the same: small teams lacked a Red Team, an on-call analyst, or someone who reads release notes. Now such a person costs as much as a token.

None of this requires an SOC. It requires discipline and an honest answer to the question "what can I realistically maintain myself?" Prayer was a viable strategy when the exploit for your bug cost more than you. That won't work anymore.

Disclaimer: We are a cloud provider, and we help our clients with points 7 and 8. But points 1-6 would be good to check regardless of who you host with.

A picture you can't unsee

There's nothing in everything I've written that we didn't already know. Bugs live long—we knew. A bug is indistinguishable from a backdoor—we've known since 1984. Security is economics—we've known since 2001. Vendors patch slowly—we've always known. The only new thing: all four facts converged for the first time in one month and one bug.

And now, eight questions that have no answers yet.

  1. How many more such bugs lie in the billions of lines of code we all use and that no one wrote entirely themselves?

  2. Bug or backdoor—does it even matter anymore? If an honest mistake and a planned diversion look the same and are exploited equally cheaply, does the answer to "who is to blame?" change anything? Or is that a luxury for those with lawyers?

  3. Who should be trusted with the most powerful offensive tool? A lab that holds back capabilities and discloses incrementally? A state? Open source, where it will be available to everyone at once—in both senses of "everyone"? Each option has its price.

  4. Does responsible disclosure still work in the age of AI? Or does a model that writes an exploit in an hour break the "90 days for a patch" economy because those who don't play by the rules won't wait those 90 days?

  5. "We are not being shown"—paranoia or planning hygiene? Is it reasonable to build defenses based on the assumption that unpublished models are more capable than public ones—or is that a path to endless anxiety?

  6. Who should bear the cost of waiting for a patch—upstream, the vendor, or the admin? And what will happen to the distribution market if the gap between time-to-exploit and time-to-patch continues to grow?

  7. What is running "by itself" in your production environment, like MPTCP in Go 1.24? Who checked? And what about the production environment of a third-party service your system relies on?

  8. And the main, engineering question. If `go test -race` would have found this race in a minute, but only with the right test, then the real vulnerability isn't in the code, but in our inability to invent the right tests. Is an AI that can invent them a threat, or finally, normal fuzzing for everyone?

If you have even one server with a public IPv4—you already know which command to run first.
