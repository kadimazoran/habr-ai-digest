Christopher Duff, 40, of Sydney, is accused of hacking the court system of the state of New South Wales.

The prosecution's version is that he intentionally used ChatGPT to write a program designed to download a large number of documents and gain unauthorized access to confidential information. Police say the case concerns almost 9,000 court documents, and Duff gained access to them back in 2025. At that time, he was first charged and his home was searched, with two laptops seized, and he was released on bail. Now it has started again.

He pleads not guilty. The court wants to use Christopher's correspondence with the chatbot as evidence.

This is the first such case in Australian justice. Below are the details and arguments of the parties.

Prosecution's Side

In early September, prosecutors stated in court that Duff used a generative AI chatbot (ChatGPT) to create Python scripts (parsers) for downloading a large volume of data.

Representatives of the Australian authorities, in turn, clarify that among the data downloaded by Christopher with the bot's help were apprehended violence orders (AVOs), information about minors, and other forms stored on the NSW Online Registry portal (managed by the NSW Department of Communities and Justice (DCJ)).

The prosecution also considers the fact that the accused sought legal advice from ChatGPT after his access to the NSW registry was blocked as additional evidence of intent. The prosecutor's representative notes that Duff “asked the neural network for instructions on what and how to tell the police during interrogation.”

The prosecutor presented their position like this.

“It means a user has used computer coding to obtain data that they weren't authorised to.

In a nutshell, that's what this matter is about.”
