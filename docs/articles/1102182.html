How It All Began

On an August summer day, I was researching the financial market and organizing notes in Obsidian.

At some point, I uploaded two CSV files, then deleted them via Windows Explorer, and discovered that Obsidian had started lagging.

It wasn't the entire application that was lagging. The editor worked perfectly, as did the search. But switching settings sections introduced an unpleasant delay.

Spoiler

The article isn't entirely about regular bug hunting

It's a minor issue, of course. But you know, there are those little things that don't really bother you until you start paying attention to them. And once you do, living with them becomes impossible. Especially for a perfectionist like me. All functions must work! I won't rest until I find a solution, even if it's someone else's program.

I decided to figure it out.

To save my own neural computations, I turned to others – to ChatGPT.

Why should a person search for the cause of a problem independently when there's a system trained on a vast amount of information and capable of explaining almost anything?

There Were Many Culprits. Only No One Was to Blame

First, a corrupted vault. I created a new one. It lagged. Then plugins. I disabled them. It lagged. The theme, configuration, hardware acceleration, program installation.

With each new experiment, the circle of suspected causes seemed to narrow, but Obsidian continued to lag.

I was starting to suspect my own computer. Maybe the problem was with Windows? The video driver? Electron? ChatGPT kept up. It proposed new hypotheses, explained how to test them, and analyzed the results.

And the most interesting part – almost every subsequent hypothesis seemed reasonable.

Indeed, why not check one more setting? It would only take a couple of minutes. And then another. And another. This is how we subtly reached Chrome DevTools. Things got serious here. Instead, I should have asked the AI how many hours we'd been digging and stopped. But not this time.

We opened Performance, started profiling the interface, and discovered that processing some interactions took around 300 milliseconds. Meanwhile, individual stages of Recalculate Style and Layout took about 5–10 milliseconds each.

Wow! Now we didn't just have lag, we had actual measurements.

We discussed event handling, long tasks, and potential sources of delays.

For the first time, I felt like someone doing software performance research, even though a short while ago I just wanted to open the settings.

And here's what we didn't expect.

"Oh, Miracle, the Settings Fly!"

At some point, we decided to check an older version of Obsidian. I downloaded 1.12.7.

We installed it. Opened the settings. And everything worked.

No delays. The interface responded perfectly normally.

Think about it. For several hours, we checked the computer, settings, plugins, configuration, studied the application's performance.

And the practical solution was to install an older version.

On the same computer. With the same Windows.

I felt both joy at the solved problem and some bewilderment about the work done. However, by then, we were so engrossed that stopping would have been strange.

After all, if two versions of the same application work differently, it looks like a performance regression!

We continued the investigation.

And even here, there were adventures. For example, it turned out that the old Obsidian installer didn't always mean running the old version – the application could continue to load an updated package from the user directory.

Obsidian version: 1.13.4
Installer version: 1.12.7

We had to deal with that too.

In the end, we confirmed the comparison: in 1.13.4, processing Settings switches took about 300–500 ms, while in 1.12.7 – 30–60 ms.

After that, I published an official bug report.

Four days later, I received a reply from an Obsidian team member:

I can't reproduce. (Russian - I cannot reproduce)

This means the problem existed on my computer, we measured it, reproduced it, and fixed it by rolling back.

But it didn't manifest for the developers.

We could end the story about the technical investigation here. Especially since we never identified the specific cause of the slowdown in the source code. Yes, we could have dug deeper, but why?

For those interested in details, here is the original bug report on the Obsidian forum. My other projects and research are collected in my GitHub portfolio.

But what interested me most in this story was something else.

Why Did I Need These Six Hours Anyway?

Let's try to go back to the beginning. What was my task?

Not to research Electron. Not to discover a performance regression. Not to write a bug report for Obsidian developers.

I needed the program to work correctly.

That's all.

For this, it turned out to be enough to revert to the previous version.

But somehow, in six hours, I managed to turn an ordinary user inconvenience into a technical investigation.

And the most surprising thing – no one forced me to do it.

ChatGPT didn't make me open DevTools. It didn't demand I investigate every hypothesis. It didn't insist on publishing a bug report. I asked questions. It answered. I tested. It suggested the next step.

And this process turned out to be so accessible that stopping was harder than continuing.

But it used to be quite different.

Suppose I had needed to understand how Electron works without ChatGPT.

First, I would have had to search for documentation. Then figure out how to open developer tools, study the profiler interface, examine the metrics, and try to understand what was happening.

At some point, I probably would have asked myself: do I really need to know all this?

Maybe just reinstall the program? Or try an older version?

But now this natural barrier has disappeared.

Don't know how to open DevTools? Ask.

Don't understand the results? Ask.

Don't know how to test the next hypothesis? Ask.

Instead of several hours of self-study of documentation – a few messages.

And suddenly, it turns out you are capable of taking on a task you wouldn't have even considered before.

AI has made complex actions so accessible that I stopped noticing how complex the task itself became

A Superpower That Can't Stop

We are used to evaluating artificial intelligence by how much time it saves us.

Wrote code in a minute instead of an hour. Processed a document in seconds. Explained a complex concept without needing to read several books.

All of this really works. But there's one effect I hadn't really thought about before.

When you can do in a day what used to take a week, it doesn't guarantee you'll have six free days. You might just take on seven new tasks.

And then fourteen. And each will seem quite manageable.

Something similar happened with my Obsidian. One hypothesis didn't pan out? Fine, let's test the next one.

Discovered a delay? Let's measure it. Got measurements? Interesting, what are they related to.

Found a difference between versions? We need to figure it out. Figured it out? Now we can write to the developers.

Each individual step is perfectly rational.

But if you look at the entire sequence, a question arises: are we even working on the original task anymore?

And here's a curious contradiction.

ChatGPT did help me conduct experiments faster.

But the experiments themselves might not have happened at all if I didn't have ChatGPT.

So, the tool simultaneously reduced the time for individual actions and increased the number of actions I took on.

How much time did I save in the end? Good question. And I don't have an answer.

But Were These Six Hours Wasted?

Here, I don't want to jump to conclusions.

Because in six hours, we really achieved something.

We tested several hypotheses, measured delays, compared versions, discovered peculiarities of the update mechanism, and prepared a public technical report. ( original bug report on the Obsidian forum)

I gained experience working with tools I had rarely used before.

We found convincing evidence of performance regression.

And, what's particularly pleasing, all of this was documented. The results can be shown to others, discussed, and attempted to be reproduced.

Is this useless? No. But then another question arises.

What exactly did I want to get when I started?

A working Obsidian? Or a new investigation for my portfolio?

If the former, we clearly didn't choose the shortest path. If the latter, the six hours look completely different.

The problem is, I didn't set the second task initially at all.

It appeared on its own while ChatGPT and I were sorting out the first one. And this is perhaps the most interesting discovery of the entire investigation.

AI doesn't just help me solve assigned tasks.

It allows me to expand their boundaries so easily that the original goal can subtly get lost.

Explore one more question. Test one more idea. Do one more experiment.

Everything is accessible. Everything is interesting. Everything is potentially useful. But my time hasn't become infinite because of it.

And Now What?

I still consider artificial intelligence one of the most useful tools I've ever had.

Without ChatGPT, I probably wouldn't have undertaken half of the projects and research I'm currently pursuing.

It has truly expanded my capabilities. But before, I had a natural limitation: I didn't always know how to do something.

Now, this barrier has become significantly lower.

And it turns out that along with new capabilities comes new responsibility – choosing what to spend them on.

Perhaps the most useful thing I could have done in early August was not to open Chrome DevTools at all.

But simply ask myself one question:

Do I need Obsidian to work, or do I need to find out why it's not working?
