How We Taught a Neural Network to Detect Deepfakes and Explain Its Verdict

Generative AI is changing lives, but not always for the better. Scammers use audio deepfakes to defraud people. Reliable Speech Deepfake Detection (SDD) is crucial, but current methods lack universality and interpretability. We developed HIR-SDD, combining large audio-language models with human-annotated reasoning. This method explains why audio is deemed real or fake, providing human-understandable cues. Our approach, presented at Interspeech, integrates large audio-language models with step-by-step reasoning derived from human-labeled data. This article details our method, experimental results, and why "I don't hear synthesis artifacts" doesn't guarantee a real voice.

Today, generative AI is changing the lives of even those who don't know what "generative AI" is. And these changes are not always for the better. For example, scammers have already learned to use generative audio models to deceive trusting citizens by imitating the voices of their loved ones and persuading them to part with large sums of money. This means that creating reliable methods for detecting speech deepfakes (Speech Deepfake Detection, SDD) is more than just an academic task.

Unfortunately, existing SDDs are often not universal enough: they adapt poorly to new audio domains and generator types. But, most disturbingly, they lack interpretability – the ability to explain why audio is genuine or fake, providing human-understandable cues.

Addressing this issue, my colleagues from AIRI, MIPT, MTUCI, and HSE and I devised a new approach to detecting speech deepfakes, which combines the capabilities of large audio-language models with step-by-step reasoning derived from a human-annotated dataset. The method is called HIR-SDD and was presented as a poster in Australia at Interspeech in late September (the paper can be found here).

Below, I will explain how our approach works, what the experiments yielded, and why the phrase "I don't hear synthesis artifacts" doesn't necessarily mean the recording is of a real person.

When we talk about a neural network explaining why it considers a voice real or fake through human-audible features, what do we mean? We mean features understandable to humans, such as noting background TV noise or unnatural, choppy speech. Here are two real examples from our paper, translated into English.

Example 1. Real Speech

Human Annotation: "Real recording for a voice assistant. Background noise is audible: TV in the background."

Model Response: "The speech sounds genuine: natural intonation, individual pronunciation characteristics, steady speech tempo, and background noise resembling a TV sound."

In this example, the model correctly identified real speech and mentioned the same background sound as the human annotator.

Example 2. Deepfake

Human Annotation: "Monotonous speech. It's not entirely clear from the context what intonation was intended."

Model Response: "The voice sounds robotic, the speech is unnatural and choppy."

The model correctly identified the fake. Here, the human and the model partially agreed on the selected feature categories: unusual intonation and atypical voice quality. Both examples provide explanations that can be compared to the recording: re-listening to the background, intonation, and speech characteristics.

It is precisely these human-perceptible explanations that we wanted to learn to obtain. For training, we used human annotations: comments on recordings and features identified by listeners. The model learned to produce explanations similar in content to its verdict, describing what sounds natural in the audio and what raises suspicion.

To achieve this, we collected human comments on 41,000 audio recordings and fine-tuned a model that processes sound. When we added explanations, the recognition quality decreased, but the next training stage helped to improve it. However, the quality of the explanations themselves proved to be a more complex issue.

Why Should a Detector Explain Anything at All?

A typical detector receives a recording and outputs a score (usually from zero to one) indicating whether the speech is real or synthesized. This is convenient for automatic verification. But if the system makes a mistake, it's difficult to understand what happened based on a single score. Imagine a real recording being rejected due to an unusual timbre, noise, or speaking style. It's useful for a developer to know which sound properties the model pointed to. Otherwise, one is left to sift through examples and find a common pattern.

With new generators, a different problem arises. The features by which a detector recognized an old synthesizer may disappear. A good result on familiar data then transfers poorly to new recordings. Therefore, our work involves two separate tasks: teaching the system to provide an explanation that a human can correlate with the sound, and verifying whether this training method improves the quality of deepfake detection.

For explanations, we used a Large Audio Language Model (LALM/ALLM): a large language model that accepts not only text but also sound. It can correlate audio with instructions and respond in natural sentences. However, the ability to work with sound doesn't automatically mean the model is good at detecting deepfakes. Original audio-language models / LALMs show very weak results without specialized fine-tuning, both with direct prompts and by adding a few examples to the instruction. Therefore, we had to teach the model both recognition and how to explain its answer through audible features.

We Had to Ask People First

We needed examples of the format "recording, decision, explanation." A simple "real speech" or "deepfake" label is insufficient if we want to teach the model to describe pauses, pronunciation, and intonation.

We collected Russian and English recordings from several open sources and supplemented them with synthesized speech. Annotation participants listened to the recording, selected a class, and explained their decision in their own words. For suspected fakes, they also selected relevant reasons from a list. This list included 14 options: unusual intonation, unnatural pauses, stress errors, overly fast speech, unusual voice quality, and other features.

We were also interested in explanations for real speech. For instance, a person might note natural tempo variations, breathing, or intonation related to the phrase's meaning. The response "sounds normal" was not sufficient; it required a description of what specifically created that impression.

We call an annotation the response of an annotation participant to a single recording: the selected class and its accompanying fields. The text comment is part of this response. After selection, the following remained:

There are more annotations than recordings because one recording was described by several people. This is useful: one listener might notice a pause, another might focus on stress, and a third might describe the voice overall. The most frequently mentioned feature by participants was unusual voice quality. Unusual intonation and monotonous speech patterns were also common.

However, the frequency of mentioning a feature doesn't guarantee its reliability: people can agree on an impression and still be wrong. Therefore, we filtered the annotations. We excluded participants with a correct decision rate below 75%, manually reviewed 30 to 50 annotations per participant for adequacy and variability, further removed "bad" annotators, and deleted examples with incorrectly predicted classes.

On average, the original human comment contained 12 words. For a human, phrases like "monotonous, strange pauses" might suffice. For training a model, we needed a more standardized format. Therefore, we translated the comments into English and processed them with the Qwen-32B language model. The model converted the original annotations into coherent textual explanations. We checked a random sample of 100 recordings to ensure the correspondence with human comments was preserved.

Thus, the training explanations are based on human observations, but their final formulation went through a language model. We also separately saved the categories of reasons. These can be compared between humans and the model regardless of the wording used in the explanation.

How Our HIR-SDD Model Works

In our experiments, we used SALMONN-7B. This is an open-source model for working with speech, sounds, language, and music. The original model is described here. In simplified terms, it works as follows: two audio modules convert the recording into numerical representations, a connecting module prepares them for the language model, and the language model receives these representations along with a text instruction and generates a response.

For our task, we compared two supervised fine-tuning modes. In the first, the model learned to output only the verdict. In the second, it had to generate three parts of the response: a textual explanation, a list of reason categories, and the final class.

The structure can be visualized as follows:

Explanation: what properties of the recording the model considers significant.

Reasons: corresponding categories from the annotation list.

Verdict: real speech or deepfake.

This separation allows for checking the response at multiple levels. The model might correctly choose the class but name different reasons. Or it might describe features of real speech and still declare the recording a fake.

For fine-tuning, we used LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation). This method allows training relatively small added matrices instead of modifying all parameters of the language model. Audio recordings were truncated or padded to ten seconds.

How to Link Explanations to Sound

A language model can write plausibly. When asked about a deepfake, it might produce a familiar set of words about a robotic voice, unnatural pauses, monotonous intonation, and so on. The mere appearance of these words doesn't say anything about the specific recording. To strengthen the link between the explanation and the sound, we used controlled audio modifications: adding noise, masking parts of the recording, and changing volume. This introduced pre-defined changes into the signal that could be used for training.

The next step was reinforcement learning using the GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) method. For one recording, we obtained six response variations and compared them based on a reward. The reward consisted of three parts:

The explanation quality was assessed by Qwen2.5–32B. It checked completeness, task relevance, consistency with the final answer, and text usefulness. The correct class received the highest weight: a well-written explanation should not override an error in the main decision. However, the assessment by another language model remains an approximation.

What We Achieved in Recognition

The main evaluation was conducted on 20,000 recordings from the evaluation set of ASVspoof 5: 15,943 fakes and 4,057 real speech recordings. The training and evaluation sets of this dataset use different generators. Such a test allows us to check transferability to unfamiliar synthesis methods, although it doesn't cover all possible recording conditions.

Due to the unequal number of classes, a simple accuracy score is insufficient. If the model always answers "deepfake," it achieves almost 80% correct answers, even though it cannot recognize real speech at all. Therefore, we also look at balanced accuracy. This metric separately calculates the proportion of correct answers for real recordings and for fakes, then takes the average of these two numbers. A model that always answers "deepfake" would have a balanced accuracy of 50%.
