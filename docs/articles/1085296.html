Next are three examples from simple to complex.

Example 1. A regular price list

The most common case is a photographed, not scanned, price list. The numbers intentionally include a space as a thousands separator, a comma instead of a decimal point for fractional values, and a discount that makes the total sum not equal to the simple sum of the rows.

The model was not confused by any of the three pitfalls. The space in 2,500 and 4,500 remained part of the number, not split into two columns. The comma in 0.5 and 1.5 was preserved. And most importantly, "Total for the full package" remained 9,720, exactly as in the photo, not turning into the sum of five rows, 10,800. The model did not recalculate for us.

An unexpected bonus from Excel itself. In the photo, the discount was in parentheses, "(10%)", and after pasting, Excel automatically converted it to -10%, recognizing the parentheses as a negative number. So, even if the model leaves the parentheses as they are, Excel often corrects the format itself.

Example 2. A report with a two-level header

The second case is more complex in structure, not in numbers. In the header "Sales, thousand ₽", it combines two columns, "Plan" and "Actual", and the "Total" row contains a percentage that is not the average of the other rows because it's calculated from the sum, not as an average.

The numbers are all correct. The percentages for each branch match the photo, and "Total" remained 98.7%, not shifting to the average of the four branches, which would have given 97.5% and looked plausible, but would have been incorrect.

However, the model did not handle the two-level header optimally: instead of a single clear line "Sales, thousand ₽ (Plan)" and "Sales, thousand ₽ (Actual)", it duplicated "Sales, thousand ₽" twice and put "Plan" and "Actual" on a separate second line. The data is not lost, but the first row after pasting consists of two identical headers in a row, and not everyone would be satisfied with such a result.

For such cases, the same model has a backup option, a CSV version of the response, which it offers immediately below the main one. There, the columns are already labeled humanly, "Sales, thousand ₽ (Plan)" and "Sales, thousand ₽ (Actual)" in one line. Copy this text, paste it into Notepad, save the file with a .csv extension, and open it in Excel; the table will be laid out in columns automatically, without the hassle of Text to Columns.

Example 3. A receipt

The third format is not a table in the usual sense, but a receipt with columns aligned to different edges. Here, the discount is shown with a minus sign, not parentheses as in the first example, and the amounts are indicated with kopecks.

The minus sign for the discount was not lost; -58.50 remained a negative number, not text. "TOTAL TO PAY" matched the receipt, 1,111.50. As a minor detail, in the table itself, the value appeared as -58.5 without the second zero; this is the general cell format in Excel, not something the model "broke." If desired, it's enough to set the number format with two decimal places.

What to look for in any photo

It's always worth manually verifying the final and calculated rows. The neural network sometimes doesn't just rewrite what it sees but subtly recalculates it in its own way, and it looks just as confident as the correct answer.

Neural networks usually don't assemble multi-level table headers into a single clean row on the first try. It's cheaper to immediately check the CSV alternative, which many models offer after the main response, or to fix the header manually than to redo the entire table.

Numbers with a space instead of a thousands separator and a comma instead of a decimal point don't always get into Excel as numbers the first time; sometimes, the result needs to be corrected by cell format.

Negative values are formatted differently: with a minus sign, parentheses, or the separate word "discount." After pasting into Excel, you should check that they are indeed counted as negative, not as text.
