Which Companies Were Included in the Study

This year, the authors divided the companies in the sample into Growth Leaders (Pacesetters) and everyone else. They provide a comparison of various results to explore: what do leaders do differently than everyone else? And how much do they spend for such rapid growth?

The sample included financial and operational data from 137 software companies: mainly ICONIQ's portfolio companies, as well as 11 public ones. The names of three companies from the sample are not disclosed.

Then, among these companies, they looked for Growth Leaders (Pacesetters). Two mandatory conditions:

  1. Rapid growth: if a company is in the top 25% of its group for ARR or revenue growth over the last three years, the first condition is met. They compare with businesses of approximately the same size by ARR.

  2. Strong product connection with AI. Either the product is initially built on AI models, or the company has integrated AI into its core products and releases separate AI solutions.

Section 1: The Public Market

Over the past year, the S&P 500 software companies index has fallen by 3%, and this is the worst performance among sectors. Chip manufacturers (Semiconductor) have grown by 47% because AI requires more and more computing power for development.

AI helps businesses create their own applications, so overall, investors have started valuing software lower. But interestingly, the latest reports show that the software sector has begun to recover slightly (despite the overall 3% decline).

In the next chart, ICONIQ shows how multipliers have changed in different niches over several years and correlates the metrics with the release dates of popular AI models.

Infrastructure and Security are holding up better than others: according to the authors, AI adoption is supporting demand for servers, data processing systems, and protection.

What Do Fast-Growing Public Companies Have in Common?

We will consider fast-growing companies as those whose revenue increases by 20% per year or more.

Currently, they are valued at 9.2x annual revenue (vs. 6.9x in Q1). All others growing slower than 20% are valued at only 2.7x:

What companies with growth of 20% or more per year have in common:

1. AI brings them revenue. Customers are increasingly using AI features and are willing to pay for them. For example, DigitalOcean's annual revenue from AI tools reached $234 million, growing by 212% in a year. At ServiceNow, the annual value of AI contracts exceeded $1 billion, and the number of AI agent implementations increased ninefold in nine months. The number of Snowflake customers using AI has grown to over 13,600, up from 4,000 previously.

2. AI helps do more with the same team. At Shopify, AI writes more than half of the code, while the number of developers remains unchanged. Thanks to AI, Snowflake avoided hiring approximately 90 engineers. At Samsara, revenue per employee has increased by more than 30% over three years.

3. Accumulated data is their advantage. AI models are becoming increasingly interchangeable, while accumulated data is difficult to copy. Shopify has been collecting trading data for 20 years. Samsara has 25 trillion records of transport and equipment operation. CrowdStrike analyzes trillions of events in computer systems daily to identify threats.

How is Valuation Determined?

Valuations of software companies are still most strongly linked to the Rule of X — a combination of growth and profitability. Growth in this formula is approximately 2.7 times more important than profitability.

At the same time, growth alone is no longer enough for investors. They are increasingly looking at how much money a company has left after necessary expenses. For the first time since 2024, the correlation between this metric and valuation has strengthened (see the growth of the FCF Margin line).

This is the first sign that investors and companies are paying more attention to cost control and business efficiency.

It is becoming increasingly difficult for companies to rapidly increase revenue, so they are trying to retain more money after expenses. This is evident in the Rule of 40 — a metric where revenue growth rate is added to free cash flow margin.

Currently, about 60% of this metric comes from margin, and 40% from revenue growth.

AI is becoming more widely integrated into daily business operations: the share of US companies paying for AI products has increased from 7.5% in January 2023 to over 50% by July 2026.

The next index shows the average cost of processing one million tokens across more than 20 models.

The more frequently a model is used, the more its price affects the index. Therefore, an increase might indicate that users are increasingly choosing expensive, advanced models, while a decrease suggests a shift to cheaper ones.

According to the authors, companies are learning to save on AI: simple tasks are assigned to cheaper models, while complex ones are handled by more powerful and expensive ones. This helps increase margins.

ICONIQ also compared what 15 software companies from the sample told investors about AI during quarterly calls. The share of mentions on how to monetize AI has tripled over the year: from 5% in Q2 2025 to 15% in Q2 2026.

The more often customers use AI, the more the company pays for request processing — and the less it earns from each dollar of revenue. Therefore, the share of mentions of computing and infrastructure in calls with investors has doubled to 6%. The size of expenses depends on token prices and model selection.

How Are Hiring and Teams Changing?

The composition of teams in Software is also changing: many companies are reducing their staff by about 10-20% this year.

Some companies are redirecting funds freed up from layoffs to hire AI-native specialists. Where employees are being laid off or their roles are changing:

  • Customer support: At Salesforce, about 50% of incoming inquiries are already handled by AI. Some employees were transferred to sales, and support costs decreased by approximately 17%.

  • Quality Assurance (QA): AI helps write and test code, so the tasks of testers are shifting to the core development team.

  • Middle management: At Meta, some managers were offered to perform the work themselves instead of managing others — or to leave.

  • Administrative functions: At Cloudflare, some financial, legal, and auditing tasks are being automated.

Who is becoming more in demand:

  • AI & ML engineers: they create models and ensure their operation.

  • Implementation engineers who work directly with clients and adapt the product to their needs.

  • AI-fluent sales professionals who can explain the product's benefits to clients and sell it.

  • Universal specialists. For example, Coinbase is experimenting with roles where one person combines development, design, and product work.

Management perspective: with AI, a small team can perform work faster that previously required more people.

Section 2: How Growth Works Now

The software market as a whole is facing difficulties, but within it, there are companies growing significantly faster than others — the very Growth Leaders, Pacesetters. In this section, the authors want to understand how they achieve this.

But even among leaders, there is a group that stands out particularly — Outliers. They go from $1M to $100M ARR in just 1-3 years, while other companies need more than five years.

Recall that Leaders or Pacesetters are typically young companies that bet on AI and are in the top 25% fastest-growing by revenue.

Who Are They?

Six out of ten are built on AI from the start (AI-native), while the others have re-engineered their products and added AI features (AI-Driven).

Two-thirds make products for any business (Horizontal), while the rest are for a specific industry (Vertical).

70% charge based on actual usage, without a subscription model.

The journey from $1M to $100M in revenue takes them 2-4 years, and the company itself is, on average, six years old.

Next, ICONIQ presents its Pacesetter Index. This is a summary of the metrics of the fastest-growing AI companies — essentially, a benchmark that other software companies are now advised to aim for.

What Exactly Do Leaders Do Differently?

ICONIQ spoke with several of them to understand. These included Anthropic, Braintrust, ElevenLabs, Glean, Legora, Sierra, and Snowflake.

Here are three common themes:

1) Consumption Pricing

70% of leaders tie a portion of their revenue to how much the customer actually uses the product.

  • Introducing this payment model requires careful planning: gradually transition existing customers to the new model, and offer it immediately to new ones.

  • Do not rely on human forecasts of usage: ML models predict much more accurately. Factor in organic growth in each salesperson's plan in advance to motivate them to exceed it.

  • Do not stop hunting for new clients: even with $500M in revenue, it's too early to focus solely on growth within existing clients; without new client acquisition, there will soon be no one to grow with.

2) Hard-to-Copy Advantages and Specialization (Product Moat and Specialization)

The best model is no longer an advantage: everyone has it now. What matters is how deeply the product is integrated into the client's specific processes.

  • Companies hire forward-deployed engineers who work on the client's side and configure the product to their needs. This way, the company generates revenue faster and sees what to add to the core product based on real data.

  • The speed at which a company releases new product versions and features (product velocity) is its defense against competitors. Moreover, it's a self-reinforcing defense, like compound interest. After each release, the company sees what worked and what didn't, and immediately modifies the product. This happens so quickly that the gap with competitors widens with each iteration.

  • Highly specialized salespeople are becoming the norm. They are now divided by industry or even by use case: each expert has a deep understanding of their specific segment.

3) Large Clients: Land and Expand in Enterprise

Leaders are seriously investing in Enterprise clients: they start with a small contract, then grow within the company, gradually expanding it. And this approach pays off.

  • One company selects 20-30 key clients in each segment and forms dedicated teams for them. These teams act as consultants, working with the client to re-engineer their processes for AI.

  • Approximately 12-15 people are assigned to one segment: a team of engineers and salespeople. The manager does not hand over the client to another department after the deal but stays with them for years.

What Do Enterprise Clients Themselves Want?

  • Flexible pricing, but with a cap. 40% of buyers want to pay for actual product usage, either fully or in addition to a subscription. At the same time, about 60% are willing to do so only under two conditions: expenses do not exceed the agreed limit, and consumption is displayed in real-time.

  • The most important people for buyers on the seller's side are three: an engineer who will show how the product integrates into their systems, a security specialist, and an expert on the product itself. The client wants to be sure that the product will work for them, solve their industry's problem, and not create data risks.

  • Pilots are key. 45% of buyers say that a quick pilot with clearly defined success metrics moves the deal forward the fastest. This is twice as important as testimonials from similar companies. During the pilot, they assess whether the product performs its core function, is secure, and integrates well with their systems.

Where Does New ARR Come From?

Leaders are outpacing others mainly due to new clients: they bring in over 70% of all new revenue. Other software grows differently — mainly by selling more to existing clients.

However, the existing client segment for leaders is also not stagnant: over the year, they started paying 20-46% more.

What is needed for a client to stay and pay more

  1. Short contracts. 65% of large buyers sign for one year or less. Value must be delivered quickly and consistently.

  2. Implementation engineers on the client's side are a plus. 73% of buyers view FDEs positively: with them, the product is deployed faster.

  3. Value must be proven before renewal. 43% say that renewing a contract is easiest when the seller proactively provides data: how much the product was used and what the results were. This is 3.5 times more important than any other factor.

Section 3: Rebuilding Profitability

Profitability for software companies is improving year over year. However, leaders (Pacesetters) with revenue up to $100M are burning cash approximately 5.6 times more actively than other companies of the same size (non-Pacesetters).

Leaders have a median FCF margin of -247%, while other companies of the same size have only -44%.

Standard software has very low maintenance costs: a copy of the program is free, so gross margins for such companies are usually high. AI is different: each client request requires computation, and the more actively clients use the product, the more the company spends on it. Therefore, large leaders retain approximately 5 cents less from each dollar of revenue than other companies.

Operating expenses (OpEx) are decreasing across the market. However, leaders under $100M ARR spend as if they were already large: 284% of revenue, or $2.84 for every dollar earned. For others, it's 124%, or $1.24.

The expense categories for leaders are roughly the same as for other companies. There is a slight skew only for companies with ARR over $100M: they spend slightly more on development and back-office functions.

Three categories are highlighted:

  • Sales and Marketing (S&M);

  • Research and Development (R&D);

  • General and Administrative (G&A): finance, legal, HR, management.

Despite significant spending, leaders with ARR over $100M have a burn multiple below 1x. This means that at scale, each dollar spent brings them more revenue than other companies of the same size.

The burn multiple shows how much money a company burns to grow its annual revenue by $1. For example, 2x means $2 was spent to gain $1 in new revenue. Below 1x means a new dollar of revenue costs less than a dollar. The lower the ratio, the more efficient the growth.

Section 4: AI as the Foundation of Company Operations

And although companies are increasingly diverging in their results, AI is already used universally in operations — in sales, marketing, development, and administrative functions.

In ICONIQ's portfolio companies as of February 2026:

  • Access to AI tools was available to almost all employees: 99% in sales and marketing, 98% in development, 97% in administrative functions.

  • Every day, 86% of developers, 74% of sales and marketing staff, and 70% of administrative staff used AI.

At the same time, AI is starting to pay off: at the top 25% of companies with revenue of $25-100M, each employee is generating more revenue.

This is also helped by the fact that companies have generally become more restrained in hiring. The exception is those whose revenue is growing by more than double annually: they, on the contrary, are accelerating hiring to sustain further growth.

When an employee leaves, they are increasingly replaced by someone at a lower level or not replaced at all, because AI has already increased productivity in all departments. New vacancies are not opened until it's verified whether the current team, working with AI, can handle the workload.

However, new roles are emerging: AI business analyst, AI integration engineer, head of AI in sales. Teams are becoming more compact, and roles are becoming more versatile.

But despite the increase in efficiency, the ratio of employees across departments has hardly changed. It seems the model of how teams are structured has not fundamentally changed yet.

AI in Sales and Marketing

Here are the results of AI implementation that ICONIQ sees in individual companies:

  • the win rate of deals has increased by 15-30%,

  • meetings and potential deals per salesperson have increased 2-3 times,

  • 60-70% of customer inquiries are resolved with the help of AI,

  • 75-100% of marketing campaigns are created using AI.

1) Initial Sales

  • Previously, a new manager would spend weeks searching for customer information across various systems. Now, an AI assistant provides it immediately, allowing the newcomer to start delivering results faster: by selling directly.

  • AI automatically distributes and responds to incoming leads, freeing up salespeople's time for live customer interaction.

2) Post-Sale Customer Management

  • AI monitors how the client uses the product, notices if something goes wrong, and advises the manager on what to do. This allows one manager to handle more clients.

  • AI generates customer reports itself: in one company, about 16,000 presentations are created this way annually; in another, a report is ready in minutes.

3) Department responsible for CRM, data, and sales reporting

  • AI automatically fills in missing data in customer profiles. This saves about 30% of the time spent on information retrieval and approximately 4 hours per week on manual reports.

  • In one company, sales forecasts have become about 20% more accurate; in another, they now take half the time.

The next major step in sales, according to ICONIQ, will be a unified system that consolidates all customer information: all data about them, who is responsible for them at each stage, and what is happening with them. With this system, each salesperson will be able to manage more clients, and clients will never have to repeat the same information to different people.

AI in Development

Results in individual companies:

  • 75% to 100% of all code that goes into the product is written by AI,

  • development takes 50-80% less time,

  • in the same amount of time, teams manage to implement 25-75% more product enhancements than before,

  • new features are released approximately 40% faster.

1) Implementation: the clearer the development process, the more benefit from AI

  • In one company, AI itself creates 90% of the interface based on designer mockups and writes 80% of the tests that verify the product. Describing tasks for developers now takes 40% less time.

  • Developers meet weekly to share prompts and techniques that work. This makes AI a habit for the entire team.

2) Acceleration: faster development means more releases

  • A fintech company releases 4 major updates per quarter instead of one. Each engineer accomplishes more, and each code change is cheaper.

  • Employees without deep technical backgrounds can now handle technical tasks themselves using AI tools. Teams expect to achieve much more with the same staff in 3-5 years.

3) Verification: the bottleneck is no longer writing code, but verifying it

  • In an internal test at one company, 10 AI agents prepared about 180 code changes, and none passed automatic verification.

  • AI checks find more issues, but 20-30% of them are false positives, and resolving them is costly for the team.

If AI is implemented into a poorly organized workflow, it will only make things worse: more money will be spent, and the results will be weaker. Therefore, it is important to establish order: which tasks to assign, what data to provide, and who verifies the work done.

ICONIQ believes that developers need a single system that shows everything: what AI has done, how much it cost, and whether the work has passed human verification. In this scenario, those who have a system that learns over time what works and what doesn't will win.

AI in the Back Office

And again, results from individual companies:

  • cost savings of $2-4M per year,

  • hiring is 40-60% faster,

  • information retrieval and analytics take 45-80% less time,

  • month-end closing and financial reporting take 30-80% less time.

1) Standardizing Routine Tasks

  • Monthly reconciliation of financial indicators, previously done by 5 analysts over 10 days, is now prepared by AI, which flags contentious points for human review.

  • An AI agent in the finance department reviews over 80% of contracts. Specialists handle only the truly complex ones.

2) Tools Built by Department Employees Themselves

  • Case Study: A head of finance with no technical background built a prototype system that processes hundreds of millions of dollars daily.

  • Another Case Study: A finance department employee with no technical skills connected Claude to the company's data repository, and now one junior specialist manages an entire area.

3) A Unified Knowledge Base for the Entire Company

  • One company connected AI search across all internal systems for 1400 employees. Each employee saves hours per week.

  • Another created a bot that answers work-related questions using internal data and shows each user only what they have access to.

ICONIQ believes that back-office managers need a single, controlled system that aggregates company data, a knowledge base, approvals, and shows the cost of each task. It's best to start with routine processes that are frequently repeated. And only with those where it's clear where the data comes from, who is responsible if something goes wrong, and how much has actually been saved.
