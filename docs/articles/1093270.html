Three Ways to Sell AI's Work

At the heart of any payment lie three basic schemes. The first is the familiar subscription: you pay a fixed amount per month and get limits (though companies usually don't disclose the exact token volume). The second is API payment, where money is debited strictly for actually spent tokens: separately for input, output, and cache. The third is payment for workplaces, where a company buys access for specific employees, and token counting remains "behind the scenes." All other features—discounts for slow responses, surcharges for speed, advertising, or payment only for results—are just additions to the three main options.

Most likely, both companies settled on $20 not by chance. It's easier for a buyer to orient themselves around a familiar figure than to compare options every time. And a familiar price tag creates a sense of normalcy. Anything that costs more is automatically perceived as a product exclusively "for professionals." 

Why then the $100 tier? In behavioral economics, this is called the decoy effect: an inconvenient option is needed to make people choose the adjacent one. Calculate it yourself: the Claude Max 5x tariff for $100 gives exactly five times more capabilities than the regular Pro for $20—meaning there's no bulk discount here. However, the maximum tariff, Max 20x, costs $200. If you calculate per unit of use, it comes out to $10. It seems very profitable. OpenAI has the exact same arithmetic: their Pro tariff for $100 gives five times the volume of the Plus tariff, and for $200—twenty times. It seems the middle $100 tier is designed solely to make the most expensive $200 tariff look as attractive and economical as possible to you.

The subscription price remains the same, but the capabilities increase. Anthropic has already increased limits for users twice. First, they doubled the five-hour limits for Claude Code and removed peak hour restrictions—just when the company agreed with SpaceX for additional capacity. Then, on September 22, with the release of the Opus 5.5 model, limits increased again, and subscribers received a one-time coupon to reset limits, which can be used at any time. The weekly limit, however, remained unchanged. It seems the company is expanding access simply as its technical capabilities grow, not because of money.

It turned out differently for OpenAI. A week after the release of GPT-6 Astra, on September 10, the company suspended new subscriptions and upgrades to the $200 Pro plan: according to the product manager, this plan loads the systems more than others. SemiAnalysis, which is cited by the review, writes that Pro 20X has the most generous share of computing power relative to price. The $100 Pro plan, meanwhile, is still being sold. It turns out that the tier with the best price per unit of computation was closed first. Apparently, capacity shortage is more important than anything else. 

Now it becomes clear what this cooldown is about, which we discussed at the very beginning. The free plan works like a funnel with a valve: it's open while you're just testing the neural network out of interest, but it closes as soon as you move on to serious work. Cooldown is a price tag, only set in time. 

OpenAI has another currency option: on February 9, 2026, ads appeared on ChatGPT's Free and Go plans. On the free plan, you can opt out of them, but then the company will reduce the number of messages per day. Almost simultaneously, the Go plan for $8 was launched . So, users are offered a choice: pay with money, attention, or waiting time. Anthropic immediately reacted to this and announced that Claude would remain completely ad-free.

For the company: a subscription protects revenue, limits protect margins, and they are set independently.

For you: compare the price per volume, not the subscription price. And check what your work is limited by—the number of requests or the session length.

What Makes Up the Token Bill

The price per million tokens looks like a single number. In reality, the bill is layered, and each layer answers a specific question: how much did it cost to read the request, write the response, and wait. 

AI Responses Are Always More Expensive Than Requests

Market leaders have the same price difference. Official data confirms this: for the GPT-6 Astra model, a million input tokens cost $10, and output tokens cost $50. For Opus 5.5, it's $4 and $20, and for Kimi K3—$3 and $15. Everywhere the difference is exactly fivefold. For Chinese DeepSeek and Alibaba, this multiplier is slightly more modest—three to four. Those who read my previous article know the technical reason: your request is processed by the neural network entirely and in parallel, but it generates the response strictly one word at a time, and each such step heavily loads the video card's memory. However, the identical price ratio among completely different companies looks more like a tacit market collusion than equal costs in AI creation. But for now, this is just a guess.

Cache That Turns Re-reading into a Discount 

For most companies, re-reading text from cache is ten times cheaper than regular input. For example, with Astra, it's $1 versus $10, and with Kimi K3—$0.30 versus $3. Anthropic offers an even bigger discount: in the Opus 5.5 model, reading from cache costs only $0.20. DeepSeek went the furthest—cache there costs mere pennies, only 2–3% of the standard input cost. Such a huge spread in discounts (from 90% to 98%) indicates that data caching has long ceased to be a technical trifle and has become a powerful weapon in the fight for clients.

However, cache has an expiration date. It's usually stored for only five minutes, so if you take long pauses in a dialogue, you'll have to pay the full price again for re-reading the text. Time turns into money here: due to limits, you pay for too frequent requests, and due to the short cache lifespan—for any work interruptions.

The final effect strongly depends on your working style. For example, in Claude Code, from March to September 2026, the model started reading 324 tokens for each one written, compared to the previous 189. The longer the sessions with AI agents last, the more re-reads are in the total bill. Anthropic understands this well, which is why they directed the cache discount precisely where such long sessions spend the most money.

Token Price and Task Price Are Different 

Anthropic reports that under normal load, the Opus 5.5 model is about 40% cheaper than the previous Opus 5. However, the price per token itself has only decreased by 20%. The rest of the savings come from the fact that the new model simply uses far fewer tokens to perform the same work.

Models should be compared by task cost, not by price list. This also includes a hidden expense item, tokens for the model's enabled reasoning. Kimi K3's reasoning mode is always enabled, meaning we pay for the model's thoughts, which we don't see in the response.

Time and Speed Are Sold Separately

The discount for patience works across the entire market: batch processing in OpenAI and Anthropic's Batch and Flex modes costs exactly half as much as the regular tariff. However, you have to pay extra for speed: the Fast mode doubles the price tag, and the Astra model in Ultrafast mode costs $60 for input and $300 for output—that's six times more than the standard. If OpenAI's tables are to be believed, the batch discount also applies to this top model, meaning the same AI can cost from $5 to $60 per million input tokens. It turns out that users pay primarily for speed, not for the neural network's intelligence level.

Intermediaries Who Hide Tokens from the Buyer 

Platforms often sell credit packages in internal points, causing the real cost of tokens to be lost. In services where you connect your key (BYOK scheme), the intermediary charges only for a convenient interface, and you pay the AI developers directly for the tokens. Prices from intermediaries fluctuate significantly, but the main point is different: in all these schemes, the buyer stops understanding how much a specific neural network action actually costs.

For Russian-speaking users, there is another nuance, which I have already discussed in more detail. Tokenizers are trained mainly in English, so Russian text takes up more tokens, and the price per million tokens is not equal to the price per thousand Russian characters. The gap varies for different models, so it's best to measure the cost of a task in Russian using your own text.

For companies: the bill is assembled from layers, and each regulates its own load. Output is more expensive than input, cache encourages long sessions, speed and batches smooth the queue.

For you: compare the task cost, calculate re-reads in your bill, and if sessions are long, adjust the cache lifetime.

Bundling, or All-Inclusive from AI Corporations

So far, we've only considered tokens, but Microsoft, one of the largest AI sellers for business, doesn't show tokens on its bill at all. The company sells not the volume of neural network work, but fixed workplaces for employees with access to it.

Let's Start with Prices

The most well-known option is the regular Copilot for $30 per month per person, which is an add-on to the main Microsoft 365 license. For small businesses, there's the Copilot Business version for $21. And for ordinary people, the Copilot Pro subscription has been replaced by the new Microsoft 365 Premium plan for $19.99 per month. Additionally, in March, the company announced a massive E7 package for $99 per user per month. It immediately included the E5 license, Copilot itself, the Entra Suite security system, and new AI agents, Agent 365.

What's Included in the E7 Cost

Microsoft claims that the new package is much more cost-effective to purchase. If you were to assemble everything separately, the bundle of E5 license, Copilot, and Agent 365 would cost about $105, and with the Entra Suite security system, the price would increase to $117. This means the discount is from 6% to 15%. Against this backdrop, the $99 price for everything together looks impressive, although most buyers simply don't need all these services at once. For companies that haven't yet implemented Copilot, this package will increase expenses by about $33 per user. But for Microsoft itself, it's an excellent way to accelerate AI adoption and increase average revenue per license.

Why a Seat, Not a Token

Tokens are paid for by the buyer, and the risk is also on them: the more requests, the higher the bill. A seat is paid for by the seller, and the risk shifts to them: an active employee costs them the same as an inactive one. This scheme benefits the seller, as "light" users who rarely access the system essentially pay for the costs of those "heavy" employees who use the neural network to its fullest. This is most often the case with corporate licenses. The new E7 package seems to work on the same principle: the more different functions are bundled into one overall price, the harder it is to understand which ones the buyer actually uses.

In the latest Copilot update, Microsoft added the Claude neural network and new models from OpenAI. The choice for users has become wider, but it will hardly affect prices. Microsoft only sells a nice external shell and access to work data, while it buys the tokens themselves in bulk from various vendors. Therefore, if they lower prices, all the benefits go to Microsoft, not ordinary customers.

Microsoft's entire strategy looks like a clear plan. First, they released AI as a separate add-on for $30. Then, they attracted small businesses with a cheap promotional tariff. And finally, they created a huge package of services where buying AI separately from other programs is simply no longer possible. The company itself has not explained the reasons, but the logic is obvious: first, users were to be accustomed to paid artificial intelligence, and then it was simply embedded into the basic subscription, and the overall cost of the package was increased.

For companies: a bundle turns variable costs into fixed revenue and hides the AI price within the package price.

For you: before buying a package, see how many of its components you will actually use. And compare the price of only the functions you need.

How China's Dumping Started and Ended

A year ago, the main news about AI prices was: Chinese models are an order of magnitude cheaper than Western ones, and this is disrupting the market. In early 2025, DeepSeek-R1 was indeed an order of magnitude cheaper than OpenAI's first reasoning models. By October 2026, the picture is different, and it clearly shows that companies revise AI prices following the load.

DeepSeek: Price Depends on Time

Until August 16, the price was always the same, and then the day was divided into peak and off-peak hours, with the peak price being twice as high. The peak windows (01:00–04:00 and 06:00–10:00 UTC on weekdays) coincide with working hours in Beijing. According to DeepSeek, the reason is that resources are distributed more rationally this way. On September 10, V4.1 Flash was released, and its peak price ($0.30 for input) was lower than the previous peak price of the previous Flash ($0.44). Prices first rose over two months, then partially retreated, and on average, the model is still cheap. However, the single price for all hours is gone.

Alibaba Introduces a Tiered Pricing System

The flagship Qwen3.8-Max model costs $2 per million input tokens and $6 for output. Meanwhile, the lighter Qwen3.8-Flash model, launched on August 26, costs only $0.16 for input and $0.47 for output. The price difference between these tiers is almost 13 times. Additionally, the company uses flexible tariffs depending on the region and time of day. For example, in Beijing, the same Max model is cheaper than in the international market, and for some models, there's even a night discount. Extreme price reductions now only exist at the lowest tiers, not defining the company's overall policy. Moreover, on August 12, Alibaba open-sourced the weights of the Max model, meaning other hosting platforms can now set their own prices for it.

Moonshot: Moving Upwards

Kimi grew on the reputation of being a cheap, open model, but K3's price of $3 and $15 is like the Western frontier. Reasoning in K3 is always enabled and is billed as output. An interesting detail: on the OpenRouter aggregator, K3 is listed at $0.28 for input and $10 for output—meaning input there is ten times cheaper than at Moonshot itself. The entry is not recent, the price should be verified, but the logic is clear: as long as the vendor maintains the price, resellers of open weights get a cheaper version of the same model.

The West, Meanwhile, Is Meeting Halfway

The most affordable line from OpenAI, called GPT-6 Luna, costs $0.10 per million input tokens and $0.50 for output. For comparison, Anthropic's Sonnet 5.5 model costs $2 and $10 respectively. At the same time, Luna's input cost turned out to be even cheaper than DeepSeek Flash's model in both peak hours ($0.30) and regular time ($0.15). Of course, these are neural networks of completely different classes, and directly comparing their price tags is not entirely correct. However, the main fact is evident: by October 2026, the common phrase "Chinese models are an order of magnitude cheaper than American ones" simply stopped being true.

Why did this happen? Officially, companies explain almost nothing. DeepSeek vaguely talks about resource allocation, and Alibaba calls its Flash model the ideal balance of price and quality. Everything else is speculation. Most likely, due to a shortage of capacity, Chinese developers had to reduce daytime load with night discounts. And when AI prices also fell in the West, Chinese vendors had nothing to distinguish themselves with. As a result, they switched to a strategy of tiered pricing, began opening up model source code for ecosystem growth, and started bringing their main products closer to Western price levels.

Only the very bottom tier remains truly cheap now. This includes either very small models like Flash (e.g., Qwen3.8-Flash has only about 6 billion active parameters out of 125 billion total), or third-party platforms that simply resell access to open-source models.

What You Pay for AI

A limit that suddenly ends mid-work seems like an unfortunate accident, but in reality, this is how one of the three "currencies" we pay for artificial intelligence works. The first currency is the familiar money: payment for subscription, for spent tokens, or for a workplace. The second is our attention, of which advertising on ChatGPT's free Free and Go plans is a prime example. The third is our time: this includes cooldowns, surcharges during peak hours, or, conversely, discounts for those willing to wait longer for a response. The final bill for the user always consists of these three elements. And companies constantly change the proportions to ensure servers and computing power are utilized as evenly as possible.

How to choose the right tariff? First, ask yourself: what does your work require?

For the average user ($0–$20), the free tier is sufficient for questions and short tasks. It's worth paying when you regularly hit the limit. For everyday scenarios, the basic $20 fee is quite enough. There's also an ad-supported plan option.

For authors, researchers, developers ($20–$200), the session length is usually the bottleneck. Tiers for $100 and $200 are justified only if the limit hinders work every day. Claude's Max 5x costs exactly the same as five Pro plans, there's no discount in it, and Max 20x is cheaper per unit. When work consists of long sessions with repetitive material, an API with caching might be more cost-effective. Calculations should be based on your own figures.

For companies, paying per seat is convenient because the amount can be predicted. But a package like E7 is only beneficial if a large portion of its components are used. Otherwise, it's insurance that won't be needed.

Detailed break-even calculations between subscription, API, and seat are beyond the scope of this review.

Where is the market heading? One direction is already actively working in customer support. For example, the Intercom Fin service charges $0.99 for each successfully resolved inquiry, and if the AI fails and transfers the conversation to a human, there's no charge. In September, Salesforce acquired this pioneer of a new payment scheme (deal closed on September 10, 2026), but kept the price the same. In the same industry, Salesforce has already changed its own tariffs twice: first, it charged $2 per conversation, then switched to charging $0.10 per specific action, and later introduced internal Flex credits. As you can see, there isn't a single formula for success even for the same vendor today.

Payment for results currently works where the result is easily measurable, such as a closed ticket. Where the outcome depends on taste (text, illustration, analysis), no one has yet devised a results-based payment model.

AI prices are not standing still. The most advanced tariffs are becoming more expensive, while mid-range ones are getting cheaper. Server capacities are growing, and with them, user limits are also increasing. The Chinese, meanwhile, are moving from simple price reductions to flexible tiered pricing and schedules. Payment for a specific result appears only where it is easiest to measure. The numbers change rapidly, but the main questions remain: what exactly are the model's resources being used for, and which "currency" is most advantageous for you to pay with right now.

And in what proportion do you find it most convenient to pay for AI work yourself: with money, attention, or waiting, and has it changed over time?
