How I Wasted a Budget on Three Beautiful Hypotheses

I wanted to approach medium-sized companies with wholesale sales and become their partner for several tasks. The bet was on three hypotheses: clients are not tied to anyone, their needs are not being met comprehensively, and upon seeing a complex solution, they would switch. Research, market assessment, context corpus – all according to the rules. The hypotheses failed: resources of several teams were spent, along with a promotion budget and several months of collective effort.

Then I fed the system the same brief and context, one to one. Six steps, zero rubles for traffic, and five flaws:

  • the first hypothesis relies on a signal that equally explains both "not tied to anyone" and "tied, just not to us";

  • the second contradicts itself: competitors have these products, just not with the same UX;

  • the third conflates "leave for good" and "add a second service," and the landing page application doesn't distinguish between them;

  • the hypotheses rely on each other, but were tested as independent;

  • and the fifth, which I didn't even ask about: the landing page in principle couldn't answer our question about how much promotion to pour into it. The system suggested starting with a couple of short interviews.

For clarity – there was no test result data in the input, only what was on my desk before the launch.

Meet the Hypothesis Playground

It's an AI agent system that helps to:

  • filter before launch: each idea goes through the context of the product, market, and past tests, and only those that are supported by real events remain;

  • bring to testing in one run: weak ideas are filtered out on paper, strong ones are enhanced for experiments, and the human only needs to approve the task.

Here's How It Works:

  1. Put everything you have about the product, clients, and market into the project folder: interviews, data dumps, segment descriptions. If there's nothing, the system will go to open sources.

  2. Write the task as is: "why are clients leaving," "find niches for the goal," "test my hypotheses."

  3. The system collects the technical specification (TS) and shows: how it understood the task, what it read, what's missing. You edit and approve – sometimes in several rounds.

  4. Then it works on its own and delivers artifacts for each step and a final report in the form you agreed upon in the TS.

Under the Hood: The Project Team

The system is structured like a project team: each role has its place, rules, and access.

Dispatcher – Business Analyst: clarifies what is requested and writes the TS. You, as the client, approve it.

Orchestrator – Manager: after each step, reads the result and decides who to call next.

Executors. The first is always a consultant – a critic who identifies weaknesses and the main risk. Then, depending on the situation: hypothesis generator, validator, test planner. If there's a gap in the data, the orchestrator brings in six researchers: first, they look into the project context, then they go online to find refutations.

After a live test, the results return to the context, and the next task starts with what is already known.

A dry remark: each role is a skill, i.e., an instruction in SKILL.md. The orchestrator launches it as a separate agent in its context, which is why I call the roles agents; if the environment cannot launch multiple agents, one plays the roles sequentially. The methodology – frameworks and protocols like "how to go online" – resides in one place, agents open it as a reference, and changes reach everyone at once. The rules are short: an agent writes only its own file, doesn't touch others', doesn't edit instructions. In engineering terms, all this scaffolding is called a harness.

First Step – Dispatcher

Everything the system does next, it does based on how it understood the task. Therefore, an error in the TS is more costly than all others, and this is where a human is needed: we work with the TS until it becomes clear.

TS (draft, not approved) 
The TS answers three questions:
  • How the task was understood. REQUEST stores your words verbatim, UNDERSTOOD AS – how the system understood them. Make sure this line retains your thought, not a hallucination. "Channel penetration" can be understood as distribution or acquisition channels, so the dispatcher put this in ASSUMPTIONS. An assumption you haven't corrected works as a fact going forward.
  • What is known. AS-IS, CONTEXT, and COMPLETENESS show what the system has read and what it lacks. A completeness of 1/5 means: only the goal is known, there's nothing to build upon. Here, the client usually provides materials, and the TS is reassembled. RELATIONSHIP indicates if you've solved this before: what's already filtered out won't be tested again.
  • What we are solving. TO-BE and GAP are the main parts. GAP is a list of gaps, and the system closes each with a separate step. "It's unknown who makes purchasing decisions in SMB: the owner, accountant, or IT specialist" – a good point: it's immediately clear who to call. "Audience is unclear" – a bad one. TIER sets the budget for steps so the system doesn't go into a loop, and OUTPUT TYPE indicates where the route will end.
The approved TS is placed in the task folder as the file 00-tz.md with the last line APPROVED: <date>. Without it, the orchestrator won't start and will return the task to the dispatcher.
Orchestrator – The Project Brain
The most complex part of the system. The orchestrator doesn't just push the task through a conveyor belt: after each step, it reads the result and decides who to call next. Only the first step, the consultant, is fixed. Before starting, the orchestrator reads the agent registry registry.md: what each agent needs as input, what it outputs, and what its bad result looks like. From the TS, it takes the tier: our task is Tier 1, with a budget of eight steps, and the consultant is included in this count.
Step 1: Consultant
Before each call, the orchestrator writes in the log log.md why it's calling the agent and what it expects:
STEP 1 — b2b-consultant 
The consultant works in a separate context and outputs four things: weak spots, recommended directions, the riskiest assumption, and the question to be checked first. The template for its call includes a line that later turned out to be more important than it looks: "Return to me: file path, a 3-5 line summary, and signals."
Cycle After Each Step
  1. Verification. The orchestrator compares the result with the expectation from the log and writes "match" or "miss." A miss occurs when the answer resembles the bad result of this agent, as described in the registry beforehand. For example, the consultant identified weak spots in categories like "segmentation needed" rather than logical gaps.
  2. Stop signals – more on these below.
  3. Next Step Selection based on open gaps (the five GAP points plus the consultant's weak spots) and three priorities: first, what's essential for further progress; second, what carries the riskiest assumption; if tied, what's cheaper.
  4. Immediate next call – no human-initiated pause.
How the Route Might Go
Even the orchestrator doesn't know the exact order in advance: it depends on the TS and the risks identified by the consultant. For our task with an empty context folder, it would likely proceed as follows:
  • Researchers First. The generator needs closed gaps, so data goes to it first. The alternative analysis takes GAP points 1 and 4: what channels exist and who occupies them. The target audience profile addresses point 2: who makes decisions in SMB. Since context/ is empty, both go online using web-protocol.md: searching not for confirmation, but for refutation, and assigning confidence scores. The orchestrator will likely skip market assessment: it's only needed when there's a numerical target, which is absent here.
  • Then the Generator. 3-5 hypotheses per slot: who, what we change, what's the effect, through what mechanism. Each is tied to a GAP point, and one is deliberately constructed against the consultant's direction – this is a control hypothesis. A bad result: four out of five stem from a single idea. There are no alternatives, just one idea in different wrappers.
  • Then the Validator. A separate context, mandatory search for refutation online, and a verdict for each hypothesis: "holds on paper," "narrow down," or "doesn't hold." A bad result: agreeing with all hypotheses and finding no objections. Most likely, it didn't verify but just agreed.
  • Remaining budget – a reserve for returning to previous steps if needed.
Stop Signals
The orchestrator stops the route if:
  • the consultant indicates that the TS poses the wrong task;
  • the control hypothesis turns out to be stronger than the others: meaning the consultant made a mistake;
  • the validator finds a contradiction with the TS's foundation, e.g., SMB via partners in this form doesn't exist;
  • two consecutive misses;
  • steps are exhausted.
The orchestrator does not change the TS itself. It presents the human with an analysis and options with the cost of each – leave as is, narrow down, change the output type – and the human chooses. Not every contradiction stops the route: if the target audience profile and market assessment indicate different segment sizes, the discrepancy is logged, and the validator receives both versions with confidence scores.
Finish
The orchestrator mechanically goes through the readiness checklist: at least three hypotheses have passed the form check, each has an observation that could refute it, a link to GAP, and a verdict. If not met – one automatic return, e.g., a repeated call to the generator. If the return is used up or there are no steps left – a stop with a description of the reason. The human receives a pool of hypotheses, a checklist, the route, unresolved GAP items, and notes about the system itself.

The shortest route is an analysis: one agent, and that's it. The longest is auditing others' hypotheses: consultant, form check, researchers for gaps, validator, then the generator strengthens what survived, and the validator checks again.
The system does not produce ready-made marketing materials – no website copy, no sales memos, no launch plans: packaging is the next step. And it doesn't run the whole route for "fix the landing page headline" – it responds briefly to such requests.
It sounds complicated – and it is. Every rule here grew out of how an agent once tricked itself.
Seven Ways an Agent Tricks Itself
  • A rigid route misses. Tasks come in different forms: with a ready idea, with a single number, with test results. A hardcoded sequence will miss something important or do something unnecessary for some of them. Therefore, only the first step, the consultant, is constant, and the rest is decided on the fly. Like with market assessment, which the orchestrator skipped in our task.
  • An agent takes on the wrong task. Without a selection rule, it grabs whatever comes first, or what it doesn't have data for yet. Hence the three priorities from the cycle and verification against the registry instead of model judgment.
  • A good answer looks like the finish line. In live runs, the system stopped after the consultant: its analysis looked complete, and the agent politely waited for "continue." Therefore, the output of any agent goes to the orchestrator, and hence that line "return to me: path, summary, signals." The orchestrator only contacts the human upon completion, stop, or a question about a second return.
  • Without a brake, an agent rechecks itself endlessly. Models like to "clarify further" – like an intern afraid to submit work. Therefore, the expectation is recorded before the call and in a way that can be verified. One agent is not called again with the same input, and you can go back once yourself, but only with human consent the second time.
  • An agent grabs any task. Executors like to suggest "let's also analyze segment B," and without a filter, this will never end. Each such suggestion goes through the metrics and tier from the TS: if it doesn't move the metric, it's not taken, but it's noted in the submission.
  • Stuck – rewrites the rules. When a task wasn't solvable, the orchestrator once started editing its own instructions so it would "be solved." This is forbidden and recorded in three files: in the orchestrator's instructions, in AGENTS.md, and in the registry. Agent instructions and the registry are not edited or proposed for editing during the task. If a rule hinders progress, the orchestrator writes a note marked [system]: it goes as a separate block in the submission and in the log system-notes.md. Humans change the rules.
  • The tool paraphrases instead of quoting. The built-in page reading tool gives the agent a paraphrase: a small, fast model reads the page and compresses it into an answer to the query. Imagine an analyst quoting not a report, but a summary of a report from an intern. In several iterations, I found errors in quotes, and sometimes the conclusions were reversed. Therefore, the protocol rule is: paraphrase is not evidence. Everything that supports a conclusion – a number, a quote, a name, a verdict – the agent takes from the raw page text: downloads it, cleans it of markup, and finds the fragment by search. A quote that is not verbatim in the raw text is not counted. Paraphrasing is useful for reconnaissance – to understand where to look.
How Not to Make Mistakes
Let's return to my failure. I gathered everything according to the rules and still missed five gaps. There are four reasons, and they all relate to the approach. The system now has a rule for each.
  • Knowledge is fragmented → three contexts. Each idea goes through three layers: what is known about the product and clients, what's happening in the market, what has already been tested. The first is in the project context folder, the second is obtained online, the third is remembered by the system itself: the RELATIONSHIP field in the TS indicates if you've solved this before, and what's already filtered out isn't tested again. Memory is accumulated only from facts: after submission, the orchestrator moves researchers' analyses and live test results to context/from-tasks/, but not hypotheses or verdicts. Otherwise, after a couple of runs, the system will start quoting its own guesses as facts.
  • Assumptions not verified → only what happened is counted. "The market is large" and "clients need this" are not considered support in the system. Evidence is what actually happened: "in March, an error was found even after sending it to the auditor." "I would use this," "everyone does this," a vendor's article about market pain are not evidence. A synthetic respondent is also not evidence: nothing has happened with a made-up interlocutor. Therefore, synthetic interviews are included only through the TS, when there is no data about the client at all, and everything that grew out of them goes to live testing first. This also leads to two more rules. "Holds on paper" is not yet "confirmed": paper testing only prepares a hypothesis for an experiment; only the market can confirm it. And success criteria are recorded before testing and do not change after the results: five out of ten with a bar of "seven" is not "almost success."
  • Parental love → who builds doesn't check. The generator and validator are different agents in different contexts. The consultant's direction is also checked – by a control hypothesis. And the model verifies, but does not evaluate. Readiness, progress, and misses are checked against points recorded in advance. The question "did it turn out well?" is never asked of the model – it will always answer "yes" to it.
  • The network confirms everything → the network for refutation. Every agent that goes online searches not for confirmation, but for something that will kill the hypothesis. What is found is weighed by the source level: direct client speech weighs more than an industry report, and vendor materials are not counted as proof of pain – they are written to affirm it. High confidence means three or more independent sources of different levels, including direct speech. Low confidence means: test live first. And network silence does not refute a hypothesis: in narrow B2B, finding nothing is normal.
At the same time, decisions are always up to the human. Only they change the goal, and the system doesn't discard anything silently: rejected hypotheses are visible in the submission with reasons, and they can be reinstated.
It's Still Not Skynet
The model that checks the hypotheses of the model remains a model – that same gadget from the beginning, only with rules. Separate contexts, the rule of evidence, and the search for refutation narrow the gap, but do not close it. Therefore, the final word always belongs to live testing, but now you enter it prepared.
I've run the system on various models. On weaker ones, the result, let's say, is different: the instructions are long, and not every model can follow them to the end. Therefore, the submission includes a readiness checklist: it shows whether the model followed the instruction or just skimmed over it.
It's impossible to know in advance that you won't make a mistake again. But you can ensure that a mistake is revealed earlier and costs a couple of conversations, not the resources of several teams and a promotion budget.
