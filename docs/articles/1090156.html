What AI Can and Cannot Do in Banner Advertising

It's worth noting right away that when someone writes a prompt like "a girl with coffee in a cafe, morning light," they are solving an illustrator's task. But when writing a prompt for a banner, you need to think like a marketer and solve that problem. An illustration is self-sufficient; its value lies in the image itself. A banner, however, is a tool with specific requirements: space for a headline, space for a logo, limitations, and even click KPIs, as we've already mentioned.

So, it's important to understand that the real unit of work is the composition of all task elements. AI can perform this work, but the prompt will need to be carefully crafted. And immediately define the quality criteria, determine what a "quality banner" means to you. As we've said, unlike an illustration, the result in numbers is important. And all of this still needs to be polished on top, understanding that this is a campaign, and you need several, say 8-15, variants for different segments and platforms. This is where AI can be of great help, whether you already have a finished banner or are still working on banners; you can immediately determine the quantity and style. But selecting working variants and finalizing them is the marketer's job, not AI's.

Formats and AI

Typically, the most popular formats in advertising marketing are static, adaptive, and video banners. And AI performs differently in each of them – sometimes it generates excellent results, and sometimes it can only lead to losses.

Firstly, the most mature category is static banners. AI confidently generates any backgrounds, abstract compositions, product scenes without complex geometry, and portraits with controlled lighting. Tasks like background replacement, atmospheric backdrops, and series of scenes in different color schemes work well. However, precise typography within the image and complex product scenes are problematic.

But in adaptive formats, AI offers huge savings. If you suddenly need the same creative in 10-15 sizes, and manual recomposition takes hours, then using a combination of master image generation with automatic adaptation through templates reduces the time significantly. It's important to note that the neural network generates the master image, not each size separately.

However, AI is still weak in video banners. If your marketing strategy mimics the popularity of AI-generated videos and focuses on leveraging AI video and stories, then the neural network itself is the gimmick. If you are capitalizing on trends and not just doing it because it's cheap, it can be beneficial. But if you're generating videos aimlessly, AI is not helpful here; frames are inconsistent, there are artifacts in motion and object usage, and timing control for text is impossible. For a full-fledged video banner with a product, actor, and voiceover, AI is poorly suited, unless, as I've already mentioned, you want to capitalize on the trend of AI stories.

Limitations

It would be dishonest not to discuss the specific limitations of AI banner generation. Firstly, there's small text. Sometimes even GPT Image 2 makes mistakes in text generation, so text generation should only be done with full verification, and it's better to create and overlay all headlines and disclaimers yourself. Within the generation, it's best to allow text as a decorative element, and even then, check for typos or pseudo-text.

Also, neural networks are poor at specific brand colors. Asking to generate "corporate blue #1A4F8B" does not mean you will get it exactly in the output. The model interprets color as a concept, not a value. The output might be a shade similar to the requested one, but not exactly the same. If you have a strict brand book, this can be critical. Of course, the difference is often unnoticeable, and sometimes it doesn't matter, but if it does, you should adjust to brand colors during post-processing.

But what you should definitely not entrust to a neural network are legally significant elements. Disclaimers, promotion terms, license links, all sorts of markings, warnings – it's best to at least provide these as full, precise text during generation, and even better, insert them during post-processing.

And from the category of legally significant elements, you should check for human faces and trademarks. Copyright is a big risk, and you could face lawsuits for hundreds of thousands or millions of rubles if you don't notice that an advertising campaign with someone else's real face or trademark has been published. So, you should specify this in the prompt and check afterward.

Composition and Text

A banner involves not only image generation but also assembly: headline, sub-headline, CTA, logo, disclaimer, and so on.

Canva Magic and Adobe Firefly can help remove backgrounds, extend frames, adapt to templates, change palettes, or arrange elements according to a grid. Firefly is particularly useful because it's trained on licensed content – for teams wary of legal surprises, this reduces risk. Figma plugins (image generation, text auto-fill, mass element replacement) excel at integration into the design process and your layouts.

Consistency is a separate challenge. The same banner in 12 sizes should look like one banner, not 12 different ones. This is achieved not by generation, but by a template with a fixed grid and auto-substitution.

For copywriting, top-tier LLMs aren't necessary; prompt quality plays a bigger role. Here, you can ask to generate 30 headline options for one hypothesis, rephrase a Unique Selling Proposition (USP), shorten offers, or compile a list. It's useful when an LLM generates the text layer and CTA options, allowing you to then select visuals for a specific headline.

The most tedious part is automating sizes and adapting to platforms. Sometimes neural networks can help, and sometimes they can't. For example, a common workflow involves using a master file in an editor with a grid, automatically generating sizes, and then manually checking key formats.

Prompting

Prompts for creating banners and advertising creatives are not the same as for regular images. You need to describe the structure and composition. The order of elements matters; first, specify the format and purpose, then the composition, object, lighting, and style, and finally, what to avoid. For example: "Horizontal banner 728×90 16:9, with free space on the left for a headline, a large product on a neutral background on the right, soft side lighting, no text or logos."
