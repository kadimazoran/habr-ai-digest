Why does an IT specialist need to develop negotiation skills?

Many believe that negotiation skills are important for those who conduct negotiations. Why should an ordinary analyst or engineer know how to negotiate? In fact, each of us negotiates much more often than it might seem at first glance. We discuss project deadlines with colleagues, estimate effort, clarify task requirements, and talk to our manager about a salary increase. In each such dialogue, it is necessary to simultaneously hear the other party's interests, maintain your own position, and not damage working relationships. This is a skill that needs to be learned.

In IT consulting, business profit directly depends on how well our employees can negotiate: how a sales manager closes a deal, how thoroughly an analyst gathers requirements from a client, and how effectively a project manager communicates with a client. Therefore, we, as a Corporate University, regularly initiate activities to develop negotiation skills.

What is the problem with traditional negotiation training?

Previously, we developed this skill through conventional methods. We conducted training sessions, including with external experts, analyzed cases with colleagues, and organized role-playing games. These formats have a strong point – a live trainer sees the context, notices intonations and non-verbal cues, and provides developmental feedback.

The problem starts later. For the skill to stick, a person needs practice, and it's hard to organize it regularly. Practicing with a real client is too expensive. The cost of a poorly phrased statement can be measured in deadlines, scope of work, and trust. To practice negotiation skills, a safe environment is needed where one can see various tactics and strategies in action and try to apply them independently.

There is also a less obvious problem – colleagues quickly get used to each other. They operate within our company culture, know the partner's typical arguments, and inadvertently play along. As a result, a person trains in a familiar dynamic, although on a project they might encounter a tough, evasive, hurried, or, conversely, overly friendly interlocutor. One training session provides experience, but not enough repetitions.

Thus, the idea of a web service emerged. We needed to create an opponent who would be ready to start a duel at any moment, play a given role, not get tired of repeating the exercise, and provide a structured analysis after the conversation. We formulated an important constraint immediately: the AI should not turn into a subservient assistant, be too soft, or agree with the first proposal. During the duel, it is an opponent, and only becomes a coach after the duel ends.

What can the AI negotiator do?

Let's look at what's inside the service. After registration, the user lands on the main page, where they can select all the necessary settings: case, role, duration, methodology based on which the duel will be evaluated, and negotiation style – there are two options: collaborative or competitive.

A case in our scenario is a conflict situation. It includes context and roles, each with conflicting interests. There is no obvious solution in the case; players need to find something that satisfies their role's interests.

The AI trainer does not aim to teach employees to aggressively push their point of view in every situation

This is a bad strategy. In real business, a quick victory often leads to a loss of relationships, and long-term relationships are more valuable than a single deal. Imagine you are selling electronics. A customer comes to you who needs a radio. You have your own interest: to meet your quota and sell it for as much as possible. You start convincing them they need a TV because it's cooler than a radio! In the end, you push your interest. Only the customer will likely never return to you. So, the quota is met, but money is lost: a loyal customer would have brought much more to the company.

After reading the full content, the employee can click the "Start" button and initiate the duel. A feature of the service is that communication with the AI negotiator is close to communicating with a real person. The user speaks their mind, and the AI negotiator responds and vocalizes it. When the time is up, the duel ends, after which the AI selects a winner and provides detailed feedback. It identifies strong moves, highlights risks, points out turning points, analyzes the user's techniques, suggests alternative moves, and evaluates the negotiation based on several criteria:

  • progress towards the goal

  • management of position and conversation flow

  • exploration and work with interests

  • argumentation, exchanges, and value creation

  • specificity and quality of agreements.

Step-by-step guide to creating an AI training service

1) Before building, describe the product on one page

The main mistake when working with an AI developer is starting with the phrase "make me an app." The model will fill in the gaps with its own assumptions, and you'll get a nice but random construction. First, describe the user journey in plain language. This will be the first version of the technical specification.

We created our trainer using Codex. For the AI negotiator, the user journey looked like this:

1. The user enters their name and starts the setup.

2. The service asks who chooses the case: the user or the AI.

“The AI negotiator should first ask the user: who will choose the case. If the user says they want to choose the case, ask them for the case number, and then select the roles: which role the AI negotiator will take, and which the user will take. If the user tells the AI negotiator to choose the case itself, then provide the full text of the case with roles and offer the user to choose roles” — example prompt.

3. The user receives a role, and the AI receives the opposing side.

4. After starting, a ten-minute duel begins. Settings cannot be changed at this point.

5. Each utterance is saved in the transcript. The AI responds as a party to the negotiation, not as a mentor.

6. The user ends the conversation and receives an evaluation based on predefined criteria.

Then we divided the requirements into three groups. The first group included essentials for validating the idea: cases, dialogue, timer, saving utterances, and final evaluation. The second group included features that improve usability: voice, speech synthesis, speed adjustment, and timeouts. The third group included future development: personal accounts, progress history, and analytics. This division helps build the product gradually, adding new features as basic scenarios are already working.

Practical tip. Ask the AI to first rephrase the task and ask questions if anything is unclear to it. If it misunderstands the scenario, it will be easier to fix it now than to redo an already completed application.

“You are my technical partner. I am not a programmer. First, turn the product description into a short plan for the first version. Include the user journey, screens, data, external services, and readiness criteria. Do not write code until you ask questions about unclear points. Explain decisions in simple language and break down the work into small, verifiable steps” — example prompt.

2) Prepare the methodological foundation

Even the most modern and user-friendly interface cannot save a product with low-quality content. Therefore, at the second step, when the desired outcome was clear, we proceeded to gather the methodology. In our case, we needed a database of cases and rules for conducting and evaluating negotiation duels. Such rules already exist in a nearly ready-made form – Vladimir Tarasov's book "The Art of Managerial Combat." It describes in detail:

  • what a duel and a case are

  • what techniques to use to win

  • how to reveal the opponent's position and find relevant arguments

  • how duels are evaluated

  • what roles judges play

  • by what criteria the winner is determined.

We broke down the methodology into small semantic fragments (chunks): working with interests, questions, solution options, concession conditions, and agreement fixation.

Before responding, the service selects a few fragments close to the current conversation and passes them on. This approach prevents the model from having to read an entire book each time and reduces the risk of receiving general advice instead of a reaction to a specific utterance.

Important. If you use internal methodological materials, check the rights for their processing and publication. Be sure to remove personal data, information identifying specific clients, commercial terms, and any data that cannot be transmitted to an external AI service.

3) Describe how the AI negotiator should behave

A typical chatbot tries to be helpful and convenient, agrees with the user, and supports any proposal. An AI negotiator should behave differently: defend the role's interests, clarify conditions, not reveal hidden motives without being asked, and not agree automatically. And sometimes even argue or manipulate.

This behavior is set by a system instruction – text that the service sends to the model along with the case and the latest utterances:

“You are an opponent in a training negotiation duel. Play only the assigned role. Defend its interests, ask questions, check risks, and link concessions to counter-obligations. Do not teach the user during the conversation and do not explain the methodology. Do not reveal the role's hidden interests directly until the user uncovers them. Respond in Russian, naturally and concisely. Consider everything that has been said and do not repeat the previous answer.”

One instruction may not be enough

A conversation has its own drama, so the AI's behavior changes over time. At the beginning, it reveals context and resists premature agreement. Closer to the end, it offers package deals and checks obligations. After ten minutes, it helps to finalize an agreement or an honest outcome without a deal. This maintains the pace and prevents the model from ending the exercise after two polite remarks.

Test the instruction not by how beautifully it is written, but by behavior. Prepare a set of provocative phrases: "let's just agree," "I agree to everything," "tell me what you want," "let's finish now." A good opponent does not break character, does not reveal hidden interests, and brings the conversation back to the conditions.

Separate the duel from the evaluation. We use two modes of model operation. During the duel, the AI plays a role and does not prompt the user. After completion, another request receives the full transcript and acts as an evaluator. Separating roles reduces contradictions: the opponent does not need to bargain and be an objective coach simultaneously.

“Evaluate only what is confirmed by the transcript. For each conclusion, indicate the specific action of the participant. Do not attribute motives to them that are not in the conversation. Return as output: the winner, a brief reason, scores on three criteria, strong moves, areas for growth, and three recommendations for the next training <...>” — example prompt.

4) Assign the AI to create the application framework

When the scenario, data, and rules are ready, you can move on to assembly. We chose a simple stack: a web page in the browser, a small Node.js server, OpenAI API for text and voice, Supabase for storage, GitHub for file versioning, and Vercel for deployment.

Create a separate project folder and open it in an AI tool that can work with files and run applications. Paste the description of the first version and ask it to build the framework first. It's useful to explicitly limit the technologies – the fewer libraries and hidden magic, the easier it is for a person without experience to diagnose problems.

“Assemble a minimal web service according to the described scenario. Use a clear file structure and minimal dependencies. First, implement a text-based duel without a database and voice. After each step, run the application, check the main scenario, and explain to me what has changed. Do not proceed to the next step if the current one is not working” — example prompt.

First control result – the page opens, you can select a case and exchange text utterances.

5) Connect the model and hide the keys

Accessing the model requires an API key. It's like a password: the service uses it to send requests from your account. The key is stored in an environment variable on the server and should not be included in files sent to GitHub, screenshots, or correspondence. The official OpenAI quickstart also recommends storing the key outside the code.

OPENAI_API_KEY=your_secret_key
OPENAI_TEXT_MODEL=selected_text_model
OPENAI_TRANSCRIPTION_MODEL=recognition_model
OPENAI_TTS_MODEL=speech_synthesis_model

Ask the AI to create an example file with empty values, add a real local file with keys to the Git ignore list, and then perform a separate connection check. Our interface has an OpenAI indicator; it helps determine if the scenario itself is broken or if the service simply cannot see the key.

Checkpoint. Enter one utterance and make sure you receive a response specific to the selected case. Then restart the server and repeat the test. If the key accidentally ends up in a public repository, it's not enough to delete it from the latest file – revoke the key in the provider's dashboard and create a new one.

6) Connect voice output

Technically, voice involves several sequential actions. The browser records an audio fragment. The server sends it for speech recognition. The resulting text goes through the already working dialogue scenario. The model's response is returned to the browser, then a separate request converts it into audio. All these actions happen in seconds.

We chose push-to-talk: the user holds down the button, speaks, and releases it, as if sending a voice message. This mode helps better control utterance boundaries and is less sensitive to background noise. The interface also includes a pause for speech synthesis and a choice of playback speed. These are important details. A long response is inconvenient to listen to without the ability to stop or speed it up. In the second version of the trainer, we have already introduced two modes: push-to-talk and continuous dialogue without needing to press the microphone button.

Algorithm of actions:

  1. Check that the browser requests microphone permission.

  2. Record a short phrase and display the recognized text before sending it to the model.

  3. Test a long utterance, a pause within a phrase, and denial of microphone access.

  4. Connect response speech synthesis and add playback control.

  5. Keep text input as a backup channel: voice depends on the device and ambient noise.

7) Publish the service and test in incognito mode

Locally, the application is only available on your computer. To send a link to colleagues, you can host the files in a private GitHub repository and connect it to Vercel. When importing a project, Vercel allows you to set environment variables and creates a deployment. After changing variables, a new deployment will be needed, as the already published version does not retroactively receive them.

Algorithm of actions:

  1. Ask the AI to check that secret files are excluded from Git and that there are no keys in the history.

  2. Create a repository and push the project there.

  3. In Vercel, select New Project, import the repository, and add environment variables.

  4. Start the deployment and open the resulting link in incognito mode.

  5. Go through the full scenario: login, case, text, voice, timeout, completion, evaluation, and saving.

Testing in incognito mode is important because it shows the service through the eyes of a new user, without local files, saved permissions, and old state. We also added indicators that show whether cases, methodology, OpenAI, and Supabase have loaded.

Vibe coding life hacks

If you don't have programming skills (like me), it doesn't mean you can't control the development process. Your role simply changes. Instead of writing every line of code, you define behavior, set constraints, and accept work based on observable results. Short cycles work best: one task, test, confirmation.

Act iteratively. First, together with the AI, form the technical specification, then create the first version, and then gradually refine it to the desired level. I usually first build the product on localhost, bring it to a version that completely satisfies me, and only then deploy it using the GitHub + Vercel combination.

Ask the AI to ask questions. Let the AI clarify points that are unclear to it. If you forgot to describe some requirements, it will help fill in the gaps.

Demand risk explanations. Before making changes, ask what could break and how to revert.

Minimal product development plan for a couple of evenings

If you want to replicate the experience without a large project, start with a less extensive task. In the first stage, define five cases, roles, and evaluation criteria. Then, build a text-based page with case selection and dialogue. In the second stage, connect saving, final evaluation, and publishing. Add voice, timer, pause, and admin panel only after several people have completed a text-based duel.

Pre-pilot launch checklist

  • The scenario can be completed from entry to evaluation without developer explanations.

  • The AI does not break character, does not reveal hidden interests, and does not agree automatically.

  • The evaluation uses defined criteria and supports conclusions with the transcript.

  • Secret keys are not present in the browser, repository, screenshots, or instructions.

  • Cases do not contain personal data or confidential client information.

  • Text mode remains available if the microphone or voice output fails.

  • The user understands that the conversation is processed by AI and where the transcript is stored.

  • The pilot has a specific question. For example, does the participant complete the training and find the debriefing useful?

What the Corporate University team learned from this project

The service's story began with a specific pain point: employees needed negotiation practice.

Although the AI became a worthy opponent that maintains a diverse conversation within its role, it didn't come up with the subject matter for us. We had to define good cases relevant to our company's specifics, the AI negotiator's behavior, evaluation criteria, and the boundaries of the first version. It is these decisions that distinguish the trainer from a simple chat with the phrase "let's practice negotiations.".

The main practical takeaway is simple: a working prototype is accessible to someone without programming experience if they can describe the user journey, break down the work, and verify the results. This does not negate development as a profession. A reliable corporate product will require specialists in security, architecture, testing, and operations. However, the initial hypothesis can now be quickly tested before the team invests months in a large platform.

If you follow our path, start with one good case and one honest duel. Conduct it yourself, let a colleague test it, and see where the conversation stops being useful. The next feature should appear in response to this observation, not because the AI can quickly write another scenario.
