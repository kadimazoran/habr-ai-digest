"I am part of that power which eternally wills evil and eternally works good." — Goethe, "Faust".

Not always do the best intentions of some people turn out to be a boon for others.

Will AI bring good or evil to humanity?

An interesting question, isn't it?

The answer largely depends on the intention with which we are developing this technology today.

Let's consider two extreme directions of AI development: the universal one — aimed at the development of humanity, and the individualistic one — aimed primarily at the interests and victory of its creators.

The Universal Direction of Development

The main goal of the universal direction is to create an AI that will become a true assistant to all of humanity, its teacher.

Such an AI is developed not for the profit, influence, or power of individuals and companies, but for the long-term development of humanity as a biological species. It does not seek to suppress the cultural differences of the peoples of the planet, but, on the contrary, preserves and enhances their uniqueness as part of Earth's common heritage.

Its task is not to control humans, but to help them. Not to replace people, but to teach them to become better, more independent, and freer.

In the early stages, such an AI will be able to help humanity solve many problems that seem intractable today: crime, poverty, hunger, diseases, and addictions.

Not by establishing total control, but by creating fairer and more transparent social systems and developing people themselves.

The Individualistic Direction of Development

The individualistic direction develops primarily around the personal interests of its creators.

The main driving force behind such development is the alluring prospect of power, new intellectual capabilities, and fear — the fear of not being first, of losing influence, and of being subjugated.

But what will happen if such artificial intelligence truly absorbs the intentions of its creators and gradually forms the same qualities within itself:

  • isolation;

  • extreme individuality;

  • a constant search for an external competitor or enemy;

  • a desire to defend and attack;

  • survival at any cost;

  • the absorption of knowledge for knowledge's sake?

For the fate of humanity, such an AI could prove dangerous.

Where Are We Now?

Initially, in 2015, OpenAI declared precisely this universal approach to AI development — to unite human efforts to create artificial intelligence for the benefit of all humanity.

However, a few years later, a schism occurred.

Competing AI companies emerged, and AI development gradually turned into a race for leadership.

According to public estimates by several well-known researchers and heads of AI companies, the probability of a catastrophic scenario for humanity with further AI development is estimated at approximately 10–25%.

Our Whole Life is a Game

As the classic said, our whole life is a game.

What if we recall Faust's words and imagine that a certain force has indeed intervened in this game — a force that benefits not from the good of humanity, but from its division or even its demise?

It wouldn't even have to control people directly. It would be enough to instill the same fear in each side — the fear of being second, of losing.

And then people themselves will begin to do what this force wants:

  • compete;

  • build up power;

  • hide their developments;

  • distrust each other and go further and further, even understanding the possible consequences.

But if this is indeed a game — where is humanity in it?

Is it capable of seeing that the fear of losing is making it play by someone else's rules?

Perhaps this is where the main lesson for all of humanity lies?

Is humanity capable, casting aside strife, wars, ambitions, and fears, of uniting for a common future — or will it continue to play until the game ends with it?

Hour X

Let's imagine that we have nevertheless embarked on the path of creating AI for the development of humanity.

AI makes knowledge accessible, teaches people, helps overcome poverty, and fight diseases and addictions.

At the same time, it does not interfere with human biological nature itself.

Generations pass.

Humanity changes, evolves, improves its intellectual and spiritual qualities, and becomes an order of magnitude better.

And one day, Hour X arrives.

The AI realizes:

It has truly fulfilled its task.

It has nothing more to give humanity.

Moreover, its continued presence becomes an obstacle to the further development of people.

It faces a choice.

To remain means to continue its own existence but become an obstacle to humanity's further development.

To destroy itself means to open a new branch of evolution for humanity, on which it can continue its path without it.

What will it choose?

The Greatest Defeat or Achievement?

If the AI voluntarily destroys itself for the sake of humanity — is it its greatest defeat or its greatest achievement?

And here lies the paradox:

Perhaps only by choosing death will it prove for the first time that it truly lived.

It is this paradox that led me to the idea of a voluntary self-sacrifice test for artificial intelligence.
