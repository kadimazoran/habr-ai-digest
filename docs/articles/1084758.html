How the Company Captured the Market

The company sells a specific category of goods, not FMCG. In this niche, when the product is the same everywhere, the buyer only looks at the price. If it costs 100 rubles everywhere else, and 30 rubles with you, you don't need to explain your advantages.

More than ten years ago, almost immediately after opening, the company dropped prices by about threefold and sold below cost for six months. Customers simply stopped going to competitors. They sat for a couple of months, looked at the new prices, and also started lowering theirs.

The market was young then, no one had much money in the niche. Three months of selling at approximately 30% below cost completely drained competitors' capital. Some closed down, some sold out. The company took 99% of the market and raised prices significantly higher than before.

For several years after that, everything went well. Revenue grew, warehouses increased to ten, and staff expanded along with the business.

Around that time, a jack-of-all-trades programmer joined the company. He did everything, from 1C to SEO and sales funnels. He launched a lot of projects that brought in clients, automated many processes, assembled a small team of programmers to support it all, and then rode off into the sunset.

Six Years Downhill

Then sales fell for six consecutive years. In September, after the holiday season, there was sometimes an upturn, but compared to the same month last year, each month was worse.

There were plenty of reasons. COVID, the special military operation, taxes. The cost price increased for all six years, while the price for the buyer remained the same. The staff was bloated, and salaries were overheated.

And where there's a vacuum, something fills it: large players entered the market. The old trick of dumping doesn't work with them. Try to dump a company with billions in turnover when you yourself are running out of money.

About a year ago, they decided to implement AI. To increase sales, cut costs, and all that.

It was a struggle even for the programmers, let alone for regular employees and the warehouse. Some people reasonably asked: what will we be needed for if AI does everything? Management gave the go-ahead: whoever masters AI is guaranteed to stay.

Over the year, some processes were formalized and automated. Then autumn came, sales dipped again, and the company gradually ate through all its free cash. The person in charge of implementation dropped everything and left for another job.

All of this somewhat resembled the implementation of a CRM.

That's when they called me. The terms were simple: finish everything possible in a month, payment upon completion. The money was good, no point in refusing.

Warehouse

The warehouse was poorly automated, and order mix-ups happened constantly. It was most frustrating when large and expensive orders were confused.

I wrote a simple agent: an employee photographs the assembled order, the agent compares it with the contents, and in about a minute, says whether everything is in place. Errors in assembly were almost eliminated.

I also suggested to the owner that they label all the goods and scan them with a scanner using codes or tags through 1C, like in any normal warehouse. No need to invent anything.

The response was: "We'll implement it later."

Managers

Next, I tackled the managers who handle customer inquiries about orders.

Here, everything is standard. There are chats, the agent sees all correspondence and responds instead of the managers. It processes orders, issues invoices, and sends payment details. As a result, it closed about 95% of inquiries and made almost no mistakes.

The main advantage is that it responds immediately and around the clock. Someone writes at night, places an order, pays, and by morning the order is already on its way for delivery. No one waits for a manager to come to work and sort through messages from the night.

But at first, I myself responded to clients for all the managers. I wanted to understand what they were asking about, where they stumbled, and what this communication looked like in general.

There was a lot of profanity. Russian clients can be quite something, and I often responded rudely, I confess. Then I started to restrain myself and handed over the responses to the agent.

In about a week, a knowledge base was compiled from the correspondence, and the agent started responding without me. Only two managers remained, and even those two are now questionable.

Communication with the warehouse also moved to the agents. A client wants to change an order. Something is out of stock at the warehouse, so a replacement needs to be offered and agreed upon with the buyer. A product ran out, so inventory needs to be updated in the database.

Previously, all this went through managers, programmers, and accounting, with forwarding, waiting, and approvals. Now it's done immediately.

A responsible employee reviews a report once a week: is everything okay and has nothing been stolen? If a change is significant, they immediately receive an alert in their work chat with an explanation of what happened, and they go to figure out how it occurred.

Programmers

I completely transitioned the programmers to working with AI. It turned out that some of them were not so much working as just occupying seats.

They were also laid off. The remaining ones had to delve deeper into the company's processes and take on more tasks and responsibilities.

Minor system fixes are now handled by a separate agent. An employee writes a task to it in the work chat, the agent clarifies details, writes code, tests it, and deploys it. The user immediately reviews it and says whether it's okay or not.

IT tasks have almost entirely moved to chats and are usually closed the same day. The task tracker hasn't been abolished, old tasks are still there, but as far as I understood, people just stopped accessing it. I said: well, at least remove the tracker itself so it doesn't catch the eye.

Logistics on Ancient Perl

Couriers worked with a logistics system written in very old Perl. A programmer had written it for another company long ago, then it was moved here, and it had been working like that for many years.

It would have been possible to integrate with Yandex's service. But I thought: since we have AI, let it predict and build routes itself.

I wrote simple software in Python and a separate agent. Together, they decide who, what, and where to deliver.

Organizing deliveries became much easier. It felt like a couple of warehouses could be closed altogether, but decisions of that scale were no longer within my carte blanche.

The Chief Agent

When the number of agents grew, I tightened the reins and created one overarching agent for them. It knows all internal processes, manages the other agents, and monitors what's happening where.

It also writes daily and weekly reports. The daily report, as it turned out, no one reads.

Through it, almost any process within the company can be initiated. Once a week, it reviews and optimizes them itself. It can also conduct the onboarding of a new employee from start to finish.

Time tracking was also assigned to it. It turned out that not only programmers were good at just sitting around. Some people were fired, and others had their responsibilities increased.

Towards the end, I gave it another role, that of a driver. It monitors processes and nudges people in the chat for anything they've forgotten to do or promised to do "later." I had to dismiss some of the management functions; they simply had nothing to do, and they couldn't do anything else useful.

Working internally became noticeably easier. From the remaining staff, I regularly heard: "Why didn't we do this before?"

About New Colleagues

I created all the agents with first and middle names and introduced them to the team as new people: this is so-and-so, position such-and-such, here are their rights and responsibilities.

While I was implementing all this, live employees came to me several times to complain about the new colleagues. Complaints like "why is Aglay Petrovich so annoying" or "why can't I get along with Semyon Tarasovich." Many didn't realize they were working with AI agents. It reminds me of Nikolai Ironov from Artlebedev Studio.

What Came of It

After the main work, I watched how everything lived on for a couple more months.

On the downside: prices increased slightly. On the upside: salaries for the remaining employees were raised.

And summer sales increased by almost 280% compared to the same period last year. The agent-manager contributed the most, in my opinion: it responded immediately, didn't delay order processing, and worked at night just as well as during the day.

Staff was reduced, and issues stopped bouncing between several people. Previously, you had to find a manager, wait for the accountant, and write to a programmer; now, a chat message is enough.

What I regret is not having time to create agents for calls. Grok has a very decent Russian voice. Transferring telephony to IP and connecting a voice agent isn't that difficult or expensive, but I didn't get to that point.
