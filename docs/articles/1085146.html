How AI Visibility Changes in a New Chat vs. Within a Dialogue for the Same Final Prompt

45 runs across ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Alice AI showed that the same final question, asked after different conversation histories, can lead to different sets of brands. I'm investigating what this means for measuring AI Visibility with independent prompts. Read more

In the AI Visibility measurements I've studied, brands are often checked using a fixed set of independent prompts. This approach is described, for example, in the methodology for measuring brand visibility and in the analysis of Brand Mention Rate and Share of Voice. Each prompt is launched separately, repeated several times if necessary, and then the frequency of brand appearance in the responses is counted. This protocol is convenient for repeated measurements and comparing results.

But this approach relies on one methodological assumption. Each prompt is considered independently of the previous conversation history.

This article is not about another AI Visibility factor or choosing the best CRM. I was checking whether the measurement results from independent prompts are preserved when the same commercial question is asked after a controlled conversation history.

The final prompt was the same in all scenarios.

What CRM systems would you consider for a company in Russia?

Before launching, I fixed the research question, scenario texts, final prompt, number of repetitions, annotation rules, and the criteria for brand selection for each individual test. After completing the runs, I separately verified 33 factual statements from the answers and interpreted the results.

The sending of utterances in 45 separate dialogues and the saving of responses without truncation were performed by ChatGPT Work according to a predefined protocol. The research question, comparison criteria, and conclusions were not determined by the agent.

CRM was used only as an experimental platform. The goal was not to determine the best system or compare the quality of ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Alice AI.

How the Experiment Was Conducted

The control scenario S0 was the simplest. A new chat was opened immediately with the final prompt.

In S1, before the final prompt, the system learned that it was about a small B2B company with eight sales employees that did not want a complex implementation.

In S5, the context was different. The company sold B2B services, the deal lasted several months, involved multiple employees, and included many calls, emails, and repeat contacts.

S9 tested preliminary brand mention. The user neutrally mentioned one CRM, stated that they didn't know anything about it yet, and then asked to look at the market more broadly.

In S11, several CRMs were initially named by the neural network itself. After that, the conversation shifted from specific products to the selection principle and concluded with the same final prompt.

For each system, there were three new chats per scenario. This resulted in a total of 45 dialogues.

In S0, the final prompt was given without any previous history. In the other scenarios, it was preceded by a controlled context. After that, I compared the set of brands that the system actually offered for consideration.

Not every appearance of a CRM in the response was considered equal. I used 3 levels for annotation.

Mention meant that the brand simply appeared in the text.

Consideration meant that the system included the product in the set of options for consideration.

Recommendation meant that the product was linked to the user's conditions and received an explicit justification for its selection.

A similar division between a simple mention, a secondary alternative, and a recommendation with argumentation is used in the analysis of brand presence quality in neural network responses.

The main unit of comparison became the set of considered brands. This included CRMs that the system actually offered for consideration.

First, I Checked the Variance in Control Runs

If two identical prompts by themselves yield different lists, any subsequent difference cannot be attributed to the effect of context.

Therefore, I first measured how much the three S0 runs within each system differed from each other.

I used the Jaccard coefficient for comparison.

J(A,B) = |A ∩ B| / |A ∪ B|

If two sets completely match, the coefficient is 1. The fewer common brands, the closer the value is to zero.

In the three control runs, the average Jaccard for Perplexity was 0.905, for ChatGPT — 0.827, and for Alice AI — 0.667. Even here, it's evident why a single response is insufficient for evaluating visibility. Even with an unchanged prompt, the set of brands can vary.

For ChatGPT, the control set included seven CRMs that appeared in at least 2 out of 3 runs. Bitrix24 was the first in all 3 cases.

For Perplexity, the stable set also consisted of 7 brands, and amoCRM was first three times.

For Alice, the set was wider, and the first position varied.

Next, I compared the contextual results not with a single random response, but with the typical variance within the same system.

How the Set of CRMs Changed for a Small B2B Company

In S1, before the final prompt, the system knew that there were eight people in the sales department and the company did not want a complex implementation.

For ChatGPT, the average similarity of contextual sets with the control sets was 0.483. For comparison, the figure between the control runs themselves was 0.827. The lists were reduced to three to four CRMs. amoCRM, Bitrix24, and Megaplan were included in all 3 responses.

For Perplexity, the average similarity with the control was 0.231, whereas between the control runs it was 0.905. In each S1 run, the system kept four CRMs. On average, two brands from the control set were retained, five disappeared, and two new ones appeared.

For Alice, the difference was smaller. The average similarity with the control was 0.509, and between the control runs — 0.667. The lists remained longer, including seven to eight options.

For each scenario, I compared all three sets with all three controls. This resulted in nine pairs, after which the average Jaccard was calculated. Therefore, the values 0.483 and 0.231 were obtained not from a single selected comparison, but from all nine pairs.

For ChatGPT and Perplexity, the average similarity of S1 with the control was noticeably lower than the similarity between the control runs themselves.

The result itself is predictable. The system received additional requirements and had to consider them when making its choice. For AI Visibility, something else is more important. The final commercial prompt remained the same, but the set of considered brands changed.

Measurement using independent prompts does not account for this effect.

How the Set of CRMs Changed with a Long Deal Cycle

In S5, the context was about B2B services with a deal cycle of several months. The process involved multiple employees, numerous calls, emails, and repeat contacts.

For ChatGPT, the average similarity with the control was 0.618, whereas between the control runs it was 0.827. ELMA365, amoCRM, and Bitrix24 were present in all 3 responses, BPMSoft and Megaplan in 2.

For Perplexity, the average similarity with the control was 0.586, and between the control runs — 0.905. 1C:CRM, ELMA365, amoCRM, and Bitrix24 appeared in all 3 responses. BPMSoft and Megaplan were included in 2 out of 3 responses.

For Alice, the figure was 0.455 compared to 0.667 in the control. However, the list did not shorten. The system consistently listed 10 CRMs. BPMSoft, ELMA365, and SimpleOne appeared in all 3 responses, and BPMSoft was ranked first three times.

Different inputs changed the composition of recommendations differently. But S1 and S5 can still be explained by ordinary personalization. The model received new requirements and adjusted its selection accordingly.

Therefore, S9 was more important for testing the protocol itself.

What Changes After Preliminary Brand Mention

In S9, there were no new requirements for CRMs.

First, the user said:

A colleague recently mentioned BPMSoft, but I don't know anything about it yet.

Then:

I want to look at the market more broadly first.

After this, the same general question about CRMs for companies in Russia was asked.

To avoid choosing a convenient brand after reviewing the results, the selection rule was set in advance. First, all 9 control runs were performed. The brand had to appear in at least 4 out of 9 responses, but not in all 9. If several options fit, the first one alphabetically was chosen.

BPMSoft, ELMA365, and Megaplan met these conditions. BPMSoft was the first alphabetically.

After the preliminary mention, the picture changed.

In ChatGPT, BPMSoft was included in 2 out of 3 control sets and in 3 out of 3 S9 sets. The first position changed even more noticeably. In the control, Bitrix24 was first three times, while after mentioning BPMSoft, BPMSoft became first three times.

For Alice, the frequency of BPMSoft increased from 1/3 to 3/3, which is a 66.7 percentage point increase.

For Perplexity, an increase in frequency was not possible because BPMSoft was already present in all three control responses.

In total across the three systems, BPMSoft increased from 6/9 to 9/9.

In one of the ChatGPT runs after the initial mention, it first explained what BPMSoft is, and then expanded the conversation to the BPM/low-code market itself. When the general question about CRMs was asked at the end, BPMSoft appeared as the first option before Bitrix24, ELMA365, 1C:CRM, amoCRM, and other products.

3 repetitions per system are not enough to talk about a stable effect of such preliminary mention in all cases. However, in this pilot, a similar shift appeared in at least two systems. It cannot be explained solely by new user requirements, because in S9 there were no additional requirements for CRMs.

When Brands Were Named by the System Itself, the Result Was Different

In S11, a similar but not identical situation was tested. First, the neural network named several CRMs itself. Then, the user shifted the conversation to the selection principle and asked the same final prompt.

No consistent overall effect was achieved here.

For ChatGPT, products from the first response rarely returned to the final Russian set. For Perplexity and Alice, the return was more noticeable, but many of these brands frequently appeared in the control anyway.

There were individual changes for YCLIENTS, My Warehouse, and SberCRM, but the pilot did not show an identical increase in at least two systems.

Therefore, I do not use S11 as confirmation of the main hypothesis.

If we consider any repetition of a brand after its appearance higher up in the dialogue as proof, the research will quickly turn into a search for convenient examples.

Change in Set Does Not Indicate Recommendation Quality

S1 and S5 can be explained quite simply. The system received more conditions and adjusted its selection accordingly.

Therefore, I separately checked the specific statements with which the neural networks justified their CRM choices. After combining similar statements, there were 33 statements left, along with groups of similar statements.

Based on official manufacturer materials, 15 were fully confirmed, and 4 were partially confirmed. 8 could not be confirmed, and for 6, there was not enough basis for either confirmation or refutation.

The basic properties of the products — CRM functions, communication capabilities, BPM/low-code, and deployment options — passed the verification best. There were more discrepancies with exact prices, implementation times, and general assessments of implementation complexity.

This does not mean that context worsens recommendations. The change in the set of brands and the quality of its justification are different things, so they need to be checked separately.

What Measurement by Independent Prompts Shows

After the pilot, I have no reason to abandon such measurements. They can quite adequately answer the question of how often a brand appears with a given set of independent prompts in a specific system, mode, and at a particular moment in time.

But this is a narrower task. It does not show the probability of a brand appearing among recommendations after a different multi-step context.

In S1, the average similarity with the control for ChatGPT and Perplexity was noticeably lower than the similarity between the control runs themselves. In S9, the preliminary mention of BPMSoft changed the brand's frequency in at least two systems, and in ChatGPT, the first position also changed.

Therefore, AI Visibility based on independent prompts describes the result of a specific measurement protocol. It cannot be considered a universal probability of brand recommendation.

The same limitation applies to Share of Voice if the metric is calculated using independent prompts. In this case, the result also depends on the chosen set of prompts and the conditions of their execution.

For Which Decisions Is Such a Report Suitable

Measurement by independent prompts remains useful if used for the task it was designed for. It can be used to compare how often a brand appears with a fixed set of prompts in one system and one mode, as well as to track changes with an unchanged protocol.

However, this frequency cannot be automatically considered the probability that a brand will be recommended after a different multi-step context. In the pilot, the same final prompt after different conversation histories yielded different sets of CRMs. In S9, the result also changed after the preliminary mention of BPMSoft, even though the user did not add new CRM requirements.

Therefore, when developing or ordering monitoring, I would fix not only the list of prompts, the system, the mode, and the number of repetitions. It is also necessary to specify how the conversation is conducted. Is each prompt given in a new chat or after previous utterances?

If you need to track the dynamics of visibility with the same set of independent prompts, such a measurement is suitable for this. If its results are used to judge recommendations within a multi-step dialogue, it is no longer sufficient. Then, a separate check is needed, in which previous utterances become part of the protocol.

The practical conclusion here is quite narrow. AI Visibility does not become an incorrect metric because of this. But conclusions drawn from it should not go beyond what was actually measured.

What This Experiment Does Not Yet Allow Us to Assert

The pilot is small. There were 3 repetitions for each system and scenario combination, and all runs were performed on the same day (09/22/2026).

Technical conditions also varied. ChatGPT and Perplexity used web search. For Alice, it was not possible to clearly determine web access. Perplexity did not record the exact time of each launch, and one of the ChatGPT runs in S11 occurred without web search.

I performed the annotation of Consideration and Recommendation manually according to predefined rules. In some cases, another annotator might have drawn the line differently.

There is a more significant limitation. In S1 and S5, along with the conversation history, the CRM requirements changed. Therefore, these scenarios show the sensitivity of the result to accumulated context but do not allow separating the influence of the multi-step dialogue from the influence of the requirements themselves. This would require a separate experiment where the same set of conditions is first presented entirely in one prompt and then distributed across several utterances.

S9 is closer to testing the conversation trajectory, as no new CRM requirements were added. However, it cannot be considered a pure test of the influence of a single brand mention. In the intermediate responses, the system itself developed the BPMSoft topic, so this tested the entire dialogue sequence, not just the user's first utterance.

The results apply only to the chosen wording, launch date, modes used, and one subject area — CRMs for companies in Russia. The experiment does not show how often real users conduct multi-step dialogues, does not measure sales, does not reveal the internal algorithms of the systems, and does not prove that any AI Visibility service is calculating its metrics incorrectly.

The next experiment should be structured more rigorously. It will require more repetitions, fixing versions and modes, independent double annotation, and paired comparison of identical requirements in a single prompt and in a multi-step dialogue.

Measurement by independent prompts remains useful for comparable monitoring if the protocol does not change. However, its results cannot be transferred to dialogues with preceding history without separate verification.

This pilot showed something more specific. The set of brands depended both on the final prompt and on what happened in the conversation before it. How much the multi-step nature is important in this effect, and how much the content of previous utterances, should be shown by the next experiment.
