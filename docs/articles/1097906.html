The use of AI can yield short-term results but may hinder long-term learning.

A new 30-month study examined the performance of 26,811 Chinese schoolchildren. Researchers assessed the impact of generative AI on their learning process.

The good news: Students using AI saw their homework scores increase by 18% and completed assignments about 30% faster. Higher results in less time – metrics that would please any educator.

The bad news: The same students' scores on exams taken without AI assistance dropped by 20% six months later. Entrance exam scores fell by 18–24%, with the most significant decline appearing about two years into AI use.

TL;DR:

  • Using AI to "think for you" can impede learning.

  • The most capable students experience the greatest knowledge loss when using AI.

  • Applying personal effort alongside AI use can foster more effective learning.

Approximately 80% of students who used AI exhibited the consequences of "offloading" their thinking process to an external tool. They completed tasks unusually quickly and received good homework grades, but performed poorly when AI tools were unavailable.

Similar trends are observed in the professional world. According to a 2026 survey by IT company GoTo, 39% of employees report that over-reliance on AI has already weakened their professional skills. The dynamic is the same, though the circumstances differ.

This raises a critical question for every parent, leader, and professional: if AI is thinking for you, what is happening to your own ability to think and achieve your goals?

Effort is Key to Learning

Psychologists call this "cognitive offloading." It occurs when you delegate thinking tasks to an external tool to conserve mental energy. Using a calculator or setting reminders are examples of offloading that free up brain resources for more complex problems.

However, this strategy becomes detrimental when the task you delegate to AI was originally intended to build skills—such as completing homework or undergoing workplace training.

Ethan Mollick, a professor at the Wharton School of Business who studies AI adoption across various fields, summarized the study on Chinese students: AI "harms learning if its use negates intellectual effort."

Our brains develop their capabilities precisely through the process of overcoming challenges.

When a task arises, the mind accesses existing knowledge, builds new neural connections, and forms pattern-recognition skills—processes that are impossible otherwise. Judgment is developed in those moments when the answer isn't readily apparent and the brain must work to find it.

Tools that eliminate the need for effort gradually undermine the learning and development process.

This is precisely why the most successful students suffered the most: they were better at offloading their work onto AI. Homework scores gave no hint of the problem—on the contrary, the work was done well, as these students were adept at prompting AI. Paradoxically, those who could benefit most from AI were ultimately the most vulnerable to its negative effects.

The Invisible Complexity of Being

The difficulty in assessing the consequences of cognitive offloading is that they manifest with a delay. Homework scores might look better than ever, but students' knowledge decline only became noticeable months later, with full consequences appearing nearly two years in.

This time lag creates a feedback loop that exacerbates the problem of learning with AI. When the outcome looks impressive, there's no clear signal that the approach to work or study needs to change. By the time the problem becomes obvious—such as after a failed exam, a botched interview, or any situation requiring independent thinking—the skill-loss process has already progressed significantly and unnoticed.

Researchers from MIT's Sloan School of Management, who studied similar dynamics in the workplace, termed this the "augmentation trap": short-term productivity gains eventually undermine the very professional competencies on which those gains were initially based. In other words, employees using AI achieve excellent results initially but gradually lose the skills necessary for long-term success when working with such technologies. According to GoTo's research, 46% of Gen Z believe AI dependency weakens their professional skills. This is a serious concern, as many young professionals are currently laying the foundation of knowledge and judgment that will shape their entire careers.

The Path to True Learning

The study conducted in China revealed that students who used AI while continuing to exert significant effort experienced minimal loss in learning quality. Using AI as a tool—rather than a "crutch"—allowed them to deeply grasp material and retain knowledge.

If you fear AI hindering learning, the antidote is an approach to technology that develops human capabilities over time. View AI as an auxiliary resource for thinking, not a complete replacement for it.

Looking at the broader picture, this brings us to the question of what "intelligence" truly is and where it comes from. As I note in my recent book, Experiential Intelligence, being "smart" means possessing a combination of IQ (intelligence), EQ (emotional intelligence), and wisdom accumulated through real-life experience. Experience shapes your thinking and abilities, helping you develop judgment—a quality more valuable than ever in the age of AI. Students who interacted with AI essentially enriched their learning process, while those who used the technology merely to avoid assignments deprived themselves of this opportunity.

Regardless of who you are—a parent, educator, or leader—ask yourself (and those you support): where AI helps you achieve results, are you continuing to think in ways that develop your capabilities long-term? Or are you getting better results by becoming increasingly dependent on the tool that creates them?
