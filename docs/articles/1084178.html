When I need to learn something about a tool or service, I want to see what other people are writing about them first.

And over the last year, this habit has started to falter. There are not fewer pages on the internet; on the contrary, their number has increased. It's just that out of eight open tabs, seven tell the same thing, in the same words, in the same order.

I understand that the text was most likely not written by a human. It was an AI model, most likely not very sophisticated and without proper prompting. For AI, the whole world, all information, all text is just tokens. Strings like "RFg♴4#-f体$fÆmZ" and any other are ultimately just a set of numbers. We humans invent the meaning behind them. And we try to convey this meaning to the neural network during its training. Artificial intelligence text is neither here nor there — something averaged between all the training data.

And so the internet is becoming — averaged. Truth and intent are becoming blurred.

Babylon

In 1941, Argentine writer Jorge Luis Borges conceived a story about a library containing all possible books. Not all written, but all possible, meaning any combination of characters that could fit into a book. Among them lie all the true texts of humanity, all their drafts, all their translations, and all their typos.

The story is called "La biblioteca de Babel," which translates to "The Library of Babel." I highly recommend reading the story to anyone who hasn't yet. It's short and can be Googled in a couple of seconds.

So, in my humble opinion, the internet is approaching such a state of a "library." But worse.

What Happened in the Hexagons

The story "La biblioteca de Babel" was published in the collection "El jardín de senderos que se bifurcan" in 1941.

The library in the story is a world composed of hexagonal rooms. The walls of the rooms are shelves filled with books.

There are twenty long shelves on each wall; each wall, of course, except two, for the height of the library equals the height of the floors, which barely exceeds the height of an average librarian.

99.9% of these books are essentially just junk and nonsense. Well, due to the infinite possibilities of these books, by the law of large numbers, absolutely everything can be contained there: the exact biography of your life, a translation of the Bible into non-existent languages, a dictionary for this non-existent language, a catalog indicating the location of all dictionaries in the library, a cookbook from Star Wars, answers to all of humanity's questions, and even questions that humanity hasn't even considered yet.

The entire existence of the people in this story, their entire meaning of life, is the search for that one book (they call them Revelations) that will reveal the meaning of their lives and predict their fate and future. Seas of meaninglessness, tens of thousands of hours of futile searches.

White Noise is Worse Than Gibberish

Let's return to our artificial intelligence.

ChatGPT and other LLMs are, after all, not quite the Library of Babel. Their answers are not random strings of letters, but predictions based on training data, which contain vast amounts of text written by humans.

They do not contain everything in the world and are not capable of reproducing everything that has ever been said or written. Therefore, when talking about such systems, it is important to remember: these are technologies with their own history. They are influenced by the biases of the source data, countless small decisions by the people who create them, as well as laws and cultural norms that lead developers to avoid certain responses.

And yet, when it comes to sources of information, I believe that large language models are much closer to the Library than to most other analogies we are familiar with.

For example, an LLM is definitely not a calculator.

A calculator can be trusted because it is usually correct, and when it rounds a repeating decimal, it makes a predictable error. A model makes errors much less predictably. If it gives a wrong answer, it is much harder to predict in advance what exactly its error will be.

Searching for information manually can also lead to an incorrect answer, but usually, links to sources remain nearby. A user who knows how to search can open them, evaluate their quality, and understand how well the found information matches their task. (Yes, AI output also uses sources as input, and still makes mistakes, and very spontaneously. Once, a friend and I argued about whether two words were related or not: "порода" (breed/rock type) and "род" (kin/gender/genus). The AI confidently stated they were related and that the root for both was род. The AI also attached a link to sources. And all the sources say this is not the case. The word "порода" has the root пород, not род. What caused the model to err, having truthful sources at hand, is unclear.)

And most importantly. Trust in a book, website, device, or person is based on the belief that someone has verified what is said against real circumstances. A reporter spoke with witnesses, a researcher conducted an experiment, an engineer calibrated a device. People lie and make mistakes, but even when they lie, they appeal to something external. Neither the books from the hexagons nor the raw output of the model have ever been verified against circumstances.

OpenAI itself warned about this when announcing the research preview in November 2022. The system "sometimes produces plausible-sounding but incorrect or nonsensical answers," meaning it sometimes gives a plausible but incorrect or nonsensical answer. Fixing this is difficult because, with reinforcement learning, "there is currently no source of truth."

Dead Language

Here is a measurement from May 2025. Ahrefs analyzed 900,000 new English-language pages from April, one page per domain. AI traces were found on 74.2% of the pages. But if we break down the entire sample by type, only 2.5% were fully generated, 25.8% were fully human, and the remaining 71.7% were a mix where humans and models wrote together.

They also surveyed 879 content marketers, and 87% admitted to using AI in one way or another. The detector is not perfect; the authors themselves acknowledge this.

Other figures. Graphite in May 2026 took 55.4 thousand random addresses from Common Crawl, kept English articles and listicles with "article" markup no shorter than a hundred words, published from January 2020 to March 2026, ran them through three detectors (Pangram, Copyleaks, and GPTZero), and averaged the quarterly shares.

Their calculation method differs from Ahrefs. There, they looked for any machine trace on the page; here, they look for articles where the machine content is more than half.

Twenty-four months after ChatGPT's launch, the proportion of predominantly machine-generated articles reached 48 percent. In the fourth quarter of 2025, machine-generated articles surpassed human-written ones for the first time, at 50.9 percent. For the last five quarters, the line has held around half, meaning this is no longer acceleration but a plateau.

However, about half of the new English-language articles in their Common Crawl sample are not half of the internet. The difference is huge, and I wouldn't blur it.

Why is this bad? Well, besides having to read generated junk, it will also negatively affect the further training of models. By training on generated texts, AI triples the digestion of meanings and truth. And averaging of the already averaged occurs.

Shumailov and co-authors in Nature showed that indiscriminate training on generated text leads to an irreversible defect. If the original human data are not maintained, degradation accumulates from generation to generation. (Truly neural incest)

I will qualify this here as well. This is a result of recursive model training, not proof that the internet has already collapsed. The latter does not follow from the former.

But we can all engage our internal analysts and make our own small forecast.

The Shortage is Not of Text, but of Verification

If we put it all together, we get unpleasant arithmetic. The amount of text that looks like knowledge is increasing every year. The number of people who step out of the hexagon and verify what is written against the world is NOT increasing. Information is NOT being verified; everything is taken at face value.

This is why I am not ready to reduce it all to the advice to learn to read better. The skill of searching and verifying information existed before, but it used to rely on the web, where a source at least led somewhere. Soon, a link will lead to almost the same article, just written in different words, so truth is diluted in a stream of retellings.

Today, we ourselves are allowing and essentially building a web in which the very concept of truth will be gradually lost.

The librarians from the story had an excuse that we do not. Their library had existed since the beginning of time (a very, very long time ago); they were inhabitants of it, not authors. Ours is still being written, and effectively, no longer by us.

So the question is not whether to turn off AI or ban it from writing. The question is how to find truth in an ocean that imitates it.
