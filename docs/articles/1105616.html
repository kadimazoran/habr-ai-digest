Why Synthetic Data at All

At metallurgical enterprises, data exists, but it cannot be made publicly available: it's a trade secret. As a result, those who are learning or want to test an idea have nothing to train a model on and compare approaches. A synthetic dataset does not replace real data, but it allows debugging the entire pipeline: from generation and training to the interface, and then substituting real melts.

The task is as follows. When producing steel from a furnace into a ladle, the steelmaker adds ferroalloys to return the burned-out silicon and manganese within GOST limits. Part of the alloy burns up, and how much exactly is unknown in advance. Therefore, the dose is taken with a margin, and the margin costs money. We want to estimate the minimum dose that is sufficient, based on what is known before the addition.

What We Are Simulating

We are simulating the melting of a casting of group 1 from 35L steel according to GOST 977–88, weighing 3 tons, in a DSP-3 electric arc furnace with a basic lining. The raw materials are ferrous scrap of categories 2A and 6A according to GOST 2787–2019 and pig iron. Scrap 2A is lumpy, clean, heavy steel, the basis of the charge. 6A is briquettes of pressed steel shavings: cheap raw material, but with oil and scale, which is where sulfur comes from. Pig iron is needed as a carburizer because the scrap itself has about 0.15% carbon.

The charge is always exactly 3300 kg: an underloaded furnace is unprofitable. About 10% of the metal is lost to oxidation, and we get about 3000 kg of liquid steel at tapping.

Melting Stages

  • Charging. Weigh and load scrap 2A, briquettes 6A, and pig iron.

  • Melting. Arcs melt the charge; carbon content after melting is entirely determined by the charge.

  • Oxygen Blowing. Oxygen burns off excess carbon and removes gases. If overdone, the metal becomes over-oxidized.

  • Slag Formation. Lime provides basicity. High basicity binds sulfur and phosphorus; acidic slag (below 2.0) does not.

  • Tapping. The metal is poured into the ladle at the specified temperature, measured by an immersion thermocouple.

  • Ladle Alloying. Ferrosilicon FS75 and ferromanganese FMn78 are added. Part of the silicon and manganese is lost to oxidation, and the recovery rate depends on temperature, oxygen, and basicity.

  • Quality Control. Final composition and overheating are checked against tolerances.

Five Conditions for Suitability

A melt is considered suitable only if all five conditions are met simultaneously:

Condition

Tolerance

Carbon

0.32–0.40%

Silicon

0.20–0.52%

Manganese

0.45–0.90%

Sulfur + Phosphorus (sum)

not more than 0.040%

Superheat above liquidus

+45…+70 °C

If the superheat is less than 45 °C, the metal is too viscous, leading to underfilling during casting. If it's more than 70 °C, the metal will burn the mold, and the casting will be covered with gas pores. Note that this is a simplified model. In the GOST standard itself, sulfur and phosphorus are regulated separately, and the superheat window was invented by me for casting into XTS molds and is not taken from the standard.
