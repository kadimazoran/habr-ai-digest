Daniel J. Singer

Associate Professor at the University of Pennsylvania. Editor of The Philosopher's Annual. Studies the philosophy of work, management, and artificial intelligence.

This spring, arXiv became the latest in a line of key institutions to block AI slop—material showing signs of uncontrolled AI use. This reaction is understandable, as cheap synthetic text is now flooding not only research repositories but also traditional and social media, art, advertising, and even legal documents. But I am concerned not so much by the slop itself as by the use of the term. Sometimes we say 'AI slop' to denote a bad AI output. But we also say 'AI slop' to take a side in an important social discussion about the role of neural networks in society. We think it's just a mockery of one specific outcome, and this illusion allows us to avoid the important questions we need to discuss.

The first idea that the term 'AI slop' can express is isolation. In this usage, 'AI slop' is an unfortunate but not indicative exception. To call something 'AI slop' is to try to fence it off from all the good that AI can produce: useful translation, genuinely helpful code, or new mathematical discoveries.

This is precisely how Sam Altman used the term when OpenAI released Sora 2. Creativity, he wrote, can face a 'cambrian explosion,' and 'RLHF-tuned AI slop' is a degenerate case they tried hard to avoid. Google tried to take a similar stance when their AI search suggested using glue to keep cheese from sliding off pizza. The company stated that such results were due to unusual queries. The awkward case was swept under the rug to continue the rollout.

The second idea is contagion. In this application, 'AI slop' is not a defective instance but a revelation. A bad outcome shows what AI really is: a technology that is inherently derivative, frivolous, and hostile to the culture it feeds on.

A critic says: look at the synthetic children's books, the student essays no one wrote, and the apology letters no one stands behind. These are not unfortunate byproducts but perfect representatives of what AI can give us. This is precisely the argument Madsen and Puyt make, interpreting the flow of derivative, reprocessed output not as a defect to be eliminated but as the main essence of AI. And Charlie Warzel of The Atlantic responded similarly to the launch of Sora 2 three weeks later. 'AI slop is winning,' the note read, and what Altman described was called 'a world of creativity without craft.'

There is a grain of truth in both positions. The idea of isolation rightly reminds us that not every AI system and not every outcome is the same. The idea of contagion rightly notes that we need to be wary of AI slop, as the production of cheap, low-quality output is well-rewarded.

I am concerned not by these positions, but by the fact that the label term allows the speaker to avoid specifying what they mean. In the optimist's mind, it's 'just slop,' with emphasis on 'slop'; in the critic's mind, it's 'all slop,' with emphasis on 'AI.' More importantly, two people with different opinions on a serious social issue can disagree, believing they agree: after all, they have 'equally' categorized something as AI slop. And the real disagreement remains hidden behind different understandings of the term.

The trick is that 'AI slop' represents an evaluation of the outcome while simultaneously implying an evaluation of the technology itself—much like the accusation that text 'sounds like AI.' Beyond labeling the output as cheap and low-value, the term evokes disgust and prompts us to reject it (language philosophers would call this a 'thick' term that simultaneously describes and evaluates). This recoil can mean two different things. In the mouth of an optimist, it's a push to isolate the output from valuable AI work; in the mouth of a critic, it's a push to sink the entire enterprise. But disgust at a specific outcome is one thing, and a verdict on the entire category is another, and the term allows us to slide imperceptibly from the former to the latter.

This slide is a substitution, as slop existed long before AI. There were bad novels, formulaic movies, empty lectures, and academic reports that said nothing at length. But no one thought that bad novels spoke to the value of literature or that a worthless report determined whether universities needed researchers. This argument works both ways. The 'slop' coming out of our machines does not show that AI is useless—just as good results do not show that it is good overall. Therefore, when conversations about 'AI slop' look like discussions about AI in general, they are actually a fundamental view on the proper role of AI. It is this fundamental view that we need to discuss, but when we finally get to the conversation through 'AI slop,' it is usually put on hold.

I am not saying there is nothing unique about AI slop. AI is indeed changing the scale, speed, and cost of producing low-quality work that is difficult to recognize, profitable to distribute, and poisons the training data for future models. These are real concerns, but they relate to how the technology is designed, used, deployed, and applied. They do not prove its overall value or uselessness.

The natural opposite of low-quality work is quality, and often quality is the only subject of discussion. The question of whether a particular manuscript is slop is usually about whether it is good at all, and that may be enough for an editor. But the quality of a manuscript does not determine how we should live. The better AI becomes, the more tempting it is to think that quality decides something more. It does not. A machine can write a flawless condolence letter, but the question of whether such a letter should be written by a machine remains open. Quality answers the easy question and remains silent on the difficult one.

Instead of fighting 'AI slop,' it is time for us to ask the questions that the discussion of 'slop' allows us to postpone as if they were resolved—questions about what kind of society we should build and what place AI should have in it. When someone is sick, lonely, or scared, do we want a human being by their side, or will a bot suffice? Do we want our children to be primarily taught and raised by humans, or are we okay with entrusting this to machines? When a court decides who to believe, or an employer decides whom to hire, do we want a human behind the decision, or can a machine handle it? Do we want systems that increasingly govern our lives to be directed by a few private companies—or to be more broadly accountable? And do we want a shared world where we can figure things out together, or a world so flooded with synthetics that no one can distinguish reality from fiction? None of these questions can be answered by asking whether AI produces quality or low-quality work. They are about the shared life we want, and when we hide behind the label 'AI slop,' we are trying to avoid discussing these very questions.

I am not saying the term 'AI slop' should be banned. Sometimes we want to clearly name what should be thrown in the trash, and this term will suffice. But we must stop closing our eyes to the difficult social discussions that should be held. To start simply: when you feel like labeling something 'AI slop,' you can try to explicitly state whether you mean a verdict on a specific outcome or on the entire technology. And our institutions, by banning 'slop,' can state what exactly they are banning and what they are deciding.

Let's return to arXiv. To its credit, the rule introduced is narrower than headlines suggested. The chair of arXiv's computer science section clarified that the rule applies only to the quality of the submitted work: when the material shows 'irrefutable evidence that authors have not verified the outputs of LLM generation,' the conclusion is simple—'we cannot trust anything in the paper.' But in relation to my main thesis, the ban was read as a statement of position. One commentator welcomed it as a blow against the 'slop machine,' while another argued that generated text 'is not a problem in itself' and that the answer to the flood of AI slop could be 'more AI, not less.' So, while everyone can focus on quality, the term inadvertently touches upon larger issues and is used in two different ways, with the distinction never being made clear. This is precisely why it is important to state what you mean and do not mean by 'AI slop,' and when others use it, to clarify what they mean. This way, we will take steps toward deciding what part of our shared life we want to entrust to technology and what part we want to keep for ourselves.
