AI is making us more productive. What are we paying for it?

I am sure that each of you, one way or another, uses LLM models in your work today.

I do too.

It used to be a taboo of sorts, but today, when AI is sometimes smarter than some developers, it's considered good practice. And it's probably no secret that at interviews, they openly ask (as they do me) if you know and use agent-based development.

So far (and thank goodness), there is no right answer to this question. I have encountered both negativity when I spoke about cautious use of this approach, and full support for my opinion.

But, perhaps, few will dispute another opinion today: AI has become so good that using it is almost always rational.

The new development approaches, using sovereign models, that are being widely discussed and openly implemented by corporations are already our reality, although five years ago most would have simply called you crazy. And all this dictates to the majority: if you don't start using LLMs now, you might be left behind tomorrow, and the day after tomorrow, you'll be thrown by the wayside of history.

Therefore, today we are forced to witness a real explosion of projects, methodologies, articles, ideas, and everything else that was previously limited by a lack of knowledge, time, or technical capabilities.

Now that we understand that there's no escaping AI, I have the first question: if the result becomes better and faster, what exactly happens to the person who obtained it?

Where is the problem if the result is good?

Ask yourself: "If AI can do almost any intellectual work faster than I can, is it a blessing or a threat?" and listen to your inner self.

I don't know about you, but my inner self tenses up when searching for an answer to such a question.

Let me clarify. What can AI do today that is definitely better than I can?

Here are a few common examples:

  • Write code;

  • Compose an email;

  • Find information;

  • Plan a trip;

  • Explain a complex topic.

And in all cases, LLMs are clearly faster, more convenient, more accurate, and, importantly, much cheaper.

But here we are measuring the result of AI's work, not the value of the process for the person. We are measuring the efficiency of the tool, but we almost never measure what happens to the person's ability to do the same thing. We are simply blinded by the results, but we don't see how reality is changing.

The questions I am seeking answers to are: if AI constantly increases our efficiency, how do we know that along with efficiency, we are not losing the ability to act independently?

What will happen to a person if external intelligence becomes so convenient that the need to use one's own disappears?

Amplifier or Replacement

Let's imagine a banal situation: you are given a task, and AI does 90% of the work.

And here, in my opinion, is the key fork in the road for using and depending on AI:

  • AI as an amplifier: the person sets the task → forms the model → defines the limitations → uses AI → evaluates the result → verifies. Obviously, AI makes the person faster and more efficient. Here, the most important part is evaluating the result.

  • AI as a replacement: the person formulates a couple of sentences → gets the result → launches → it works. AI does not only the work but also processes the thinking required to perform that work.

And the most unpleasant thing is that the person will likely not even notice what is happening and where the line between these options lies.

Here's the conclusion I've reached: the percentage of work done by AI, in itself, says nothing about the degree of human dependence on it.

You can delegate 90% of a task to AI and still remain completely autonomous. Or you can delegate 10% to AI and already be dependent on it if those 10% are the most important and critical part of the thinking in the task. This is an important boundary that not everyone fully realizes.

I am sure, and many will probably agree with me, that a person who regularly uses AI to solve problems gradually blurs this line; they may lose the ability to independently assess the task, its complexity, and the final result. Personal expertise, sometimes unconsciously, can be replaced by the opinion of LLMs – partly because public pressure urges faster AI adoption before personal caution towards it can develop.

Is it necessary NOT to lose the ability?

Have you ever thought about how many life skills you have delegated to technology?

Once, as a child, I heated everything in a frying pan or an iron cup on an electric stove. I knew exactly how long to heat, how to stir, and what to do in general to make it edible.

Today, I have a microwave. I put it in, turn it, or press a button – 2 minutes, and it's generally ready to eat. Have I lost important skills?

Calculator, GPS, search – historically, humans have not lost anything catastrophically, although they regularly change tools. Is it really critical for a person to give up some part of their personal expertise, for example, in programming, in favor of process efficiency?

And here, in my opinion, it is very important to understand: there are tools that save effort. And there are those that eliminate the need for effort altogether.

The former are a normal technological process on which all modern progress is built. Writing in an IDE is certainly more convenient than in a notepad.

The latter can create unprecedented problems. Because the scale of delegation becomes fundamentally different.

The analogy with physical training works well here: if a mechanism does 99% of the work, you can indeed perform it faster. But it does not follow at all that your own ability to perform this work has become stronger.

But this does not mean that all work must now be done manually. If a person gains independence through a tool that they would not otherwise have had, it can be a great benefit.

A prosthesis itself is not a problem, and not all dependence on tools is bad. You don't have to remember all phone numbers, navigate by the stars, or know how your car engine works. You might not even know how to program all algorithms manually.

But can one afford not to be able to check the result, assess its plausibility, and formulate the conditions of the task itself?

And what will degrade first – the skill, motivation, confidence, or the very habit of solving a problem independently?

Can a person notice their dependence themselves?

For every quickly and efficiently completed task, the team lead most likely praises you at the retro. Says that you are extremely effective in conjunction with AI and everyone should look up to you.

All this positive reinforcement is just like Pavlov's dogs. And no one will notice or ask – how long has it been since you wrote code with your own hands? Business doesn't need it)

In fact, I am sometimes scared to read articles here, in the first paragraphs of which there are similar phrases – "I haven't written code with my own hands for a year" or "I delegated everything and accelerated my work hundreds of times." And God forbid they publish the code for study in the public domain (there are many simple errors).

In my humble opinion, a person who already depends on a tool may not have the ability to detect their own dependence.

Therefore, personally, I have introduced a very simple criterion for understanding how much I depend on AI – I do not pay attention to or evaluate myself on the question – how well do I work with AI?

I always ask myself – how well and quickly would I complete this task without AI.

And if I understand that the task presents no difficulty for me, that I clearly and fully understand how to do it – then, and only then, do I give it to AI. I am not dependent on AI for solving the task.

And from this, I will derive the main thesis: the main indicator of healthy AI use is not how well a person works with AI, but how well their ability to work without it is preserved.

In conclusion

I am not calling for not using AI. Only madmen would act like that in our time.

I have determined for myself that we are used to evaluating technologies by how much more they allow us to do. But this criterion is no longer sufficient. Whether we like it or not, we will have to learn to measure not only what a person can do with the help of a machine, but also what remains with the person when the machine is not around.
