Regardless of your opinion on AI-assisted programming, software development approaches are changing. Everyone is wondering: "What will remain important?" There are as many opinions as there are people, but here's mine: most ideas from Domain-Driven Design (DDD) are now more relevant than ever, as the DDD paradigm was never limited to code itself. The more agent-based programming develops, the better we need to understand the domain, model it well, and master teamwork. Let's talk about what DDD can still teach us that AI cannot replace.

The usual disclaimer: I'm writing about complex projects supported for months and years, about what teams struggle with, and where DDD shines. There's no need to use such advanced patterns in pet projects or CRUD.

What hasn't changed since 2003

Eric Evans published the book Domain‑Driven Design over twenty years ago, but it remains surprisingly fresh. The book covers many topics, but the main message is that in software engineering, it is particularly difficult to understand the domain and model it well in code. The book urges developers not to get too bogged down in technical details and to focus precisely on the problem being solved, as this is the hardest part to achieve.

Indeed, a talented engineer begins to build well-thought-out frameworks, trying to solve domain problems with technological means. The task of studying and modeling the domain is delegated to someone else. The inherent complexity of software is tackled head-on, because if you do otherwise, you risk doing something irrelevant.

Eric Evans, Domain‑Driven Design

Now we see that, thanks to AI tools, implementation details are no longer as important as they used to be. To some extent, one can remain productive without deeply understanding a specific language or framework, and switching to a different technology stack has become easier. If you've already used top-tier programming agents, you've probably seen how quickly they generate decent code.

Nevertheless, it is still important to know high-level software concepts to guide the agents' work and decide how meaningful their output is. In most cases, it is the complexity of the domain (the business problem you are working on) that causes the most problems. There's good news too – programming has become easier than ever, so now you can focus on solving these complex problems instead of getting lost in implementation details.

Furthermore, you no longer need to bother with building frameworks (or adding more microservices or following some other trendy pattern), but can simply track model benchmarks and optimize agent configurations to generate higher quality code – with less effort. As Evans noted back in 2003, we are still trying to find technologies to solve domain problems. It's quite amusing that nowadays CEOs also think this way and are driving companies in this direction.

Only one small detail remains to be decided: how should it work. If someone tells us about it, we can spend the required number of tokens on implementation – and that's that. And if there's no one else, maybe an agent can write the plan for us too?

The Domain Model Is Not an Artifact

The foundation of DDD is model-based design. You need to distill the principles of how the system works and formulate them as a model that can be described in code. A domain model is an abstract concept, so it can be represented in various ways. It can be in the form of documents, diagrams, and code, but all of these are simplifications that only give an idea of how the model works. To create an accurate model, you need to switch between design and implementation, and iterate, applying what you have learned.

In DDD, this is called "knowledge crunching." You will need domain experts (those who understand how the business works) and engineers who professionally build software. They work together to understand what the software should do and how to implement everything necessary. (For example, an Event Storming session is structured as a workshop where developers and decision-makers map out the business – using sticky notes is convenient for this.)

This outlining of the model is hard work, requiring the effort of the entire team, so you want to delegate it to someone. For instance, AI agents excel at research and analytics, can access your documents, chats, and code. It seems like it could create the domain model for you and then implement it as well.

But this naive approach misses the point. The value of developing a model lies in the fact that you (and your team) ultimately understand how the domain works. If AI generates a wall of text, it won't help you better define the business problem. The agent will simply create an impressive artifact for you that no one will read.

The task of writing a long document about how something might work has never been the key problem. Software projects fail much more often for other reasons:

1. Programmers develop the wrong thing.

2. Nobody knows what needs to be done first.

3. The team tries to do everything at once (scope creep).

This is a trap I've fallen into repeatedly. In engineering teams, it's easy to get the impression that we've already figured out all the details: we're expert programmers, we know our patterns, and we adhere to best practices. We just need someone (an interested business representative or product manager) to tell us what to do.

But often, domain experts also lack a clear plan. They know the problem they face but don't yet know how to solve it. They don't have a complete document describing all requirements. The developer's first response is: "Okay, let us know when you've formulated a proposal, and we'll be in touch." In other words, we'll get back to you when we can do our favorite thing – programming (note that this is precisely what AI agents automate).

There's another extreme: non-technical managers try to provide a complete solution and hand it over to the team for implementation. Or a new trend has emerged: someone uses AI to generate an entire feature or product specification, doesn't even read it, and passes it to the team. Thus, the plan seemingly exists, but it's nowhere near ready for production. (If anyone claims otherwise, check for yourself how complex the thing they think is ready is.)

In all these scenarios, no one clearly understands what exactly is being done. Regardless of who writes the code or how fast, the value of such code at this stage is minimal.

Conversely, when you work on the model together, your team delves into the domain, and decisions are made collaboratively. Developers handle implementation, and domain experts can step in if something unexpected happens or at the start of a new iteration.

Even if you generate most of the code using AI, you need to deeply understand the domain to guide the agent – just as you would guide a colleague. It's not as important how you configure the agents or which LLM you use – the key is to have a clear vision of what the solution will look like. If I had to choose, I'd rather stop writing code myself than stop thinking about what needs to be done.

Design First, Then Write Generate Code

Writing your own code has always been easier than reading someone else's. Today, things have fundamentally changed – an agent can deliver a large feature for you in one go. Even if it works, you don't understand what's happening under the hood.

We now find ourselves getting stuck more often during code reviews, but this isn't a new problem. There's even something familiar about dealing with a solo developer who prepares complete features for you and submits a huge pull request. You know nothing about how the system is designed, and there are often gaps in its structure that no one has considered. Reviewing a pull request is always painful.

But there's a way to avoid these situations, which I've seen work on many projects. You design the solution and discuss it as a team, and only then do you start working on the code. First, let's talk about the domain elements: what problem is being solved and how the system being created is supposed to work (see "knowledge crunching" above). This is the technical side. After you start the implementation, there's little chance of anything unexpected popping up.

You don't need to agree on every minute detail, nor do you need a fully written specification. It's enough to outline the ideas in general terms so that everyone is aware of the plan. Expressing ideas on sticky notes and a whiteboard is sufficient.

After a few such sessions, pull request reviews go smoothly. If you're not discussing the entire design in the comments of a pull request, the work can be easily divided into small chunks. Moreover, when the feature is in production, there will be fewer gaps because you've already agreed on the details, and everyone understands what exactly you're building.

It might seem like we're saving time by neglecting design. Nobody likes unnecessary meetings. But if your team learns about a new feature for the first time only from a pull request, it will take much more time to review, discuss, and fix all the issues. Since reviews are usually done asynchronously, there are many rounds of comments. Code review serves as an additional check and helps ensure the correctness of the implementation; it is not a discussion about whether the proposed approach is justified in principle.

Furthermore, by designing first and then writing code, you can work more effectively with AI agents. If you provide the agent with more high-quality material, its output will be more accurate, and the programming phase itself will be shorter. The more your team knows about the solution, the easier the review process will be, even if you generate all the code.

Finding Common Ground with Agents

Recently, we were trying to cut our cloud infrastructure costs. This is an ideal task for AI, so obviously, this metric needed to be measured. I instructed an agent to find where our Go service was consuming the most memory and to reduce it.

After a couple of runs, we managed to save a lot of megabytes of RAM. But then I realized that memory is very cheap, and the updates allowed us to reduce costs by $1/month. Not surprising, right? I didn't explain to the agent that I was trying to cut costs, but simply set memory usage as the target metric.

This probably sounds familiar, as it's exactly what happens during team communication. The practices recommended for team communication also work when interacting with AI. Who would have thought that soft skills would ever be useful when dealing with computers?

DDD offers several ideas related to language to address this problem.

First, a Ubiquitous Language is established: that is, a single set of concepts must be used across different teams and in the code, so that everyone understands what is being discussed.

In many teams, confusing names are the norm. In code, developers use technical terms whose meanings gradually diverge from the meanings of terms used in other departments of the company. Even if you feed the AI documents and decision notes, the agent will still have to make sense of the language you use.

When crunching knowledge, consider what you are classifying as domain concepts. It's common for the same entity to have different names. Choose a name you already use, or find a more precise one. Then use these names in documents, spoken language, and code.

You can't choose names once and for all, as the project's language evolves with the project itself. Language needs to be addressed during design sessions and discussions.

There's another idea directly related to the ubiquitous language: in large systems, the names you choose will be universal across all domains. In DDD, each of these domains is called a Bounded Context. You can view your client as a profile in the support context and as a customer in the e-commerce context. Within each context, all names must be mutually consistent, but they don't need to be imposed across the entire project.

This is especially important when working with relatively large products, as the software has to be distributed among teams. If you focus too much on creating a single model for very different tasks, it becomes difficult to differentiate them. However, thinking in terms of bounded contexts makes it easier to create a loosely coupled system.

Agents have access to your entire repository and look for the exact names you provide. They might naively try to unify similar entities, so explain to the agent why certain entities should be treated separately.

If you use well-known and clearly defined concepts, it will be easier for you to express what you mean. This rule applies both when interacting with colleagues and when formulating prompts.

Given a vague prompt, an agent might spend too many resources on the wrong solution:

Create a user, add them to CRM and support

You can formulate the goal more clearly using precise names:

As soon as a user logs into the site with their username and password, we will asynchronously create: 1) a user account in the CRM, 2) a user profile in the support system.

The details of your domain language need to be incorporated into the context of your agents. Here's another reason to keep your documentation up-to-date. It's good to start by placing markdown files close to the code, as no special integration is needed to extract information from these documents.

Developers Become Domain Experts

Models and agents are constantly improving, but they cannot be fully trusted. They still make mistakes, and a human is needed to be fully responsible for the project. How can you ensure that the agent's work is correct?

Trust and the conviction that you understand what you are doing are built through immersion in the domain. Those who design the system and understand how it works can tell you if any part of it is malfunctioning.

Developers become experts in the subject they are working on. They acquire skills beyond just writing code, and a curious engineer will gradually intuitively understand even a complex business domain. If you need help in this area, you know who to turn to. You will save yourself a lot of trouble by working with colleagues you can trust, knowing they will help see things through without requiring detailed instructions.

Two narratives are currently developing in the AI field. The first is that you can now clone a well-known application. This was shocking at first, but in the long run, it turned out to be not very interesting. We already have enough half-baked applications even without AI intervention.

It's much more interesting to talk about how experienced engineers can now create increasingly complex applications. For example, a team might replace expensive third-party software with their own alternative. But how confident can we be that such a homegrown version will work well?

We can be, because these developers have already acquired the necessary knowledge by working in that domain. They combine their accumulated knowledge with AI and create their own version of the required program so quickly that it turns out to be cheaper to write it than to pay for a license. Moreover, any specialized features needed in this case can be added to the new program.

It is also very beneficial for employers to hire engineers who are familiar with the product and know the domain well. Such people can clarify unclear details and steer discussions in the right direction. It has always been frivolous to assume that developers are people who will just program what you ask them to, and even more so now.

The desire to understand a previously unknown domain is more beneficial to a developer than focusing solely on developing technical skills. It will be easier to work and profit if you understand business problems, rather than just mastering one framework perfectly.

Don't Let Them Think For You

The DDD methodology demonstrates how much software development is not just about writing code. If you've worked with complex systems, I'm sure you've realized this many times.

This topic reminded me of my first IT job. I was once working on a complex problem, spending hours with a pencil in hand. Finally, I found a solution, having barely touched the keyboard. At the time, I was a junior developer, so I was speechless: "Wow, they're paying me to think."
