Part 1 – Project Kick-off

I was hesitant to launch Astra. Not for lack of time, but because I knew once I started, it would pull me into an abyss. You ask one question, then another, 'Can this be done?' and the model delivers something that makes it impossible to stop, with the clock showing midnight and work in the morning. So, I waited. I also wanted an idea and curiosity to ignite in my mind, something that couldn't be contained. So, when I felt it on Friday afternoon, I decided it was time. Exactly at five, I closed my work laptop, turned to my home Mac, placed my iPad with ChatGPT to the right, and a large glass of water to the left. The task ahead was not trivial. Let's even say it was crazy – we decided to programmatically access the data from Euclid (Euclid) – the new telescope hanging a million and a half kilometers from Earth at the L2 Lagrange point, and process its data through a new GPT 6 model – Astra Extra High, and…

That 'and' was the main question.

Alright, to be honest, we're not accessing the telescope itself, but the data in tables, chilling somewhere on servers. But that doesn't change the essence.

I learned about Euclid recently; well, it was a series of malfunctions at different observatories. It all started a year ago when 3I/ATLAS broke down. I wanted to see it myself in the center of the frame with a amateur telescope, which required me to figure out and connect to an online observatory with access to robotic telescopes. Needless to say, at that point, I didn't even know how to calculate coordinates for an observation mission.

I thought the obstacles would stop me halfway, as they usually had before. But no, my AI assistant, which at the time was GPT 5, constantly encouraged me, saying, 'Let's just analyze the telescope files today and understand what's in them, and tomorrow we'll get to the analysis code.' You could say it didn't let me give up, and after 2 months, I captured the first spectrum of a comet and processed it through my own Python pipeline. A few months later, when the interstellar object disappeared from view, I wondered – what's next? Maybe capture the spectrum of an interesting star? And at that moment, the only spectrograph in my observatory broke down, and tech support wrote that repairs would take about a month.

I looked around and went to examine the alerts from the Vera C. Rubin Observatory. There, every night, you could download public data to your home computer through a broker and catch asteroids, comets, and even interstellar objects. For this purpose, we also built our own Python pipeline. The only difference was that in November 2025, our team consisted of two people: myself and ChatGPT, but in the spring of 2026, the Codex agent joined us, and things started moving faster. By June, the pipeline was not just ready but could extract fast-moving Solar System objects from millions of incomprehensible signals based on speed, magnitude, morphology, and overall 'cometary' score.

When on June 30, 2026, the observatory announced the official launch of the main observation window, I cast my net for a big fish and waited. And… two weeks later, on July 14, a severe snowstorm, the worst in 50 years, hit the observatory area. Its consequences destroyed roads and part of the infrastructure, and the observatory still hasn't recovered. I waited for several weeks, reading news updates from the observatory director, but the phrases were disheartening: by August, they reached the summit and launched the generators; in September, they were waiting for equipment vendors but still didn't launch the telescope. That is, work was ongoing, but there was no end in sight, and I started looking around again.

In one of the videos, I heard astrophysicist Boris Stern talk about Euclid and its publicly available data. And this was a different scale. This is also big data, but of a different order. With Rubin, you get a million alerts per night, but you filter out almost all of them as unnecessary. With Euclid – millions of galaxies, but what to do with them?

The problem is also that between the phrase 'telescope data is public and available to everyone' and actual use, there is a chasm, and no one has built a bridge. You have to build it yourself.

Part 2 – Launching Astra

I mean, when you dive into big data, it's almost impossible to keep its structure in your head. You either have to constantly refer to the documentation or figure it out anew each time, which leads to extra errors and incorrect assumptions. I didn't want to go through that again. So, at a joint AI council, we decided that the role distribution would be strict, and each would have their own area of responsibility:

Part 3 – Hydrogen-alpha: Scientific Discovery or Beautiful Ghost?

At this moment, I felt like the protagonist of Keith Laumer's story 'The Matter of Matter,' which I happened to be listening to a few weeks before our experiment. In it, two pilots are testing a spaceship with a new faster-than-light engine. The first incredible jump is successful, and then a completely human thought arises: what if we pull the lever again?

We pulled it too. Only instead of exceeding the boundaries of spacetime, we flew beyond the spectral range where the Euclid telescope could show us the hydrogen-alpha line shifted into the red part of the spectrum. Here's why.

Galaxies have their own marker of active star formation – hydrogen-alpha. Just as humans have demographics, and we build a graph where dips or peaks can indirectly indicate global events, galaxies do too: for example, two galaxies collide, gas compresses, and intense star birth begins – and the bright hydrogen-alpha line literally screams about it in the spectrum. Of course, Hα itself doesn't always mean star formation: it can be amplified by, for example, an active galactic nucleus or shock waves. But as a marker of recent star formation, it is widely used in astronomy.

Scientists, of course, have long known about these processes. But what if we measure this marker on the timeline of millions of galaxies – what is its 'demography' over a long period of the Universe's life? This is precisely the scientific question we asked. And the redshift filter is our time machine. It allows us to cut out a thin temporal 'pancake' and select galaxies from that epoch.

In other words, I want to measure the proportion of galaxies with intense star formation using the hydrogen-alpha line marker in different time epochs, determined by redshift, and compare these proportions – to build a graph of this dynamics.

So, inspired by the first result with redshift z=1.27, we decided to jump even further back in time and set the galaxy selection filter to redshift z=1.8, which means the light traveled to us for about 10 billion years.

To save tokens, we downgraded the model to Sol; Astra completed the main task, and now it was time for statistics. We outputted data for two sets: galaxies with z=1.27 and galaxies with z=1.8.

The data looked similar, as if the Universe was laughing at us: 'So what do you hope to see here? On large scales, I am very homogeneous.' Despite the fact that after filtering, the sample decreased by 100 times, the distribution of galaxies with a bright hydrogen-alpha line repeated the geometry of the full sample. It's like scooping a teaspoon of water from three distant points in the World Ocean and being surprised that the salt percentage is almost the same.

But there was something unusual.

If at redshift z=1.27 the number of galaxies with a strong and reliable hydrogen-alpha signal was about 1% of the total, then in a slightly earlier epoch of the Universe, galaxies with z=1.8 had almost double that figure! Wow, why? We needed more points on the timeline, and we jumped closer to our time, setting z=1.0. Actually, we first 'turned the knob' to 0.5, but a mishap occurred – galaxies passing our hydrogen-alpha filter disappeared to zero. We started investigating and found out that Euclid's infrared spectrograph reliably sees lines approximately between 1250 nm and 1850 nm. The laboratory value for hydrogen-alpha is Hα = 656.3 nm; at z = 0.5 → it shifts to approximately 984 nm, which is outside the range of Euclid's infrared spectrograph!

Therefore, we had to back up, add other valid points on the timeline, and build a graph that showed a strange picture: the proportion of galaxies passing the same strict Hα selection first noticeably dropped, and then rose again as we moved towards the earlier Universe.

I asked the AI to check scientific sources and received amazing news. It turned out that the population of Hα-emitting galaxies does indeed change depending on the epoch of the Universe we are looking at, and this is a subject of scientific research. But we had one 'but.' In our graph, it wasn't a uniform increase in emission as the Universe aged. It was more like a bowl, and when we increased the step and built a graph from 21 time epochs, it became a 'ghost flying on the wings of night,' with surprising symmetry over a huge dataset: 401,874 galaxies participated in the calculation, and 5,402 of them had a bright hydrogen-alpha line:

Part 4 – Reaching for the Heavens: How to Compare Galaxies from Different Epochs?

I had no doubts about Euclid's data: the telescope's settings, raw data, astronomical pipeline that creates calibrated scientific products from the data – all developed by big science and available for analysis. I didn't even doubt my AI agent, although I did manual spot checks afterward. I doubted my own approach: is it correct to select all galaxies from one epoch through redshift and assume that Euclid has shown me 'everything in the universe' at that distance? No, of course not, because the farther away, the weaker the light, and thus the telescope's sensitivity. So, how to make a selection? The AI suggested – by limiting luminosity or galaxy mass. But I hesitated, unable to mentally agree with the assumption that my approach might initially take incorrect data for calculation.

Therefore, after consulting within our small team again, we decided to seek advice and asked astrophysicist Boris Stern the question, and simultaneously launched research into scientific papers.

As a result, it first turned out that our temporal 'pancake' needed to be trimmed by mass so that comparable mass samples of galaxies could be compared at different distances. But then it turned out that this was not enough: it also needed to be trimmed from the spectroscopy side – to ensure that at different redshifts, a comparable proportion of such galaxies falls into the sample with measured redshift.

We gave Astra a new task, and it began analyzing Euclid data under the new approach and returned to us with disappointing news after a few minutes. Galaxy masses are available in the data, but we couldn't find a justified solution on how to select comparable mass samples of galaxies for different epochs while considering that Euclid doesn't obtain reliable spectra for all galaxies equally well.

We hit a methodological wall, and I wrote an email to the Euclid team with one simple question: is it even possible to correctly perform our calculation with the current release, or do we need to wait for the new release (Data Release 1 Foundation) in November 2026? A few minutes later, I received an automatic reply with a Jira ticket number on the Euclid team's dashboard.

Satisfied, I closed my laptop and went for a bike ride.

And Finally. Euclid's Smoke Test on the Oxygen Doublet

And so, I'm riding and thinking: why did we even tie ourselves to redshift and hydrogen-alpha? Don't we have other tasks?

For example, Vera Rubin, working with data from just one galaxy, Andromeda, discovered that stars on its outskirts move much faster than expected based on the distribution of visible mass. This became one of the key steps in the history of dark matter – the hypothesis of invisible matter forming a massive halo around the galaxy and explaining such stellar motion.

And what prevents us from probing dark matter? We have half a million galaxies in Euclid's tables – take and calculate.

The next morning, Astra received a new assignment and brought me back down to earth, saying that this time too, Euclid's data would not be enough for a full experiment. At the same time, sensing my downcast mood, the model offered a compromise: 'There is one simple and clean experiment,' it said, 'that will allow us to verify the selected Euclid spectra, and thus, in general, give us more confidence that we are correctly analyzing the telescope's data.'

And we found the oxygen doublet in the selected spectra – two oxygen lines – and calculated the ratio of the intensities (brightness) of these lines. The value of this ratio is well-known from atomic physics. Therefore, such a calculation is like a smoke test for the spectrum of a distant galaxy and, at the same time, a local check of Euclid's data.

The idea is beautiful and interesting, but it deserves a separate article, especially since we are 'playing solitaire' for confidence, or rather, using the Monte Carlo method. Therefore, I will try to briefly explain the essence, and if someone wants to repeat it – the pipeline is at the link below.

In the ionized gas of a distant galaxy, for example, in the star-forming regions around hot young stars, oxygen atoms can lose two electrons. Such oxygen is called doubly ionized and is denoted as O III. Collision with a free electron can move one of the remaining O III electrons to a higher energy level – the ion becomes excited. Returning from this excited state, it can emit a photon at a wavelength of 500.7 nanometers or undergo another transition, emitting a photon at a wavelength of 495.9 nanometers.

Both of these lines arise from the same upper energy level, but the probabilities of the two transitions differ. From atomic physics, the ratio of the intensities of the [O III] 5007/4959 lines should be about 2.98.

Accordingly, when Euclid captures a galaxy's spectrum, these two lines produce two peaks in the spectrum. But we compare not just their height, but the measured flux in each line – essentially the area under the corresponding peak. If the ratio of fluxes is close to 2.98 within the error margin, the spectrum passes this local smoke test. If the ratio differs significantly, we need to figure out what happened: the cause could be noise, peculiarities in line fitting, contamination from neighboring sources, saturation, or incorrect calibration.

And this is where the Monte Carlo method came in handy: not to obtain the value of 2.98 itself, but to estimate the uncertainty of our measurement on a real noisy spectrum.

And then, step by step:

  1. Take a galaxy by object_id, look at its spectrum – two oxygen peaks are in the expected positions considering the redshift – done

  2. Calculate the intensity of both lines – done

  3. Find their ratio and compare it with the prediction from physics – done

The theoretical value is 2.98, and my selected galaxy gave 3.07 ± 0.37.
