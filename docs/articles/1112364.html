Why Persson's Case Isn't Just Another Corporate Bullshit

When some corporate CTO talks from the stage about how AI agents have accelerated their development by 40%, your hand instinctively reaches out to check their measurement methodology, quarterly reports, and plans for developer layoffs.

Markus Persson is 47 years old. In 2009, he started coding Minecraft on the fly, and in 2014, he sold his company Mojang to Microsoft for $2.5 billion and retired. In 2024, bored in retirement, he founded the studio Bitshift Entertainment and started working on Levers and Chests – an old-school first-person voxel roguelike.

Notch has what's called fuck-you money. He doesn't need to report to investors, inflate startup capitalization, or meet KPIs for LLM implementation. He codes simply because he enjoys the process. And if a tool is garbage, Markus will gladly send it packing. So it's all the more interesting to observe how exactly this man got hooked on vibe coding.

Stage 1. Denial: "Programming is about logic, not typing"

In early 2026, Notch sounded like the most upvoted commentator in Habr threads about AI.

On January 26, he harshly criticized neural network code generation, calling proponents of this approach "incompetent or evil." In February, Fortune magazine dedicated a separate article to his critique. Markus's argument can be chiseled in stone:

"It's about logic, not about typing."

Persson then compared handing over code writing to AI to handing over the writing of a country's laws to a neural network. The thesis is perfectly understandable to any normal engineer: source code is a strict formal description of a system's behavior, not just "lots of text" whose generation needs to be cheaper and faster. Quickly generating spaghetti code and quickly solving an architectural problem are orthogonal concepts.

By April, his skepticism turned into outright disgust: the account of Mojang co-founder Jakob Porser was hacked, and hackers started posting odes to AI coding from it. For Persson, this became the final proof that all the hype around AI was an artificially inflated scam. The culmination was the two-word manifesto on July 14: "Reject AI."

The fall from grace was exactly one week away.

Stage 2. Bargaining: "I'll just write an IDE plugin, honestly"

On July 21, Persson suddenly announces that he is going to poke this vibe coding thing with a stick. The justifications sound maximally pragmatic:

Hiring skilled programmers is difficult, and closing a tab with a dumb chatbot is psychologically much easier than firing a living person. There's a ton of tedious routine in gamedev: map editors, graph visualizers, and other tools that make your teeth ache.

Under pressure from advice in the replies, Notch admits: he was afraid of becoming a grumpy Luddite and decided to give the technology a chance. But he promised to keep grumbling even if the neural network turned out to be useful.

The chronicle of further "fall" is well-documented in materials from Business Insider and Regen Raygun:

Late July. Notch feeds Claude routine tasks (e.g., translating from TypeScript to JavaScript) and cautiously asks his followers: can he continue to mock vibe coders if he has become one himself? It immediately turns out a piquant detail: he had long suspected that AI could be useful, but stubbornly suppressed these heretical thoughts.

August 10. Publishes a screenshot of a conversation with the bot and admits that it's time to take back his words. To fans' reproaches ("Using AI to create games is depressing"), he responds like a seasoned senior: I won't betray fundamental principles, but ignoring a powerful tool is foolish.

Mid-August. First real profit. Persson is coding in WGSL (WebGPU Shading Language), he's annoyed by the buggy syntax highlighting in WebStorm, but he's too lazy to write his own plugin. The AI rolls out a working plugin on the first try.

And right away, Markus stumbles upon the main psychological trap of the technology. On August 26, in a public conversation with Jonathan Blow (creator of Braid and The Witness – another legendary gamedev hardcore enthusiast), Notch articulates the main fear of a modern developer:

"I'd prefer to stay a developer and not become middle management".

The problem isn't even the code quality. The problem is that you transform from a creator and engineer into a wrangler of silicon juniors, whose day consists of assigning tasks and endless code reviews.

But the drug had already taken effect. On September 22, his feed features a candid admission: "I admit, I like vibe coding. Maybe I was a little wrong."

You'd think we're done here? No. The real horror began in November.

Stage 3. Eternal Game Jam and Battle of Egos: Notch vs. Claude

To understand what happened in November, you need to know Persson's character.

On November 17, he recalls an old interview with Jens Bergensten (Jeb), who complained that working with Notch was like a "continuous game jam" and it was impossible to work normally in such chaos. Well, Persson is proud of this style! He still codes like he's at a hackathon, not for money, but for pure fun (Blow in the comments immediately suggested renting a house, locking themselves in with a couple of coders, ordering a mountain of pizza, and coding projects all night long).

And on November 19, Markus tells an illustrative story from his youth – how he got a job as a hired coder at the AAA studio Avalanche Studios and lasted there for exactly two weeks. The reason? A purely technical dispute: the leads demanded that the character's movement speed be tied to the animation, while Notch argued that this was nonsense and the animation should depend on the speed. As a result, he handed in his resignation. – Do you really think you can make games better than us? – asked the management. – Yes, – replied Persson. The thread ends with the legendary punchline: "And then I made Minecraft."

And now, watch closely! The next day, November 20, the same story repeats, but instead of a stupid boss – a neural network!

Notch is no longer using AI to write boring plugins. He's discussing hardcore math with Claude: ray marching algorithms for sparse voxel octrees (SVO) and decoding rotation matrices. At some point, after a series of experiments, Persson decides to roll back to an old feature, and Claude suddenly takes a stand and states unequivocally: "This is impossible to implement."

Notch's reaction is priceless: he points out to the model that the "impossible" code has already been written by him personally and is working perfectly right now. Sound familiar? Yesterday you were arguing with the team lead that he didn't understand the architecture, and today you're having the exact same argument with a piece of matrices on Anthropic's servers, which sometimes flatters you ("Oh, your matrix solution is so elegant!") and sometimes confidently hallucinates in your face.

But on the same day, Notch posts a video that makes skeptics uncomfortable. He asked Claude to rewrite his old game engine from scratch, simply describing the old functionality in words and adding wishes that he was too lazy to code manually before. The video shows a full 3D voxel space, octrees, Sierpinski pyramids, dynamic voxel objects (swaying trees, rotating windmills, city fires) tied to spatial grids with side lengths of 2ⁿ. Yes, he already had .vox format import ready, and yes, the video is full of visual artifacts. But the fact remains: in three months, the man went from "convert this script snippet for me" to "rewrite the core of my custom voxel engine for me."

Stage 4. Withdrawal: "Give me more tokens!"

On November 23, the breakdown happens. A billionaire capable of buying his own data center hits the standard API limits and goes begging on Twitter (which was immediately reported by the world media). He swears allegiance to the company's ideals (apparently hinting at Claude's Constitution) and asks for only one thing – to remove the speed and token limits.

And on November 25, he delivers a final double-whammy that puts an end to the "AI replaced programmers" theory:

First, he boasts that he vibe-coded a cool utility for easy work with WebGPU and is ready to give it away for free. And exactly 80 minutes later, he posts a call: looking for skilled specialists with a fundamental Computer Science background, wants to code together, paying well above market rate.

That is, after squeezing all the juice out of Claude and hitting the limits, he still went looking for live people.

What's the Bottom Line? (Instead of a Moral)

It's easy to laugh at how quickly Notch's crusade against neural networks fizzled out. But if you dig deeper, Markus Persson hasn't changed his January thesis: programming is still about logic, not typing.

It's just that in July, he realized that "typing" could be delegated without losing control over the logic.

Why did he succeed? Because he has decades of experience behind him. When Claude stated that the ray marching algorithm was "impossible," a junior vibe coder would have obediently gone to change the game concept. But Persson laughed in the machine's face because he knew exactly how his code worked under the hood.

For a person with Notch's character – a brilliant, difficult individualist who hates corporate processes and works in eternal game jam mode – AI became the ideal sparring partner. The bot doesn't whine about overtime, doesn't argue about what to tie the animation to (and if it argues – it can be silenced with a single prompt), and allows one person to move mountains that previously required a department of programmers.

Has Persson turned into the very "middle manager for bots" he was so afraid of becoming in August? Or has he found that balance where AI acts as an exoskeleton for a true engineer?

Judging by the fact that he's once again looking for live CS specialists for above-market salaries – the neural network couldn't replace a human. But it could replace routine.

P.S. I suspect that the most surprised in this whole story, of course, was Anthropic itself. Just imagine the faces of their engineers and PR people on November 23: the guy who, just in the summer, got 5 million views with the slogan "Reject AI" and called you evil, suddenly bursts into the replies, flashes his billions, and begs you to increase his API limits because he "ran out of tokens at the most interesting part."
