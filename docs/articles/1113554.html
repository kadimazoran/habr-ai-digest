Google AI Overviews: Speech, Subtitle Errors, and the Importance of the First 15 Seconds

In October 2026, three studies on YouTube in AI responses were released. According to Tenpoint Labs, YouTube appears in 31% of Google AI Overviews among its own sources and in 35% when a separate video block is included.

In a test by Visilay, 24 active queries for the Italian market were processed: Google showed sources for 17 overviews – 52 YouTube links out of 170 total Google links, compared to one out of 152 in ChatGPT. All 52 excerpts contained spoken words in lowercase, with incorrect punctuation and errors from auto-generated subtitles: 'lean production' became 'Liam production,' and 'ESG report' became 'bilancio ISG,' and Google quoted these verbatim.

Visilay found timestamps in 25 out of 52 links (none among the 11 Shorts citations), with 14 out of 25 leading to the first 15 seconds. A large-scale study by Foundation confirmed the trend: 48.4% of Google's YouTube links (50.4% in AI Mode and 46.2% in AI Overviews) include a timestamp, while none were recorded in ChatGPT.

The main surprise from Foundation concerns manual subtitles. Subtitles were present for almost all videos, but manually uploaded ones appeared slightly less frequently (14.0%) in the top 150 cited videos than among videos with a single citation (16.6%). The data showed no advantage from manual uploading.

Checking My Archival Runs of ChatGPT and Perplexity

External measurements show the proportion of links in neural network responses, but after running them, you rarely check them manually – where exactly in the video is the phrase from the neural network's response located. I reviewed my archival run on AI promotion and application: 225 error-free responses across five channels (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Perplexity, and Yandex with neural response) – 45 responses each, 15 questions in three repetitions.

Searching for watch, Shorts, and youtu.be addresses in the response text yielded 20 hits, all in ChatGPT – these were eight different videos. Upon manual verification, none of the links were adjacent to a number or a thesis; all 20 were placed at the end as a separate recommendation, like "watch the video."

When asked about practical examples of neural networks for business in 2026, ChatGPT indicated a 35% conversion increase with a link to a third-party site, and at the bottom, it recommended the video Best Neural Networks of 2026 for Work and Your Business / My 5 Favorite AI for Daily Tasks. Searching for '35%' in the video would be pointless. Considering four points: the title mentions five favorite AIs, the description has seven connections and seven chapters (counting units differ, no contradiction), the intro 0:00–0:22 promises examples without the number five, on the open pages below the video (business trips and brief format) there is no list, and another link leads to a closed Telegram bot.

Since all ChatGPT links in this run were just recommendations to watch the video, I looked into another old measurement – I wanted to find at least one example where a neural network cited a video for a specific reason. I found one such response from Perplexity to a question about a content factory for an online store:

For marketplaces: Watch the YouTube guide strategies for WB/Ozon — lookbook, Reels, trends.

Here, there's a specific promise: the video should include an analysis of Lookbook for marketplaces. I opened the video and checked the same four points. The title was general – just about seven strategies. The description contained the relevant chapter Lookbook conveyor at 19:06. But when I turned on the sound at that moment, the author uttered the desired phrase slightly later, at 19:11: "The first is the lookbook conveyor, video plus photo." And the link below the video to the mind map again led to a Telegram bot without open text.

In both examples, the picture is similar: the title doesn't contain a precise answer, the links below the video lead to closed bots, and the desired phrase is in the description and in the speech itself.

Why Speech Coincidence Doesn't Prove Where the AI Got the Fact

When you see an excerpt in Google with an auto-subtitle error, you immediately want to say: it's clear, neural networks just read the subtitles. But there's an important caveat here.

Researchers Wallat and co-authors directly distinguish between two things: whether the link confirms the phrase and whether the model actually relied on that source when writing the response. It's not uncommon for a neural network to formulate an answer first and then find a suitable link. Confirming a fact in a video and the method by which the neural network obtained that fact are different questions. Coincidence with speech, description, or a related page allows verification of the statement, but in itself does not prove the path of information acquisition.

What We Ultimately Told the Client

After this analysis, we adjusted our answer to the client – resulting in four simple rules instead of general advice:

  • State the main answer aloud in the first 15 seconds and at the beginning of each chapter. In Google AI Overviews, excerpts come from speech, and more than half of Visilay's timestamps led to the beginning of the video. A long introduction simply eats up the main slot.

  • Don't spend budget on manual subtitles for the sake of increased citations, but correct brand names and terms. Foundation's study showed no advantage of manual subtitles in the top 150, but Visilay demonstrated how Google verbatim pulls auto-subtitle errors into its responses.

  • Create clear titles and chapters in the description. ChatGPT records no timestamps (and in our run, the videos were generally recommended at the end), and in our Perplexity example, the link led to the entire video. Clear titles and chapters help the video at least get into the right topic.

  • Don't hide the only text version behind a Telegram bot. In both our examples, useful materials below the video were hidden behind bots – neither a registered reader nor search engines could see them.

Our own figures are based on just two archival runs, but together with the October Google measurements, they clearly show where it's really worth investing effort in a video and what can be skipped.
