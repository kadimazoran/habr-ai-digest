Introductory Part

Imagine a situation: you have received data for a small study - say, about a hundred patients and a dozen biochemical indicators. You know for sure that the patients are divided into two classes - healthy and sick, but in the graph, these classes are heavily mixed. The first thing most people will advise is: take logistic regression, build a straight boundary - and you're done. You try, but the line simply doesn't separate your two classes, no matter how you rotate it.

Okay, a straight line didn't work - let's try something more flexible. We take a polynomial function - a little better, but still there are points that stubbornly remain on the wrong side. We try a sigmoid - and get even worse than with a regular straight line. It would be logical to complicate the model even further, but there is so little data that a neural network with its millions of parameters is out of the question - it will simply memorize these points by heart and make mistakes on new ones.

Here there are two problems at once: the classes cannot be separated by a straight line (non-linearity), and there is too little data to train something complex (data scarcity). Most methods can only solve one of them at the expense of the other - either flexibility or resistance to overfitting, rarely both at once.
