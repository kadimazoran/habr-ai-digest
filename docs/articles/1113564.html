The hype around "AI transformation" will collapse when businesses stop paying for buzzwords. The quality of models has nothing to do with it. What will remain is a specific process with pain points and metrics, and the word "AI" in its description will become optional.

What is already visible

"Vedomosti" published a survey of 50 of the largest Russian organizations (December 2025 – February 2026). Only 7 –10% of pilot projects with LLMs, chatbots, and agents reached industrial operation. About 30 –40% of pilots were shut down: they did not deliver the expected financial effect. The main reason cited was poor integration into processes.

Gartner predicts that over 40% of agent projects will be canceled by the end of 2027. This is still a forecast; we will be able to verify it in a year.

In a randomized trial by METR, experienced developers using AI spent 19% more time on a task and, at the same time, believed they had sped up by 20%. This was in early 2025, with 16 people and 246 tasks; tools have changed since then, and METR itself marks this data as outdated.

Where the imbalance comes from

The same result can be described in two ways. "A script parses requests, checks rules, and creates orders." Or "An AI agent independently analyzes requests, makes decisions, and manages the process." The latter is sold as an autonomous digital employee and costs more. Internally, it's often the same process: get data, check conditions, perform actions, record results.

It seems to me that the industry has confused the model's ability to reason with the necessity of reasoning at every step. This imbalance will be corrected.

What will remain

"An order gets lost between sales and production" sounds like a task for AI transformation. In reality, it's a lost order. First, map the process, interview the performers, export from the accounting system, and reconcile the results. The model, perhaps, comes later.

Tokens should be spent where language is needed: to parse technical specifications or ambiguous documents, to write code. Where repeatability is needed, they are superfluous.

An "agent" is a wrapper. The model is getting cheaper and better; the value remains in the environment around it: rules, tests, logs, and rollback.

Our experience

I am building an application constructor for Integram and projects on it, using only AI for writing code. For example, a cutting scheduler for a factory was written by an agent based on technical specifications with numbered rules; tests refer to rule numbers. The planning itself is done by regular code; the model is not involved in building the plan. The order of cuts within a day is found by exact enumeration, and where enumeration doesn't work, greedy steps are used. Tokens cost approximately $1.5–3 thousand at API rates, once.

Every action that changes the plan is written to the log by the scheduler. From August 6 to November 27, three dispatchers made 3,148 such actions over 80 working days: an average of 39 per day (median 29, in November 56). About 25 of these are initiated by the dispatcher with a button press; the rest are reconciled by the scheduler itself upon opening. Forecast

I bet that by the end of 2027, there will be more agents that write deterministic code once than agents that call a model at every step of a calculation task. If most agents sold for calculation tasks still charge per call for every request, then I am wrong, and I am seeing an incomplete picture right now.

If you answer the poll at the bottom of this post, we can estimate the size of segments who think the same or differently, and perhaps even influence the outcome of the AI agents versus code battle that is objectively already unfolding.
