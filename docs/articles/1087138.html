The Man Who Was Asked

Leonid Pereverzev was born in Moscow on October 18, 1930, and died there on March 18, 2006. Encyclopedias describe him as a jazz musicologist, journalist, and design theorist. But that's not all.

From the age of seventeen, he worked at a factory, participating in the development of the first Soviet industrial Geiger counters. Later, he worked at the Institute of Earth Physics and the Institute of Mining of the USSR Academy of Sciences. His fields included seismoacoustics, earthquake prediction, and forecasting sudden coal and gas outbursts in mines.

His bibliography features essays on Duke Ellington alongside articles on "Mine Borehole Geophone" and "Impulse Method for Calibrating Electrodynamic Vibrometers." Pereverzev was an experimental engineer who ventured into art.

He first entered the art world through jazz. Alexei Batashev recalls his lectures during the Thaw era. Assistants would bring a home tape recorder the size of a transformer booth, a tube amplifier, speakers, and reels of recordings onto the stage. Pereverzev would quote aesthetic masters from memory, in the original language. From 1965 to 1967, he and Batashev conducted a series of lectures on jazz in the Grand Auditorium of the Polytechnic Museum.

In the early 1960s, Pereverzev wrote a book about jazz. It was rejected for publication, and he intended to burn the manuscript. Friends saved it, and parts were later published in "Muzykalnaya Zhizn" magazine. George Avakian, the producer for Ellington and Armstrong, wrote that Pereverzev sat quietly in his ivory tower, producing criticism that had few equals in the world.

The title of the article also comes from Batashev's recollections. When musicologists found themselves in a quandary, the advice was "Ask Pereverzev."

Cybernetic Spring

In the early 1950s, cybernetics was denounced in the USSR as a bourgeois pseudoscience. By the end of the decade, the pendulum had swung the other way.

In 1959, mathematician Rudolf Zaripov visited Moscow State University to learn the "Ural" computer. He spent his evenings and nights teaching the machine to compose music. By the end of his assignment, the "Ural" was composing waltzes and marches. In 1963, Zaripov's booklet "Cybernetics and Music" was published, followed in 1964 by "Creativity and Cybernetics" by Yu. Filipiev, and in 1966, translations of Wiener ("The Creator and the Robot") and the aforementioned Moles appeared.

Pereverzev later debated Zaripov's booklet in his 1966 book, mentioning him by name. Zaripov saw the limitations of machine music as a *temporary* constraint, attributing it to imperfect programming. Pereverzev, however, saw a fundamental difference between a machine and a composer, though he valued Zaripov's experiments. He wrote that they "brilliantly confirm the initial propositions of materialistic aesthetics by proof by contradiction."
