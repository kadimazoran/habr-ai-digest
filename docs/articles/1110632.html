Why Split Tunneling in Android Clients Leaks

Whitelists and autostart checkboxes in Android VPN clients create a false sense of security. Force-stop the client, and the next request silently goes through your home IP. This is a minor issue for YouTube but can lead to a permanent ban without a refund for a $200 Claude Max subscription. The problem isn't flawed client implementations but an Android limitation: split tunneling is incompatible with the system kill-switch. AOSP Dead End: Why Happ Can't Combine Split Tunneling and Kill-Switch

There's a seemingly ironclad opinion: why complicate things when you can go into the settings of Happ, v2rayNG, or Hiddify, select the desired apps, enable autostart, and live happily ever after. The problem isn't with the developers of these clients, but with a strict limitation of Android itself.

Within the main profile (user 0), per-app split tunneling works through VpnService.Builder: the client passes a list to the system via addAllowedApplication (only Claude and ChatGPT go through the tunnel) or addDisallowedApplication (banks and delivery services go directly). Everything looks perfect until you try to enable Android's only true kill-switch: system Lockdown mode ("Block connections without VPN").

Under the hood, the OS's firewall, netd, in Lockdown mode, completely blocks any packet in the user 0 profile that tries to bypass the tun0 interface. This is even directly warned about in the official Mullvad help page for Android: enabling "Block connections without VPN" will cause all apps from the split tunneling exclusion list to permanently lose internet access. You can only create exceptions from Lockdown using the DevicePolicyManager.setAlwaysOnVpnPackage method, and it's only available to the device owner or the work profile owner. A regular VPN client doesn't get these permissions from the system.

This leads to a simple dilemma:

  • Enable system Lockdown on top of split tunneling, and your banks, taxis, and marketplaces will lose network access.

  • Disable Lockdown to pay at the store, and you lose the kill-switch entirely. The smartphone operates in fail-open mode: upon any tunnel failure, Android silently routes packets directly through your home Wi-Fi or mobile carrier in milliseconds.

This can be tested in ten seconds. Open any app from your proxy list without Lockdown, then kill the VPN client process in settings or via an adb shell am force-stop command. The connection won't drop; the next request will simply go through your real IP.

And you don't even need to reboot your phone daily for such a leak. Android itself is perfectly capable of dropping background clients:

  • You open the camera or a heavy game, and the system low memory daemon LMKD (and on HyperOS, ColorOS, and EMUI, a proprietary background process killer) quietly kills the background client. Claude, still in memory, immediately sends its background request directly.

  • The subscription auto-renewal within the client is triggered. When the config is recreated, the Xray core restarts and re-establishes tun0. A gap of a couple of seconds occurs, and without Lockdown, packets freely fly past.

  • The smartphone switches from Wi-Fi to LTE as you leave a building, and the proxy kernel freezes for a second during the interface change.

  • The phone finally dies or updates overnight. The autostart checkbox relies on the system broadcast ACTION_BOOT_COMPLETED. Android delivers it in a general queue with a delay of 15-40 seconds and throttles background starts on newer firmware. While the client is just waking up, background synchronization and your finger, automatically tapping the neural network icon, have already leaked your real IP.

"Then I'll tunnel the whole phone and split domains within Xray"

Another popular option among experienced users: enable system Lockdown for the entire phone, and route Russian services directly using routing rules within Xray or sing-box. This scheme is indeed smarter, but on Android, it has three weak spots.

The first is domain whitelists. The mobile client for Claude or ChatGPT doesn't just connect to a single, neat address like claude.ai. It simultaneously pings Cloudflare Turnstile, OAuth servers, the Stripe payment gateway, analytics and feature flag services (statsig, sentry, datadog), and a bunch of changing CDNs. The geosite.dat database is a week out of date, or the developers add a new subdomain, and your chat goes through the Netherlands, while the authorization or telemetry request flies directly from your home IP. To anti-fraud systems, a session simultaneously appearing from two different countries looks even more suspicious than a direct connection without a proxy.

The second weak spot is Russian banks and government services. When the entire user 0 profile is routed through tun0, the system's ConnectivityManager honestly flags it as an active TRANSPORT_VPN, even if their domain is set to direct. As a result, payments hang, delivery services don't see the address, and you're back to pulling down the notification shade to disable the client.

The third nuance concerns battery life. Many don't notice the battery drain of Happ until a direct comparison. Constant background keepalive timers, server pings, and subscription checks regularly wake up the processor and prevent Android from properly entering Doze mode. On my smartphone (I'll admit: a typical everyday measurement on a single device, not a lab), the background drain overnight differed significantly.

My Own VPS with AdGuard DNS and SNI Proxy: Respect to the Authors, but It Doesn't Work on Android

There's another approach, to which I have great respect for the authors: setting up AdGuard DNS on your own VPS (so it returns the server IP for desired domains) and configuring an SNI proxy there as well. For a home router, TV, and laptop, this is a truly elegant engineering solution. My frustration isn't with such DIY schemes, but with the myth of the reliability of mass Android clients.

But as soon as you try to protect your Claude subscription on a smartphone with a DNS + SNI proxy combination, four purely mobile problems arise:

  • Manual domain whitelists don't go away. While you've listed claude.ai and api.anthropic.com, auxiliary domains for captchas, billing, and telemetry are still resolved directly.

  • Classic SNI proxy works over TCP (ports 80 and 443) and reads the open hostname from TLS ClientHello. Mobile apps on Android and services behind Cloudflare actively use QUIC (RFC 9000) over UDP port 443. A regular TCP SNI proxy doesn't intercept it, and ECH (Encrypted Client Hello) technology encrypts the hostname altogether, breaking SNI-based routing.

  • Apps themselves and mobile carriers often ignore custom DNS. Clients use built-in DNS-over-HTTPS (DoH) or cache IPs, and cellular networks intercept DNS on port 53 and inject their own IPv6 DNS (RDNSS) when switching towers.

  • An open SNI proxy on a VPS can be found by scanners within half an hour and turned into a free proxy for bots. You have to restrict access by whitelisting your IPs. This is manageable at home with a static IP, but on a phone that jumps between Wi-Fi and dynamic carrier CGNAT five times a day, the scheme turns into a daily sysadmin duty.

How Android's Work Profile Solves the Problem (and Why Pocket Won't Become a Regular VPN)

Instead of another split tunnel within user 0, I relied on the OS's built-in mechanism: Android Work Profile. The system creates an isolated user 10 next to the main user 0, with its own /data/user/10/ directory, separate cookies, and its own network visibility. To avoid tying together multiple utilities, I combined the open-source Shelter and the Xray-core engine into Pocket Android (source code and ready-to-use APKs are open on GitHub; you can install it and run the same force-stop test).

Separating into two profiles removes all the architectural conflicts mentioned above in one go:

The main user 0 profile has no VPN at all; banks and government services connect directly. Only Claude, ChatGPT, and the desired browser are cloned into the work profile user 10. Since Pocket manages this profile as the Profile Owner, it strictly enforces system Always-on + Lockdown for user 10. There are no whitelists inside: 100% of traffic (TCP, UDP, QUIC, DNS, IPv6) either goes to the proxy or dies at the Android firewall. As a bonus, you get a clean /data/user/10/ environment: its own cookies, a separate (or empty) Google account, isolation from your Russian apps, and the ability to run two copies of the same app on different servers. Place shortcuts with the briefcase icon on your desktop and forget that a proxy even exists.

I'm often asked to add a "tunnel the whole phone" option to Pocket Android. Such a feature will never be added. Launching a regular VpnService in user 0 is an evening's work, but in the same instant, the app would turn into another leaky all-in-one with all the same problems of banking and leaks that I myself ran away from.

Limitations You Should Know About in Advance

Android's Work Profile doesn't grant magical invisibility. The system doesn't create separate Linux network namespaces, so the tun0 interface is still visible in the kernel's general routing table /proc/net/route (a regular check via ConnectivityManager from user 0 won't see it, but a low-level scanner will).

Isolation doesn't mask GPS, SIM card data (TelephonyManager), system language, or time zone. And most importantly: no Lockdown can save a cheap datacenter IP if its subnet is already on Anthropic's blacklists. A clean exit server is still your responsibility, and the Work Profile merely guarantees that your real IP will never slip through alongside it. Plus, there's an Android limitation: a phone can only have one work profile, so you'll have to choose between corporate Intune/MDM or an already installed Shelter.
