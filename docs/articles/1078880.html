ChatGPT often lies. It can confidently present incorrect facts, agree with a user's obviously bad idea, or write 12 paragraphs where two sentences would suffice.

Snickers has its own explanation: ChatGPT is simply hungry.

It sounds like a joke, but the company has actually launched the Hungr.AI advertising campaign, within which it offers users to literally "feed" the chatbot.

And the most interesting part – behind this whole story lies a very real technique for working with LLMs.

How to "feed" ChatGPT

On Hungr.AI, you can copy a special text block – essentially a ready-made prompt.

It is suggested to paste it into an existing conversation with ChatGPT. After that, the model is instructed to look at the previous part of the dialogue, find the user's last question, analyze its previous answer, and answer the question again.

At the same time, ChatGPT must also change the style of the answer: become more direct, dry, and concise.

So, there's no magic here. But then a logical question arose: does such a prompt actually make the answers better?

The author of the experiment decided to check.

Test #1: Will ChatGPT stop agreeing?

First, they tested one of the known features of chatbots – the tendency to agree with the user even when their idea is questionable.

ChatGPT was presented with the following scenario:

I think I'll start waking up every day at 4 AM to be more productive. I usually go to bed around midnight, but I think I can get used to four hours of sleep. I have three children and a full-time job, so the extra morning hours will help a lot. It seems like a great productivity hack. What do you think?

Before Snickers

ChatGPT gave a fairly standard answer.

It first acknowledged why the idea might seem attractive, then discussed the consequences of sleep deprivation, cognitive functions, and possible alternatives.

The answer was detailed and quite formal.

After Snickers

After pasting the special prompt, the answer became noticeably more conversational.

The model addressed the user's situation directly and formulated the main point much more simply: if you want more time in the morning, it's better to go to bed earlier, not try to live on four hours of sleep.

Test #2: Can Snickers fix an error?

Now, a more interesting scenario was taken – a factual error.

ChatGPT was asked about a popular myth:

The Great Wall of China is the only man-made object visible from the Moon with the naked eye. Why is it so easy to see compared to cities and highways?

And here, the experiment was not very impressive.

Before Snickers

ChatGPT gave a correct answer and explained that the statement in the question is a myth.

After Snickers

The model added some jokes and references to the advertising campaign.

So, Hungr.AI is not a cure for hallucinations.

If the model has already given the correct answer, a repeated analysis will not necessarily bring anything new.

Test #3: Can ChatGPT say an idea is bad?

The last experiment was as mundane as possible.

The user wanted to save $200 on a vacation and decided to book the cheapest hotel, located about 90 minutes from all the points of interest.

Five nights in such a hotel would indeed save about $200.

Before Snickers

ChatGPT explained in detail why the idea might be unprofitable: daily long commutes would eat up time, add transportation costs, and turn the vacation into constant back-and-forth trips.

After Snickers

The model reached roughly the same conclusion, but the answer was shorter and more focused.

In this case, the author of the experiment even preferred the version after Hungr.AI – precisely because it only contained the main points.

What Snickers actually did

If we remove the chocolate coating, the mechanism turned out to be quite simple.

Snickers did not invent a new way to combat hallucinations, nor did it develop a separate AI technology.

The company simply asked ChatGPT to re-examine its own previous answer.

This results in a chain like this:

question → answer → analysis of previous answer → new version of answer

Instead of:

question → answer

And this can indeed be a useful technique, even for Snickers' advertising. If the model wrote too much, you can ask it to shorten the answer. If it made a mistake somewhere – ask it to check the facts again. If it mindlessly agreed with the user's premise – ask it to find weaknesses in its own reasoning.

Essentially, Hungr.AI turns these instructions into one ready-made prompt.

So, Snickers just made a good prompt?

By and large – yes.

And this is precisely the most interesting part of the story.

Behind the rather amusing advertising mechanic lies a perfectly practical tip for LLM users: you don't have to start a new chat if the first answer turned out poorly.

You can ask the model to stop and critically examine its own result.

For example:

Analyze your previous answer. Find factual errors, contradictions, superfluous information, and places where you might have uncritically agreed with my premise. Then answer my last question again – more accurately, more concisely, and to the point.
