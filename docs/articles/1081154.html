While companies spend billions of dollars creating ever-smarter language models, one startup has gone in the opposite direction. They launched a chatbot that has absolutely no artificial intelligence. No LLMs, machine learning algorithms, or automatic response generation. A user types a question into a regular chat window, waits for a response, and a human answers on the other end.

The project is called ChatTJB, and its creator is 32-year-old Tucker Bryant, a former Google project manager. Initially, he answered all questions himself, but it quickly became clear that one person couldn't handle even the simplest chatbot if it suddenly became popular.

The World's Slowest AI (NI?)

ChatTJB emerged in April 2026 as an art project dedicated to how people are getting used to delegating more and more decisions to artificial intelligence. The website itself looks completely ordinary: a user opens the page, sees a familiar chat window, types a question, and sends it. Then, all that's left is to wait for a response. Except the text on the other end of the screen isn't generated by ChatGPT, Claude, or any other model — it's read and written by Bryant himself.

In the summer, he decided to further play into this idea and placed a huge advertising billboard in San Francisco. On it, ChatTJB was advertised as the "leading AI-powered chat interface," and a small asterisk at the bottom explained what AI meant in this context: Average Individual. However, even this clarification turned out to be quite symbolic: when Bryant posted a photo of the billboard, part of the text was obscured by a tree branch.

The joke unexpectedly took off. After the billboard appeared, users sent over 100,000 requests to ChatTJB, and at one point, the service was receiving over 5000 questions per hour. Naturally, one person couldn't physically answer them all, so Bryant announced a call for volunteers — ordinary people willing to read user questions and answer them instead of him.

By the time Fortune's article was published, there were already over 10,000 such volunteers in the queue, with the list growing by about a thousand people a day. This resulted in a rather amusing inversion of the entire artificial intelligence narrative: while tech companies try to make machines more human-like, ChatTJB invites people to voluntarily impersonate artificial intelligence.

Why is Such a Chatbot Needed at All?

There's a quite serious idea behind this whole story. Bryant noticed that people are starting to delegate more and more decisions to AI that they used to make themselves. At one point, he caught himself asking a neural network whether to wear a short-sleeved or long-sleeved shirt at 65 degrees, even though he had lived in that city for seven years and knew the local weather perfectly. His girlfriend suggested a term for this behavior: "cognitive surrender".

This refers to a situation where a person uses AI not just as a tool but gradually begins to transfer responsibility for their own decisions to it. Instead of getting information, thinking, and making a decision independently, the user asks the neural network what to do and then simply follows its advice.

Bryant's idea didn't come out of nowhere. In a 2026 study, Wharton professors Stephen Shou and Gideon Nave studied precisely this phenomenon. After conducting a series of tests, they found that participants quickly completed tasks when the AI provided correct recommendations. But when the model made mistakes, the opposite happened: participants who relied on AI assistance performed about 15 percentage points worse than those who received no guidance from artificial intelligence at all.

A paradox arises. The problem isn't just that the neural network sometimes makes mistakes. It's much more important that the user might stop verifying its answers. The faster and more convenient AI becomes, the easier it is to simply accept the result and move on.

And this is precisely where ChatTJB transforms from a joke into a peculiar experiment. Regular ChatGPT strives to remove almost any friction between question and answer: ask a question — get a result a few seconds later. ChatTJB does the opposite. A response might take several hours, the person on the other end might not know something, might disagree with you, or might give a completely subjective piece of advice. No infinite scalability, no guarantee of a correct answer, and no magic.

Why Do People Want to Wait for an Answer?

One user wrote to Bryant on the first night of his honeymoon. He explained that for some reason, he didn't feel completely relaxed and asked if that was normal. Bryant says that it was precisely these kinds of messages that gradually changed his attitude towards the project.

ChatTJB started as an absurd joke about AI dependency, and then it turned into a place where people simply talk to other people.

Perhaps this is why the project turned out to be so popular. We've spent the last few years and a huge amount of money teaching computers to talk like humans. ChatTJB offered the opposite service: a person pretending to be a computer, and sometimes the user doesn't even know who is on the other end.
