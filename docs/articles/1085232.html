Remember the study about 'AI slows down by 19%'? It was repeated – and it broke

July 2025: METR showed that experienced developers work 19% slower with AI, although they are confident they are 20% faster. February 2026: the experiment was repeated on 57 developers – and METR itself explained why the numbers cannot be trusted: more and more participants do not want to work without AI even for money, and 30-50% hide tasks where AI is particularly good. In July 2025, METR published the results of an experiment involving 16 experienced Open Source developers. For the study, tasks from their own repositories were used, and participants were randomly divided into two groups: 'with AI' and 'without AI'. The result showed that working with AI was 19% slower. At the same time, participants were confident that they had become 20% faster. Since then, the 'minus 19%' figure has been cited in debates about whether AI actually helps programmers.

After that, METR released two more posts on the same topic: in February 2026 – about a repeated experiment with 57 developers, and in May – about the results of a survey of 349 people. The February post is titled 'We are Changing our Developer Productivity Experiment Design', and it is dedicated not to new numbers, but to explaining why the old method stopped yielding reliable results. Below are all three publications in order with numbers and conclusions that emerge from reading them sequentially.

July 2025: What was measured?

Let me remind you of the experiment conditions, as they are important going forward.

Who and Where:

  • 16 developers, each with an average of five years of experience and about 1.5 thousand commits in their own repository

  • Repositories exist for about ten years on average and contain over 1 million lines

  • 246 tasks: about half were formatted as Issues in the repository, the rest were created specifically for the experiment (more details in footnote 4 of the article)

How:

  • Each task was randomly assigned to the condition 'AI allowed' or 'AI prohibited'

  • Participants' screens were recorded

  • The primary tool used was Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet, with 56% of participants not having used Cursor before the experiment

  • Payment was $150 per hour

What was expected and what was received:

  • Participants predicted a 24% acceleration in work with AI

  • Economists surveyed separately expected a 39% acceleration, and ML experts – 38%

  • In fact, tasks under the 'AI allowed' condition took 19% longer (confidence interval – from +2% to +39%)

  • After the experiment, participants still believed that AI had accelerated their work by 20%

In the arXiv paper, the authors examined 21 possible reasons (don't ask – the blog mentions 20). Five of them were confirmed:

  1. Developers overestimated the usefulness of AI

  2. They knew their code too well

  3. The repositories were large and complex

  4. AI proved unreliable: participants accepted less than 44% of its suggestions, and about 9% of the time was spent checking and correcting

  5. AI does not see the implicit context of the project

For ten reasons, there was insufficient data, and six were rejected.

And one of the paragraphs from the FAQ on the blog, which almost no one paid attention to at the time. Here and below, the translation is mine:

It's possible there are experienced Open Source developers who chose not to participate because they believe in significant AI-driven acceleration and don't want to be forced to work without it on half of the tasks. None of the participants reported such motivations, but we cannot rule this out (like other sample biases).

The second cohort started a month later, and this became the main problem.

February 2026: The Repeat

The second cohort began in August 2025.

  • Ten people from the first study and 47 new developers from more diverse projects, including small and young ones

  • Total – 57 developers, 143 repositories, and over 800 tasks

  • Payment was reduced to $50 per hour

  • Regarding tools, METR notes that by 2025, the use of agents like Claude Code and Codex had sharply increased among Open Source developers

Results:

  • For the ten 'old' participants, tasks with AI now took 18% less time (range – from -38% to +9%)

  • For the 47 new participants – 4% less (range – from -15% to +9%)

Both intervals contain zero. METR formulates this as 'weak evidence of acceleration' and does not consider -18% as a result. Instead, the entire post is dedicated to the reasons for the deterioration of sample quality. The summary highlights three reasons:

1. Developers are increasingly refusing to participate. Literally: 'A growing proportion of developers say they don't want to do 50% of their work without AI – even for $50 an hour for tasks they choose themselves.' METR emphasizes that the study 'systematically loses developers with the most optimistic expectations of AI.' One of the participants from the first cohort responded to the invitation:

I'm torn. I'd love to help with updated data on this, but I really enjoy working with AI!

2. Payment. The rate was reduced threefold, which also deterred many. METR directly points this out and does not hide it behind the first reason.

3. Time became difficult to measure. For some participants, the agent works on one task, while the person takes on another. What to put in the 'hours' column is unclear.

As for those who remained, in a survey, 30-50% of participants admitted that they do not include some tasks in the experiment because they do not want to perform them without AI. One of the new participants noted (ellipsis is a METR cut):

I've noticed I'm actually heavily biasing the task selection... I'm avoiding tasks that AI will do in two hours, and I'll do in 20. It's going to be very painful if 'without AI' comes up.

Another compared working without AI to walking across the entire city when they are used to calling an Uber. Yet another participant did not complete any of the tasks assigned to them in 'without AI' mode. METR classifies this as a 'less serious' problem – formally, it's about only one person. However, this is how that unaccounted-for developer from the FAQ might have looked in 2025.

What METR concludes from this:

  • Participant selection currently affects 'a minority of developers and tasks, which limits the magnitude of the bias.' The experiment has not turned into garbage.

  • However, the estimated -18%/-4% is likely the lower bound. The true effect 'could be much higher' for those who dropped out of the experiment.

  • The main reason for selection is the growth of AI expectations among developers. And further: 'As AI capabilities and developer expectations grow, these effects will only intensify, further limiting the validity of such a design.'

That is, to measure whether AI speeds up a programmer, it is necessary for the programmer to sometimes work without AI. In early 2025, for $150 per hour, people agreed to this; by the end of 2025, for $50, fewer people agreed, and those who did hid tasks. METR suggests combating this with short, intensive experiments with high pay. This might help, but they themselves have described the trend.

May 2026: A Survey Instead of an Experiment

Since the experiment encountered difficulties, METR decided to ask directly.

Who was asked:

  • 349 technical workers (after filtering out ten questionnaires with contradictory answers)

  • Among them – 87 engineers, 71 researchers, 129 academics and graduate students, 48 founders and managers

  • Half regularly use Claude Code

  • The sample is 'convenient': GitHub, university lists, METR employees' acquaintances, X (the share of respondents was about 2%, and 70% of respondents received a reward of about $200)

While listing all this, METR itself notes that 'the results likely suffer from significant sample bias.'

What they answered:

  • The median estimate of acceleration is 3 times

  • Increase in work value – 1.4–2 times, depending on the question's wording (METR specifically distinguishes between 'speed' and 'value': quickly creating an unnecessary dashboard is speed, not value)

  • The same question about value: March 2025 – 1.3 times, March 2026 – 2, forecast for March 2027 – 2.5

  • The median respondent is willing to give up 29% of their salary for a month of AI access – METR calls this estimate weak, as respondents' salaries were estimated by a language model

METR then explains why the results should be treated with caution. Not 'we don't believe it,' but 'there are preliminary grounds for skepticism' – this is how they formulate their position. Here are three observations.

First: the lowest estimates among all subgroups came from METR employees themselves. The authors consider four explanations and 'slightly lean' towards the first (employees remember last year's gap between perception and measurement). METR estimates this gap at an average of 40 percentage points for developers (the aggregate results show 39, but the calculation is done for each participant).

Second: seven respondents reported a tenfold or greater increase in value. METR analyzed each of them.

  • For two, public work results were checked – 'we are confident that both exaggerate; at least, the orders of magnitude more valuable work is not visible externally'

  • Two participants raise 'some suspicion'

  • For two participants, there is no data

  • One, according to METR, truly performs an impressive amount of work through agents, although this is more about quantity than value

Third: the conclusion. Such average indicators 'do not seem implausible,' but 'there is a real probability that respondents are giving larger numbers than they would if they thought longer.'

What I understood from this

In 2025, developers thought that artificial intelligence had accelerated their work by 20%. However, according to stopwatch measurements, they became 19% slower. The error was not in the number, but in the direction.

In 2026, repeating such measurements became more difficult. More and more developers refuse to work without AI for the offered compensation, and those who agreed hide tasks from the experiment where AI is particularly effective. At the same time, developers themselves estimate the acceleration at 3 times.

The question 'Will AI replace a programmer?' is not being solved at all in this form, and METR's three posts are not about replacement, but about acceleration. But they provide an understanding of the question itself. It implies that one can take a programmer, take away their AI, and see what changes. In early 2025, such an experiment was successfully conducted. By the end of 2025, the control group began to disintegrate, and METR expects the situation to only worsen going forward.

This does not mean that measurement is no longer possible. Other RCTs exist – Peng et al. with Copilot, Google's internal experiment – but they were conducted on simple or Greenfield tasks, which METR notes in its paper. Additionally, METR lists six directions for future research:

  • Intensive experiments with high pay

  • Observation of real commits (link to SemiAnalysis, about 4% of commits on GitHub are already made by Claude Code)

  • Surveys

  • Fixed tasks instead of personal ones

  • Agent benchmarks

  • Randomization by people, not by tasks

Some of these have immediate caveats regarding their disadvantages.

The takeaway for news readers is simple: if you see 'AI has accelerated our team N times,' ask where this number came from. If it's from a survey, remember that in the only comparison of subjective estimates with a stopwatch I know of, people were wrong about the sign. If it's from an experiment, clarify how much participants were paid and who refused to participate.

If you know of a recent 2026 agent RCT where this is all in order, please share it in the comments; I haven't found one yet.
