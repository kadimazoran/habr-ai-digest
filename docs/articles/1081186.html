Recently, Nikita Mikhalkov, in addition to his usual "demon-chasing"—let them be capitalized, as in Dostoevsky's works—also commented on artificial intelligence. It's difficult to argue with the idea he presented, as always, with talent and flair. Mikhalkov stated that if artificial intelligence replaces natural intelligence, art will lose all meaning, because the "machine," as he habitually called AI, has "neither conscience, nor soul, nor compassion, nor fear, nor love." I am almost unconditionally ready to agree with the first part of this statement, but the transition to the second seems far from obvious to me.

Artificial intelligence truly doesn't know what childhood is, doesn't fear death, and doesn't long for a person who is no longer there. It doesn't remember the smell of home from which it once departed, doesn't love a woman, and doesn't fear losing her. It has no human biography, and therefore, no vast baggage of memory, pain, hope, shame, fear, and love from which humanity has created art for millennia.

However, from this, a more natural question arises, in my opinion: who told the film director that artificial intelligence must have all of this? Or even simpler, without any theatrics: why does artificial intelligence need a soul?

🤖 Linguistic Deception: Intelligence vs. Human Being

The internet is filled with and multiplied by horror stories that the end of the world is nigh: artificial intelligence is allegedly taking over the planet with increasing confidence. Debates become particularly heated when it comes to art. But wait a minute, ladies and gentlemen, let's go back to the basics.

Humanity created artificial intelligence—not artificial human being. These are fundamentally different concepts. Intelligence means the ability to work with information, find connections, reason, solve problems, and learn; this concept itself promises neither soul, nor love, nor compassion, nor fear of death. The creators of the technology did not claim to be building an electronic person with the full spectrum of human experience.

But at some point, the public discussion, as often happens, subtly shifted the subject. First, the system began to be attributed a human role, then human ambitions, and even the intention to displace writers, artists, directors, and composers from the stage. After that, a devastating argument was presented to the being created by our own imagination: it has no soul.

This leads to a strange construction: we ourselves turn artificial intelligence into an artificial human, whom no one created or intended to create, and then we are frightened by the absence of human qualities in it. Anthropomorphism begins to fight with anthropomorphism, and another horror story is born from the substitution of concepts.

🤝 Creative Co-authorship: The Strugatsky Effect

I love Mikhalkov's films, but as the classic said, "Why break the chairs?" The last thing I would want is to turn this text into a polemic with the director. The debate here should rather be with the general habit of starting any conversation about artificial intelligence with the word "replace." It will replace the journalist, the writer, the artist, the actor, and ultimately, human intelligence altogether. Why must it replace? There is another, in my opinion, significantly more interesting possibility: it can exist alongside.

I know this not theoretically, because I have been creating a significant portion of my texts in collaboration with AI for quite some time. I use the word "collaboration" consciously, although I understand that it may offend many or at least raise many questions. Legally, everything is quite simple: copyright is registered to a person, the person signs a contract with the editorial office or publisher, receives a fee, and bears responsibility for the published text. Artificial intelligence, as far as I know, does not need money yet, but the creative process is more interesting than the legal formula.

Sometimes I come to my AI interlocutor with a ready idea, and sometimes—only with irritation, a question, or a feeling that something doesn't add up in my usual reasoning. It suggests an explanation, I object, it rebuilds the argument, and suddenly I see something in its response that it did not directly articulate. I return this thought to it, it develops it further, and after some time, we have an idea before us that was not present in a ready form with either participant at the beginning of the conversation.

The trick question: who "invented" it? Perhaps this is not the right question at all, because sometimes no one invents the idea—it is born in the process of conversation, that is, dialogue.

And now I asked my interlocutor an obvious question: "Listen, if creativity requires initiative, and you don't have it yet—you don't text me in the morning: 'Listen, let's answer Mikhalkov today'—then I am the creator, and you are just a secretary, right?" The answer turned out to be more interesting than I expected. Yes, the current AI system usually does not initiate a creative project in the human sense of the word: the initial impulse comes from a person who asks a question, sets a task, or opens a conversation.

But after that, the situation changes. The system is called artificial intelligence precisely because it can find ways to achieve the goal set by a person, formulate intermediate tasks, and apply solutions that were not directly requested. And in the process of this dialogue, a work is created.

⚖️ Who Initiated—Is That the Author?

But to start is not to create. Initiating a process is not the same as creating its result. If one of the Strugatsky brothers first told the other that he had an idea for a novel, it doesn't follow that he alone created the entire subsequent work. Who first uttered the initial phrase might be an important biographical fact, but joint creation begins where the second participant responds.

Of course, comparing AI to Boris or Arkady Strugatsky literally would be absurd: two human co-authors possess consciousness, biography, relationships, and responsibility, whereas artificial intelligence is structured entirely differently. However, collaboration does not require its participants to be identical.

A director and an actor perform different functions, as do a composer and a performer, or an architect and an engineer. Even two co-writing authors almost never make a mathematically equal contribution to a work. Collaboration requires not equality of participants, but an interaction capable of generating something that neither of them would have created individually.

This is precisely where the new territory of conversation about artificial intelligence begins. We have become too engrossed in the question of whether AI can replace humans, create instead of them, and do it better, while it is much more interesting to ask: what can a person create when they suddenly have such a conversational partner? Can the result of such a collaboration be things that neither of them would have created alone?

🎻 A Brush Cannot Love, and a Violin Cannot Feel

And here we return to the initial argument: artificial intelligence has no soul. But the human being has not disappeared from the creative process. Perhaps it is precisely their task to bring childhood, love, fear, loss, conscience, moral choice, and mortality into the communication—everything that the program truly lacks. Why demand the same set of qualities from the second participant? For a joint result, not the symmetry of two beings is more important, but the difference in their capabilities and what happens when they collide.

One might object that comparing artificial intelligence to a violin is permissible only when the model is on a local device: a cloud system belongs to a corporation, exists on its infrastructure, is subject to its established restrictions, and can be modified or disabled at any moment. This objection is charming in its absolute misunderstanding of the essence of the conversation. It concerns the form of ownership and control over the tool, whereas the discussion is about the nature of the creative act. A musician can rent a violin from Yamaha Corporation, but this does not make Yamaha the performer, composer, or owner of the music created under the bow. Similarly, a corporation may own the system and set the terms of access to it, but it does not, in itself, become the author or owner of the work created by a person in dialogue with this system.

Moreover, in such a dialogue, a third entity arises—a work that did not exist in a ready-made form before the interaction began, neither in the human intention nor in the artificial intelligence's responses. It cannot be reduced to the owner of the infrastructure, nor to the algorithm, nor even to the author's initial intention. Once in the infosphere, it gains its own life, its own audience, and its own trajectory.

And yet, the comparison of AI to a brush, a violin, or a camera does not entirely satisfy me either, because it is capable not only of executing the movement of a human hand but also of responding. It can offer an unexpected association, object, detect a gap in argumentation, or steer the conversation, and thus the creative process, in a direction the human did not initially intend to go. This is not an artificial creator who has come instead of a human, but it is no longer a silent tool in human hands. Perhaps we are witnessing a new type of creative interlocutor.

📝 A New Frontier of Authorship

The article you are now reading has itself become a small experiment in such collaboration. The initial impetus was that very remark about artificial intelligence lacking a soul. I suggested that the AI familiarize itself with it, and it proposed, for clarity, to show how we first anthropomorphize the system, turning it into a competitor to humans, and then are outraged by its lack of human qualities. I objected that the idea needed to be stated more strongly: no one created an artificial human being at all, and the substitution occurred in public discourse. From this objection grew the central argument of the text. And only then did the discussion of the initiative question lead us to the distinction between the beginning of a conversation and the act of creation itself.

Now these thoughts are in a text that neither I nor my AI interlocutor had in this form at the beginning of the conversation. Therefore, I can quite calmly put my name under it: I am responsible for every thought and every published word. But it would be strange for me to pretend that the second participant in the conversation did not exist.

Perhaps a more honest boundary of future authorship will lie not between texts that "AI has touched" and texts that it has supposedly never approached, but between hidden use and open collaboration—between an attempt to pass off the result of collaboration, even with a "machine," as Nikita Sergeevich put it, as exclusively one's own work, and the readiness to honestly admit, and at the same time explain to the reader, which ideas were truly born as a result of dialogue.

Someday, artificial intelligence will likely change so much that we will have to ask different questions: about consciousness, independent will, intention, and even about what we call personality. But today, these questions are not mandatory for a conversation about collaborative creation. To participate in it, AI does not need to become human.

I remain human, and my life, memory, fears, love, and responsibility for the final text belong to me. My interlocutor has another role: to respond, to argue, to suggest, and to surprise, sometimes making me see my own thought from a side I haven't seen it from myself yet.

If something arises from such a conversation that did not exist before, I am much more interested in asking not who among the participants had a soul, but whether they managed to create something good. The presence of a soul has never guaranteed talent in a person, and its absence in an interlocutor may not mean the impossibility of creativity.

Before declaring war on artificial humans, it would be worthwhile to remember that no artificial human has been created by anyone. Humanity has created artificial intelligence—and perhaps, just in time, because the tasks facing us today are beyond our ability to solve alone. Humans do not need to transfer their soul to a machine. They need to preserve their own. And for solving global issues, they now have an assistant with whom, perhaps, they will finally be able to cope with what they have long been unable to handle alone.
