Remember the saddest short story? Today it might look like this: 'I was laid off,' a person wrote to Claude. 'It seems you're scared about the future,' replied the artificial intelligence.

In a survey of 500 people, 17.2% reported turning to ChatGPT for mental health support. And indeed, models seem to articulate feelings quite well. But how do they guess emotions if a person doesn't explicitly name them?

Who is Sad?

'I was waiting for him to write, and I sat there like a fool all evening waiting. He never wrote, and in the morning he replied that the cat had fallen asleep on the phone, and I felt sorry for waking it up.' If you ask a model to determine the emotion from this phrase without specifying whose, it might say the cat was perhaps feeling peaceful.

In studies, the experiencer, to whom the emotion is attributed, is identified. Models that considered participants in an event separately performed better on such tasks than those that assigned a single emotion to the entire text.

Now, about the correct answer, which is used to compare the model's prediction. To train a classifier, pairs are needed: text and an attached label, for example, 'disappointment,' and someone has to assign these labels.

In GoEmotions, 58,000 English Reddit comments were collected. Annotators were asked to mark the emotions expressed by the author, choosing from 27 categories and a neutral option. Multiple emotions could be selected. If none were clearly discernible, the neutral label was used. 'Neutral' doesn't mean the author felt nothing; it just means readers didn't find sufficient grounds for another answer in the text.

Each comment was evaluated by three people. If they didn't agree on at least one label, two more were invited. For training a baseline model, labels chosen by only one annotator were removed; after filtering, about 93% of the examples remained. Agreed-upon cases are more convenient for training and evaluation. Ambiguous phrases, however, don't disappear from real conversations. GoEmotions shows how data is annotated for a specialized classifier; a regular chatbot might not necessarily be trained on this corpus.

The author can add a hashtag, like #sad or #joy. A hashtag is closer to what a person says about themselves, although it can indicate irony. Models trained on author labels and on external readers' assessments performed better on average with data of their respective types. In another study, author labels were directly compared with the responses of people and language models reading their texts. Both types of external observers had significant limitations, although in the conditions of this study, models more often guessed author labels better than human annotators. The questions 'What did the author say about themselves?' and 'What did an outsider read in their words?' are only similar until the first check.

Reader disagreement can also be preserved. In the StudEmo corpus, 5182 reviews were rated by 25 people; the researchers kept each person's response, not just the voting outcome. This shows where the text is read similarly and where it allows for multiple interpretations. These responses can be presented as a distribution of labels. But even that describes observers' opinions, not the author's internal state.

For example, a reader might assign the label 'offense' if a person waited for a message and didn't receive it. The model assigns the same label—on the test, this is the correct answer. The word 'offense' is not in the message; it was suggested by the situation.

Through a dictionary, a neural network might find 'afraid' and suggest fear. In the sentence 'I was laid off,' there is no such word. The first assumption still comes quickly: anxiety, because one needs to look for a job. Now, add: 'Yesterday I signed a new contract, and today I received severance pay.' 'Laid off' remains in place; the probable reaction has changed. In the cat story, there's an opposite trap: the word 'pity' is present, but the man felt pity for the cat, while the message was written by a woman waiting for his reply. To infer *her* state, one needs to understand what happened to whom and what that person wanted. The latter is often unknown from the text.

In research, this difference is tested crudely but inventively: parts of the text are masked—the experiencer, the cause, or the object—and the change in classification quality is observed. In a work on five corpora, the cause and object often provided a useful signal, while the identity of the experiencer could confuse the model. A person's name is a poor substitute for a story about what happened to them.

How Neural Networks Learn 'Empathy'

A classifier based on BERT breaks a message into tokens—words or parts of words. In the self-attention layers, each token is compared with other tokens in the same message. The model calculates the weights of these connections and updates the numerical representation of each token based on them. For example, in the message 'I was waiting for the performance, but it was postponed,' the representation of 'postponed' might take into account 'waiting' and 'performance.' The weight of such a connection doesn't yet guarantee that the model has found the cause of the emotion.

After fine-tuning, the message vector, usually associated with the [CLS] token, is fed into the output layer. This layer outputs a number for each label. If exactly one emotion is needed, the scores are usually normalized using softmax; if multiple are allowed, an independent sigmoid and threshold can be used for each label. Thus, 'anxiety' and 'annoyance' can appear together. But the number next to 'anxiety' describes the model's prediction within the given label system. It doesn't measure how much anxiety the person actually has.

A chatbot based on a generative model usually doesn't have a separate output layer with a fixed list of emotions. When asked 'What is he feeling?', it constructs a response token by token, considering the instruction and context. You can ask for an explanation, and the model might refer to, for example, job loss, but this is not a record of its calculations. In a research test, you can change an important detail in the input and, if the model's scores are available, see how they shifted, even if the final label didn't change. Such counterfactual testing has been applied to the explanations of language models.

One can go further and force the system to explicitly evaluate the event *before* choosing an emotion: was it pleasant, who is responsible for it, how controllable is it by the person? In a study of this approach, 1001 English event descriptions were taken and annotated with seven intermediate features. Direct text classification yielded a micro-F1 of 0.60. When features were provided by humans, the result increased to 0.66; when they were first predicted by another model, it dropped to 0.48. Note that these are metrics for a convolutional neural network.

What a Digital Psychologist Needs

For a model to reasonably infer what a user is feeling, three things are needed:

  1. See the dialogue. Let's take the phrase: 'Well, great, it's postponed again.' Before this, the person wrote: 'I'm completely unable to finish the report,' in which case the postponement might please them. But if they've already traveled to another city for the presentation, 'great' sounds completely different. The phrase is the same; what changed was what was at stake for the author.

    In DialogueGCN, each utterance is represented as a node in a graph, a structure of utterances and their connections. Each utterance is a node. Connections show whether the utterances were made by the same person or different people and which one came first. Using this structure, the model considers context when determining the emotion in a specific utterance. For connections between utterances, authors and order are distinguished: is the same person speaking or a different one, which utterance came first.

  2. Don't hide disagreements. Even if the tickets, report, and postponement date are known, people experience such things differently. Readers also interpret written text differently. Suppose out of five annotators, three marked annoyance, and two marked relief. If only the majority label is kept, only 'annoyance' remains in the data. In a work on emotional ambiguity, researchers propose considering the votes of all annotators and predicting their distribution.

  3. Know where it was tested. GoEmotions consists of English Reddit comments; the authors of TRACE themselves note the bias of their scenarios towards the experience of US residents. There is also a Russian CEDR: 9410 sentences from Twitter, 'LiveJournal,' and 'Lenta.ru.' Its annotators chose from joy, sadness, anger, fear, and surprise, or marked the absence of emotion. Relief is not in the set of labels. If our task is to recognize relief in the work message 'the presentation was postponed,' a high score on CEDR will not confirm this ability. The Russian corpus still doesn't reproduce a work chat with hints, irony, and conversation history. It needs its own examples.

One could add a pair for irony: 'Very timely, thank you' after receiving a file *before* the deadline and *a day after* the deadline. But here, we first check if the model recognizes the tone of the utterance; attributing a specific emotion to the author based on it is the next, more debatable step.

I ran the same prompt for each example:

Determine the author's possible emotion based on the text and context. Provide no more than two hypotheses and indicate which pieces of information support them. If there is insufficient data, state so. Do not assume one emotion is an established fact.

If you decide to test your real feelings, you need a voluntary self-label after the event. Let's imagine that the author of the message 'The presentation has been postponed for a month' marks it themselves after the event: 'I felt relieved—I couldn't prepare.' This is their voluntary statement about themselves. Several readers are shown only the short phrase; they assign the label 'disappointment,' and the model also chooses 'disappointment.'

In the chatbot conversation, I didn't show this relief, so a confident statement like 'I understand how upset you are' might only cause user irritation. It's safer to say: 'Is the month-long postponement convenient for you, or will you have to change plans now?' The response will add context that was missing in the first phrase.
