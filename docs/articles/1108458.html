Hello, Habr!

Imagine a typical university assignment. The instructor asks a neural network to create a task, the student gives it to their neural network and submits the finished answer, and a neural network checks the work again. From task preparation to checking, it turns into a correspondence between the student's AI and the instructor's AI — the irony is that it could be the same model. And people in such correspondence only pass texts to each other and act as living proxies. Of course, this doesn't always happen, and not all instructors create assignments using neural networks, and not all students solve homework with their help. But this scenario can no longer be called a rarity. At the same time, banning chatbots is pointless — they are already part of our daily lives, like the internet and smartphones.

At the same time, AI itself may not be an enemy to learning. This year, MIT conducted an experiment: seven student teams had one month to design and build a small jet engine, and they were allowed to use AI. The neural network acted as a personal tutor, filling knowledge gaps, answering questions around the clock, without breaks for lunch or sleep. This helped even those who previously didn't know how gas turbines worked in principle to start working.

However, only two teams, both composed of senior students, reached the full engine tests, and only one managed to launch it. The neural networks hallucinated, agreed, and didn't understand physics, doing so confidently. The winners treated the AI's suggestions more cautiously than the others and relied on their own knowledge.

Why am I talking about this?
We wanted to take what AI does well and put the student in the same position as the MIT teams. This led to the idea of creating an AI trainer for court proceedings, ChatCourt. In short, the idea is to simulate a real court process with roles of judges, secretaries, defendants, and plaintiffs. The student can act as a defendant or plaintiff, and the task of each is to win the case. There are no right or wrong answers in the simulator, so it's not a linear or adaptive test. Each case review is essentially unique and depends on the student's and other participants' remarks. The opponent might make a mistake, or the judge might err — just like with people. The instructor evaluates the student's actions through the lens of the process, not the outcome of the case.

Naturally, the student will use their own chat with AI during the hearing, and no one can prevent it. But AI can give bad advice. To notice this, one will have to study the case documents, verify the model's answer, and formulate their own. AI can also provide basic knowledge, but for this, critical thinking and a desire to understand the process are needed, and then AI can become a good partner. At the same time, the AI trainer does not encourage the use of AI during the process; it can be completed on your own. That was the idea. What happened in practice?

How it Works

Arbitration process, first instance. The student chooses a side, plaintiff or defendant, and goes through six stages: preparation, preliminary hearing, court hearing, evidence and motions, closing arguments, decision. The AI judge makes the decision.

The model in the product must be Russian. The trainer is intended for Russian universities, which have restrictions on the use of foreign AI services. Chinese models are also subject to these restrictions.

The concept started as a deterministic "trainer-navigator" without AI for the Civil Procedure Code and Arbitration Procedure Code. But over time, it was narrowed down to the first instance of the arbitration process, and AI participants appeared on the condition that the program still controlled the process.

The case begins after the acceptance of the claim: preparation, preliminary hearing, trial, conclusion. Three training cases based on the course lessons. The filing of the claim, appeal, cassation, and enforcement were postponed.

From the very beginning, it was clear that the process management should not be handled by the model. It was necessary to develop a process management engine. At the same time, it should not be rigid, so that the simulation of the process is as natural as possible. The code should decide what is permissible, and the model should decide what to do next.

Next, I would like to talk about what I encountered during development, namely, the nuances of fine-tuning the AI judge in search of a balance between the quality of the response and the realism of the judicial process, so that there is no feeling that you are chatting with a stupid bot.

Technically, it's a synchronous modular monolith on Python [FastAPI] with SQLite: for a pilot with a few students, this is quite enough. In general, the development did not foreshadow any major problems.

During development, I used Anthropic's Sonnet model as the AI judge for testing. It has excellent agent skills, and the trainer for it was not difficult. But this was my mistake. I planned to use GigaChat Ultra in production, but recently it was removed from commercial use, and developers were given 50 million free tokens for research; I wanted to save them for beta testing.

My mistake was assuming that Sber's model and Sonnet could accept and return the same format — JSON. But Sonnet can call multiple functions in one step, while GigaChat can only call one function at a time.

This was a period of long head-scratching and banging my head against the wall. The engine was almost completely incompatible with GigaChat. It was easier to rewrite it from scratch.

Prompt Engineering

And when I finally rewrote the engine for GigaChat, I encountered the problem of model looping — the agent wrote the same phrase in the chat. This usually happens when the same prompt is input, and the model's temperature is set to zero. In this case, the model chooses the most probable next token, and the answers start to repeat.

Why is the prompt the same?

Because the engine is deterministic, and if the model writes an incorrect answer, and the engine does not accept it, it returns the same prompt again with a note on how to fix it. And if the answer is still incorrect, the repeated prompt can be exactly the same, 1 to 1.

I determined the model's temperature empirically by roles: judge 0.2, AI side and hints 0.3, service checks - 0.01. GigaChat does not accept zero. But adjusting the temperature didn't help much — if you increase its temperature too much, the model stops listening to instructions and starts hallucinating. The introduction of "Pause" mode saved from endless loops of token consumption, when the engine sees repeating messages from the model, it stops the process with an appropriate service message.

The solution to the looping problem itself was to include several innovations in the prompt:

  • explanations of what exactly is wrong and what to do instead;

  • implementation of refusal codes;

  • For each refusal code, an example of how to fix it. There were as many as 45 such examples;

  • progress log — what the model has already suggested and why it was rejected;

  • after an answer break due to length, the repeat continues with the note "write shorter" and with an increased limit of 4000 tokens.

Indeed, I haven't conducted so many prompt tests and checks before.

It wasn't just one prompt edit. In one of the runs, the judge asked the defendant seven times in a row, even though it was the student's turn. After this, a "guided" mode had to be introduced into the engine: the engine itself chooses the type of the next step and offers the model one to three actions. An action rejected due to repetition is removed from the menu until the end of the turn. After a repeated question, the judge is offered a transition or conclusion. And if it's the student's turn, the judge can only ask them.

How Memory Works

Initially, I considered using LangGraph in the engine.
Each AI participant needs their own "desk" that they can see: the judge should not see the parties' strategies, the opponent should not see the student's folder. LangGraph can work in a multi-agent scheme with a divided context, but by default, agents share a common message history, and the checkpointer stores the state of all roles along with student texts. This meant that I would have to write the isolation myself, and disable storage. After that, only a thin cycle would remain from the framework, which is easier to write yourself, which is what I ultimately decided to do, abandoning LangGraph.

The model has no "memory" of its own.
GigaChat Ultra supports a context window of 128 thousand tokens. Before each call, the engine rebuilds the "desk" for the role anew from the case log and the published version of the scenario. After the call, nothing remains of it. The desk consists of seven sections:

  • Role position: the strategy of one's own side with weaknesses or the judge's hearing plan. The hint provider does not get this section.

  • Case inventory: table of contents of materials. Short documents are included entirely; the model opens long ones itself using the "open document" tool.

  • Protocols of past hearings instead of a raw event log.

  • Role notes, up to 15 items. This is the model's only "long-term memory." It writes them itself as a separate action; they are stored as events in the case log and survive restarts.

  • Working section: stage, permissible actions, unflagged checks, agenda, what has already been done in this part, norms; the judge also has system hints and arbitration flags.

  • Last 10–15 remarks of the current hearing.

  • Progress log: what the model has already suggested in this turn and what has been rejected.

Initially, I didn't use a cache; it wasn't needed for developing and testing prompts when I used Claude code CLI with the Sonnet model as the AI judge. But when it came to real tests with the cloud model via API, the cache provides significant savings. It works on the provider's side, and there are several nuances:

  • Session key. The header X-Session-ID, a hash of the "attempt + role" pair, is added to each call. Service calls use the type of service task instead of the role. GigaChat caches the common start of requests with the same key, and without the header, the cache hardly worked.

  • Section order. The immutable part comes first: system instruction, role position, inventory, protocols. The mutable part comes at the end: remarks, progress log, guided step, and correction layer. Therefore, a repeated call to the same role hits the cache for the entire immutable part.

Using the cache had a quite significant effect. On a run of 25,981 input tokens, 25,920 were taken from the cache. In general, during tuning, the cache share increased from about 8% to 44% in some cases. Tokens from the cache are billed separately and are noticeably cheaper.

After all the manipulations with model settings, it was possible to get rid of loops and optimize prompts so that the total cost for one full test run of a case scenario decreased from 1.7 million tokens on Sonnet to 0.55 million tokens, of which 0.39 million were billable and 0.17 million from the cache (30% on average) on GigaChat, and the number of calls decreased from a peak of 100 to 62.

The risk of errors by the AI judge in substance has not disappeared, and the two points below are precisely about how to reduce it further.

The most noticeable thing the trainer cannot yet do is work with judicial practice. The AI judge and AI opponent rely on case materials, the scenario, and what the model knows itself. They do not have databases of judicial decisions and current legislation in RAG format.

Foreign analogues have such a layer. In the Chinese AgentCourt, agent lawyers go through a thousand civil cases and accumulate three knowledge bases: legal norms, debate experience, and a library of cases. However, this is a research simulator, not an educational trainer — agents learn there, not students.

In Russia, there are legal reference systems with databases of judicial practice and AI assistants for lawyers. Connecting one of them to ChatCourt would be a logical next step. Then the judge and opponent would refer to real court positions, and the student would have to verify these references, as in a real process.

If you are developing such a product and are interested in trying it in an educational court, please write. We are open to partnerships.

The second thing is the court decision itself. The trainer brings the case to a decision, but the text of this decision is still weak: it is far from a real judicial act. The next step is clear: settings and working with the prompt. But there is reason to believe that prompt engineering alone will not be enough, and the model will need to be retrained.

And this is a task of a completely different level. A large language model is needed, which cannot be trained on a gaming laptop. A dataset is needed: tons of real court decisions, where the input is a list of facts after the case, and the output is the text of the decision. It still needs to be collected and prepared.
