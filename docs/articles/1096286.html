What Happens to Your Application

The mechanism is described in the help sections of job sites: the employer sets criteria and questions in advance. After you apply, a bot conducts an initial screening and saves the answers in the candidate's profile, which the recruiter can see. If the answers match the requirements, the candidate automatically moves forward; if not, the system can close the application without human intervention.

In theory, the scheme seems quite reasonable. But the catch is that people go through it blindly: no one announces the rules of the game in advance. For illustration, Marina saved the questions the bot asked her:

  • Why are you interested in this vacancy;

  • What would you like to do in the team, what is important to you in future work;

  • Why are you currently considering offers, what work format suits you;

  • Are the skills from your resume still relevant, what is your desired salary level;

  • Are you willing to work entirely in the office;

  • Would a company without IT accreditation be a problem;

  • Do you have experience working under a fixed-term contract.

These are literally the same questions that a recruiter would ask in a first twenty-minute call five years ago. But during a call, a person reacted to the answers, and the candidate understood where the conversation was heading. The bot remains silent, and the candidate doesn't know what happened to their answers.

Bypass the Filter or Pass

All three thought about how to bypass auto-filters and came to different solutions.

Alexander has a whole technique. At the end of his resume, he puts a column of keywords from the desired vacancies in white font on a white background. The recruiter's eyes won't see them, but the parser, he calculates, will and will boost the rating. Alexander takes the wording from the vacancy text: if it says "microservice architecture," his resume will have it, not "monolith splitting." He removed everything older than seven years, except for the most relevant projects: he is sure that a long work history can be read by the system as an "ageist candidate" and lower him in the search results. I have no way to verify if this works in specific ATS, so I'm recounting Alexander's personal experience.

Sergey gets by without exotic methods: he describes results in detail and selects words that he believes are used for filtering.

Marina tried and gave up. She took AI recommendations for adapting resumes for ATS, applied them, and got, in her words, "a very truncated version of her resume." She reverted to the old one.

'It's an endless game of cat and mouse,' says Alexander. 'You can adapt your resume, you can use the right wording, but if your experience doesn't match the vacancy, it's still difficult to get further.' He understands that a human ultimately makes the decision. But without resume optimization, Alexander believes, you can't reach that human.

It's a similar story with cover letters. Alexander generates them with a neural network: he feeds it the vacancy and his resume. By his estimate, recruiters don't open ninety percent of the letters, and for the rest, they read the first paragraph. Therefore, he writes for ATS and keyword search: 'This is not a letter to a living person, but SEO optimization of a resume for filters.'

Marina writes cover letters herself and only for positions she's genuinely interested in. Sergey once wrote a basic text, ran it through AI, and now edits it by hand for each vacancy.

Their goals are different. Alexander wants to pass the filter more often, Marina doesn't want to become a set of keywords, and Sergey seeks a middle ground. The commonality is one: none of the three expect their resumes to be read carefully at all.

What Bots Can't Do

Whether to respond to a bot, all three decide differently, and the candidate doesn't see the consequences of this decision immediately.

Marina takes the dialogue to the end, to 'thank you, we will contact you.' Sergey almost immediately gives up: 'The bots are raw, I quickly ended the correspondence. I didn't get to HR.' This saves him time, but the application ends there, and the candidate doesn't find out.

The main complaint about screening for everyone is the same: you can't explain anything in it.

'With a bot, you're taking a lie detector test without the right to context,' says Alexander. To the question 'have you worked with microservices?' a recruiter could give a detailed answer like: 'I haven't deployed to production, but I designed the architecture and ran load testing on a pet project.' A recruiter would see the potential and make a note, while the bot would count the answer as 'did not work.'

Marina talks about the same thing ironically. You can't ask a counter-question, although there are 'definitely questions,' according to her: 'It's always a one-way street: you are interrogated, you answer. Thank goodness they don't shine a lamp in your face.'

Sergey calculates the losses. A recruiter might look at a candidate more broadly than formal criteria, find another vacancy for them, or recommend them for a neighboring position within the company. The bot compares answers to requirements and closes the dialogue at the slightest mismatch.

The requirements have remained the same, the selection hasn't become stricter. But the context, through which a person without a formal match could show that they fit the essence, is unfortunately lost. Moreover, it's lost from both sides: the person whom the bot screened out on the third question, the recruiter will never see. They certainly won't reach me, even though there might be a very smart and great person there.

Why All Three Have Lost Faith in Applications

All three independently came to the same conclusion: applying is no longer the primary way to look for a job.

Alexander estimates the proportion of hidden vacancies and positions filled through recommendations before publication at about seventy percent. He has no way to verify the figure, but he looks for work this way himself: 'Applications have turned into a combination of "applied and immediately wrote to a friend at that company."'

Marina confirmed Alexander's opinion: 'The most effective option is to go through acquaintances.' Sergey talked to colleagues, including those in HR, and decided that it's faster to search through Telegram chats and direct contact with recruiters.

That is, applications are now more of a background activity for the sake of a checkbox, while the real search is through acquaintances, chats, and direct contacts.

The choice of where to apply has also changed. Alexander approaches this like a sales funnel: 'Now it's not about "I want," but about the funnel.' First, he checks the publication date. An ordinary vacancy that has been posted for more than two weeks is almost certainly a phantom for him: the company is building a database, monitoring salaries, or forgot to remove the ad. The tech stack should match at least seventy percent, because, he believes, the position will still be filled by someone with a complete match. He skips vacancies without a salary range: in his experience, the budget there is usually a third lower than the market rate.

It turns out that the employer screens the candidate based on formal criteria before the conversation, and the candidate screens the employer before applying. Both sides save time, and both lose their potential working match.

The Problem Isn't Just Bots

If you list everything that irritates my interviewees, bots, surprisingly, are far from the top of the list.

Alexander has a long list. Out of fifty applications, by his count, forty-five go to the archive unread. He considers a test that takes eight hours before the first human conversation an attempt to get free work. He describes a one-way video interview, where you record answers to a robot's questions, as: 'a humiliating procedure that takes a lot of time, where you don't even see any reaction.'

Salary ranges of 'from zero to a million' turn out to be half that during the call. Three agencies and the company itself post the same position: if you apply to all of them, you'll end up in your own spam folder.

Marina is annoyed that applications remain unanswered for weeks: 'Then I don't understand the point of posting a vacancy and keeping it open if no one is processing the applications for it.' She considers a rejection without explanation to be bad form.

Sergey is annoyed by the huge forms that are requested before any conversation.

In none of these complaints is the algorithm primarily to blame. The same problem of opacity exists everywhere: it's unclear who will see the application, what will happen after the answers, and whether the vacancy even exists. None of the three suggested canceling automation. Everyone wants the 'simple human' understanding of the rules by which they are selected.

'The market is now heavily segmented,' says Alexander. 'There are those who spend months looking for work, going through all these circles of hell, and those who are poached quietly, without a single application.'

What does this all mean? There will be no moral here; I don't know yet, and imagining the future in such a context is quite scary, strange, and a little sad. Therefore, I suggest discussing the stories and opinions about the current situation in the comments.
