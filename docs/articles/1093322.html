Glory to Robots (and More Tips)

It's 1985, and Japan is experiencing a truly golden age! The entire world is clamoring for its electronics and stylish cars. Nintendo game consoles are about to become the most coveted gift, largely thanks to a certain mustachioed plumber. And the "mecha" genre is storming the popularity charts.

A scene from Expo 85. The robot revolution seemed to be in the air. Source: Cybernatic Zoo.

One of the Expo 85 installations. Source: en.namu.wik.

In short, Japan had enough accolades and achievements to fill more than one trophy cabinet. And then there was the international exhibition Expo 85 in the all-Japanese science city of Tsukuba, which, by the way, was largely inspired by our Novosibirsk Akademgorodok of the sixties and seventies.

I wonder if Tsukuba's panel buildings were also copied from Novosibirsk? Source: Wikiwand.

In general, Japan had firmly established itself in the collective unconscious as a country of super-technologies, where building a robot was as easy as stepping out for a smoke. And some enterprising Japanese sought to strengthen this image even further. By any means necessary.

And so, one evening, it arrived at a Tokyo restaurant in the Roppongi district called Grazie's, which served Italian fare. Literally a meter tall – this creation's height did not exceed 1.2 meters – the strange android moved unhurriedly and diligently from table to table, gliding almost silently on its chassis. Approaching guests, it would hasten to introduce itself in a clipped digital baritone: "Konnichiwa, watashi-no namae-wa Ken-san desu" – "Hello, my name is Ken-san."

A Hardworking Robot

Ken-san was the brainchild of the Japanese company Daimichi Koki. It wasn't a major zaibatsu, so sadly, not much is known about it. But apparently, Daimichi had plenty of "mecha" fans, including among the "kanbu," meaning management, to put it in Russian. Ken-san's software was written by another company – Seibu Saison Group.

So, Ken-chan busily scurried between tables, engaging visitors in conversation. However, it could hardly be called a conversation, as its repertoire of pleasantries was smaller than that of a budgerigar: the wonder-robot knew only 15 stock phrases like "Sit down and eat, please", "Would you like dessert?". And it might ask about dessert after a guest had already paid and was leaving. Or vice versa – they hadn't even ordered their main course yet.

Ken-chan at work. Source: Paleofuture.

But it evoked indescribable delight, invariably drawing a crowd of curious Tokyoites. The US Navy's daily newspaper, "Pacific Stars and Stripes," published a note that further fueled the intrigue.

It reported that when it came time to choose wine, fruit, or dessert, a visitor risked being greeted not by a charming waitress or a nimble waiter, but by something resembling a refrigerator or a vacuum cleaner on wheels, speaking in Darth Vader's voice and offering to sing "Happy Birthday." And it wasn't cheap: a musical number performed by the robot in honor of your birthday would have cost $425.

Mitsugi Watari with Ken-san. Source: Paleofuture.

Thus, Ken-chan attracted crowds of onlookers from all over Tokyo, the suburbs, and even the Yokosuka naval base, where American sailors on leave would come. But almost none of them understood how the wonder on wheels worked.

In reality, Ken-chan was a feat of both Japanese engineering and human operators. Like a puppet on a leash, it moved around the dining room on a wire only 3.6 meters long, which connected it to a human operator who set its route and chose which phrase from its arsenal the robot would utter.

Ken-san could indeed function as an expensive trolley for $43,000. Source: Paleofuture.

Alas, the Japanese robot, which, incidentally, first appeared at Expo 85, could do almost nothing autonomously. It couldn't serve dishes, pour wine, or even offer recommendations from the wine list, although its creators initially planned to make it a kind of electro-sommelier. It was no match for the Soviet "Arsik," which could pour lemonade and turn on the TV according to the newspaper program!

The only thing that hinted at even a little autonomy was an acoustic radar that helped Ken-chan avoid bumping into tables and patrons. Apparently, the operator couldn't always control the situation due to a limited field of vision, and to prevent "accidents" in the guest area, it was equipped with an echo-location sensor.

The Star's Decline

Ken-chan only lasted in the waiter staff for two years. Apparently, the novelty effect wore off over time, and fewer and fewer people came to see the robot. By the way, it's unknown whether the restaurant manager Mitsugi Watari managed to recoup the investment in Ken-san, which cost $43,000 (equivalent to $133,867 today).

In 1980s Japan, robots were literally everywhere. Source: theoldrobots.com.

As the once-celebratory newspaper "Pacific Stars and Stripes" sadly noted:

"Scientists predict that labor-saving robots will revolutionize industry in the 21st century and free millions of people from hard, low-skill service jobs. However, restaurant manager Mitsugu Watarai views these predictions with skepticism. He tried to introduce a mechanical waiter at his Italian restaurant Grazie in the entertainment district of Roppongi and calls the idea 'impractical.'"

Interestingly, Ken-chan became a kind of symbol of unfulfilled hopes for robotization. The 1980s, with their "Blade Runners" and "Terminators," were literally breathing the neon-drenched air of the future, where black and ungrateful work could be offloaded onto robots, as long as they didn't decide to exterminate humanity in gratitude for such a gift. And this feeling generated hype, which quickly became a bubble destined to burst.

Ken-san eventually remained in a corner of the Grazie's restaurant, gathering dust, and its further fate is unknown. It's amusing how the aspirations of that time turned out to be far from the realities of the future, where manual labor is precisely the least likely to be replaced by robots. Well, at least for now.
