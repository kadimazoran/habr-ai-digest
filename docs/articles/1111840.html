Types of Videos

There are essentially two types of videos. Cartoony: characters are drawn, they can be made funny and recognizable. Realistic: real people, interiors, products. As a marketer, I mostly create advertisements, but videos for businesses, historical channels, and anything else are assembled the same way. The assembly process is identical; only the character's appearance changes (step 2).

Three Ways to Assemble a Video

AI videos are currently assembled in three ways. Which one to choose depends on the scene.

  • A. Single Frame. You generate a frame, describe what's happening in the prompt, and the model animates it.

  • B. Frame A and Frame B. A starting frame, a prompt, and a final frame that the model should reach. This is good for transitions: a room before and after renovation, real video transforming into a cyberpunk scene.

  • C. Storyboard. A visual plan of several frames on one sheet, like a comic. The model uses this to assemble the entire video.

Previously, videos were assembled from "start and finish" pairs and then stitched together in editing. With a storyboard, the model assembles everything frame by frame itself, making it the most convenient method.

Video Length

One generation in most neural networks allows for videos from 5 to 30 seconds. But this is a limitation of a single run, not the video itself. Want it longer? Create several clips in sequence. Use the last frame of the previous clip as the start of the next, or ask the model to continue. The key is to write a complete script in advance. This is how both minute-long videos and mini-films are made.

The examples below are all 15 seconds: a convenient length to test the process.

Six Steps: From Script to Final Video

It's recommended to keep the entire video creation process within a single conversation with the neural network. Proceed step-by-step: the neural network writes the script and prompts, you check them and transfer them to the aggregator. If you're not satisfied with the result, ask to adjust the script or prompt right there in the same conversation and repeat the step.

Three Principles Before the First Step

You come up with the plot. Without direction, the neural network writes scripts as dull as its texts. Its job is to help, not to invent.

The neural network writes the prompts, you check. Immediately specify which models you'll be generating with and ask it to study how to write prompts specifically for them. Then it will tailor the text for each model. In my tests, ChatGPT wrote more concisely, while Qwen was verbose.

Check the prompt before running. An error spotted before generation costs zero credits.

Step 1. Brief and Script

I detail each scene using five fields: location, character, visual, duration, and dialogue. Example: the character's car, the character inside, driving through the city, one line of dialogue, 4 seconds. Each point in the script becomes a separate scene. Then, we create an image for the scene and assemble it into a video.

I no longer write prompts myself: the neural network does it, and I check. Therefore, I immediately tell it which models I'll be generating with.

First, help me fill in the blanks in the square brackets. Don't fill in the format, duration, or product features for me. If I ask you to suggest a style or timing, give a simple option. Check that the intervals are sequential without gaps or overlaps, and their sum equals the chosen duration, not exceeding 30 seconds.

Briefly formulate the brief and wait for my confirmation. Then, suggest a script with the chosen timing: what we see, one main action in each scene, and the narrator's lines. Separately, provide short titles for editing if needed. Do not impose a number of scenes. Maintain a single main character or product, minimal locations, and one core idea. Do not invent characteristics, achievements, prices, or guarantees. Wait for script approval before proceeding to the next steps.

Step 2. Character or Product Turnaround

A turnaround is a sheet with the character from different angles. I attach it as a reference to each generation; otherwise, by the third scene, the character will have a different face. Emotions are added to the sheet: this makes it easier for the model not to lose the character. Complex inscriptions, like a logo on a cap, are attached as a separate reference.

For a realistic character, it's best to have a close-up of the face and a full body shot from all sides. If you want yourself in the video, attach your photos to the prompt. Without a turnaround, the face isn't fully preserved: in my test, when I used only one photo, the character's hairstyle changed. Very simple characters don't need a turnaround; the model holds them well.

Step 3. Storyboard

A storyboard is a single sheet with the video scenes drawn in order, like a comic. The video model uses it as a reference and assembles the video frame by frame. I do this in five steps:

  1. I ask the neural network to prepare a storyboard based on the approved script. And separately, a prompt to draw this sheet in an image model. I specify both models: the one I'll use to draw the sheet and the one I'll give it to later.

  2. I generate the sheet. In the test, I did it in Seedream 5, and it turned out well. If the sheet needs a specific character, I attach their photo and use GPT Image: it works better with attached images. 1K quality is sufficient, and these generations are inexpensive.

  3. I check each frame. It should be clear what the scene is and what's happening in it. My example has five frames.

  4. I remove frame numbers. In the test, the number from the storyboard appeared in the final video for both Seedance and Wan. Therefore, the prompt below prohibits drawing them, and we'll add titles during editing.

  5. I double-check the prompt before running. This is a free check before credits start being used for a retake.

Step 4. Video

Before the prompt, I ask the neural network to study how to write prompts for the chosen model. For Wan and Seedance, they are almost identical; Wan's is slightly simpler. For Kling 4, it's useful if high-quality Russian speech is needed. Here are four rules that save credits.

  1. Label attachments. The aggregator labels files as @Image1, @Audio1, @Video1, etc. The model might not understand the phrase "use storyboard," so always write @Image1 (and so on) in the prompt. If the neural network wrote "storyboard" in the prompt, replace it with the number.

  2. The upload order must match the numbers in the prompt, otherwise the model will pick the wrong images. You can change the order by dragging and dropping. Select the attachment type as "reference," not "starting frame." You can attach multiple references; my aggregator allows up to seven or nine.

  3. Test at minimum quality. Move to high quality only when you're satisfied with the result. For the final render, 720p is usually sufficient. 1080p is not much more expensive, but the difference is not visible after editing.

  4. Do not ask the model to draw text. Even a successful video in the test had an error in the inscription, and the text broke again in the final generation. Solution: ask the neural network to remove texts from the prompt, and add titles during editing.

A couple more settings. Set the frame format directly for the platform: 9:16 for mobile, 16:9 for widescreen. Enable sound only if you want to test the built-in speech. If you want to compare models: keep the references, delete the prompt, and insert the prompt for the next model. You don't need to re-upload images.

The prompt below takes a reliable path: the video has no speech or music; we'll handle sound separately. Wan sometimes provides clear Russian speech immediately, but you can't rely on it for every take.

Step 5. Voiceover

I do the voiceover separately: most video models have poor knowledge of Russian and make mistakes even with inscriptions. Exceptions: Gemini Omni Flash (and Gemini Omni Video), Kling 4.

ElevenLabs v3 and Google TTS are suitable for voiceovers; both are good. Use the one that is more convenient and accessible. The voice style is set by the prompt, and the voice itself is chosen from a library with a Russian language filter.

Two Pitfalls

Remarks in the text. If the narrator's text includes notes like "(pause)" or scene titles, the voice will read them. The narrator's text should only contain words to be read, and the delivery should be specified in a separate prompt.

Length. The recording may turn out longer than the video: mine was 20 seconds instead of 15. Check the duration by the finished audio, not by the word count. Extra pauses will be cut during editing, and if it's still too long, shorten the text.

Step 6. Editing: CapCut or HyperFrames

All that's left is to stitch the video with the voiceover. You can do this manually or delegate it to an agent.

Option A. In any editing software

The simplest way. Load the video and voiceover onto the timeline, then follow four steps:

  1. Cut pauses in the audio track, not the video. Zoom in on the timeline; it's easier to cut that way.

  2. Synchronize the voice with what's happening on screen.

  3. Enable auto-subtitles in Russian and choose a template. You can place your own subtitles over the generated ones to hide them.

  4. Export in the best quality. For codecs, choose H.264, for example, instead of HEVC: it's compatible with more players.

If editing isn't your forte, hire an editor: the source files are already prepared.

Option B. HyperFrames via an agent

You can hand over the editing to the neural network itself. Desktop applications like ChatGPT and Claude, as well as Codex and Claude Code, can edit on their own. Place the clips and voiceover in a folder and ask them to assemble the video. The agent will need free tools like FFmpeg or Remotion. In my test, the agent synced the voice better than manual editing: it sped up some parts and compressed others.

For this type of editing, HyperFrames is suitable: an open-source framework from HeyGen under the Apache 2.0 license. The video is described as an HTML page, and headless Chrome and FFmpeg render it into MP4. The framework equips agents with skills for Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, and Gemini CLI. Requires Node.js 22 or newer and FFmpeg.

Where Each Model Fails

  • Wan 3.0. Weaker in action. Can break text in the frame. Dialogue voices sound too studio-like. Russian speech sometimes sounds like a narrator's, but it might distort surnames: correct with emphasis or re-record the fragment.

  • Seedance 2.5. Best physics and action. Storyboard frame numbers can appear in the video, transitions are sometimes strange. Russian voiceover is weak, but Russian inscriptions are often error-free, more so than with others. Often refuses to generate realistic faces. In video editing, it only accepts video references; the source video cannot be attached.

  • Kling V4. Strange physics, "rubbery" noises, no video editing. But it has clear Russian speech!

  • Gemini Omni Video. Max 10 seconds, sound is always on, cannot be turned off. Best for Russian speech and lip-sync, but punches in action scenes look strange. Rejects generations with real people due to the safety filter.

How to Bypass the Safety Filter

If the model rejects a generation due to a real person, remove the name from the prompt (e.g., write "The Man") and replace the real photo with a generated one. There are no such problems with random characters.

How to Save

  • Check the prompt with a short take and low resolution. Run the final version when you're satisfied with the movement and composition.

  • Create frames and storyboards in ChatGPT if you already have a subscription.

  • Upload references once and only change the prompt for the next model.

Memo: What to Remember

  1. The storyboard eliminated the need for stitching. Wan, Seedance, and Kling V4 assemble up to 30 seconds in one run, and a long video is made from several clips based on a pre-written script.

  2. You come up with the plot, the neural network writes the prompts, and you check them before running, not after.

  3. A character needs a turnaround; otherwise, the face "floats" from scene to scene.

  4. Label each attachment as @Image N or @video1 and maintain the upload order. In tests, I encountered mixed-up references and text that the model was not asked to draw.

  5. Text in the frame and Russian speech are more reliably added during editing. Built-in speech in Wan, Gemini, and Kling V4 is a bonus, not a plan.

  6. For advertising, use Wan; for action and cinema, use Seedance; for Russian speech, use Gemini and Kling V4.

  7. Editing can be delegated to an agent: in the test, it synced the voice better than manual cutting.
