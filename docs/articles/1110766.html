On November 18, at the APEC business summit in Shenzhen, Vladimir Putin stated: "Russia is at the forefront of digital transformation. Our two flagship language models are among the best in the world" (according to Prime agency). The phrase instantly spread through the news, but the most interesting part is not the pathos, but its verifiability: Russia has four major families of language models, and almost all of them participate in open ratings where their position is determined by live user votes, not press releases.

I am not an ML engineer and do not claim to be a judge: my maximum is coding lab assignments in university, and for a neural network, it's important that it responds in Russian and doesn't require a VPN. Therefore, instead of arguing about "the best in the world," I looked into the open crowdsourced rating llmarena.ru, collected data on all four families, and compared their characteristics with the developers' statements. Spoiler: one Russian model is indeed in the world's top-10 – but the overall picture is more complex than the news headline suggests.

What does "among the best" mean: how the open rating works

LLM Arena (llmarena.ru) is a crowdsourcing platform for comparing large language models in Russian. The mechanism is simple: a user asks a question, two random models answer it anonymously, the user votes for the best answer, and only after voting sees the model names. From millions of such pairwise duels, using the Bradley-Terry model – the same mathematics behind the Elo chess rating – a final score is calculated: the more often a model wins, the higher its Elo.

At the time of preparing this material, there are 112 models in the rating, and votes are collected continuously; my snapshot includes data up to November 9, 2026. It's important to understand the limitation of the method: Elo here measures "whose answer people liked more," not "which model can solve an olympiad problem." However, it is the only metric where comparison is done blindly and without developer involvement.

Who are these "two flagships": four Russian families

Russia has four major LLM families: GigaChat from Sber, Alice AI LLM from Yandex, T‑Pro and T‑Lite from T‑Technologies, and Cotype from MWS (MTS ecosystem). They are developing rapidly, so I'll start with brief overviews of their current status.

GigaChat – a flagship with open weights

Sber's GigaChat has been developing since April 2023 and has grown from an experimental chat into a multimodal platform: in addition to text, the family works with documents, audio, and images – its stack includes proprietary ruGPT-3.5 and FRED‑T5, ruCLIP for connecting text and images, and Kandinsky for image generation. The current flagship is GigaChat 3.5 Ultra, released in July 2026: MoE architecture, 432 billion total parameters (28 billion active), a context of 262 thousand tokens, and generation of long texts, according to Sber, has accelerated up to four times compared to the previous generation. The main feature is its open weights: the model is available on Hugging Face under the MIT license, meaning it can be downloaded, fine-tuned, and run on your own hardware. More details in the analysis on Habr.

Alice AI LLM – an assistant grown into an ecosystem

In October 2025, Yandex switched "Alice" from YandexGPT to the new Alice AI LLM family: the flagship is built on an MoE architecture, includes 235 billion parameters, and was trained from scratch on Russian-language texts and ecosystem data. The model is closed, but its strength lies not in its weights but in its integration: it responds in search, browser, and speakers, and through the API in Yandex AI Studio and agent scenarios, it completes tasks – booking a table, assembling a shopping cart, placing an order in "Yandex Taxi" and "Eda." On September 21, 2026, Yandex released the pre-trained Alice AI Foundation version with 80 billion parameters (3 billion active) for open access, allowing commercial use; technical details are in the publication on Habr.

T‑Pro and T‑Lite – the banking path through fine-tuning

T‑Technologies (T‑Bank) introduced the Gen‑T family in December 2024. The first models were built on Qwen 2.5 and underwent deep fine-tuning on a Russian-language corpus – according to the developer, this approach reduced costs by about 90% compared to training from scratch. The current versions T‑pro‑it-2.0 and 2.1 are already based on Qwen 3: 32 billion parameters, with a focus on instruction following and tool calling. The weights are open under Apache 2.0 (model card on Hugging Face), the lightweight T‑Lite with 7 billion parameters is designed for fast chatbots and standard hardware.

Cotype – not an assistant, but an office tool

MWS from the MTS ecosystem took a different path: instead of a universal chat, they developed models for business, trained on business correspondence and corporate documents. The lineup is divided by tasks: Pro 3 with 27 billion parameters handles a context of 262 thousand tokens – roughly an entire annual report; Light 3 with 9 billion is for routine processes; the multimodal VL with 32 billion reads handwritten text from scans; the open Nano with 1.5 billion is suitable for drafts. In the independent MERA benchmark, Pro 3 took third place, second only to models several times larger (MWS AI review on Habr).

Place in the world ranking: verifying the statement with numbers

The president said "two." If we measure by the open rating, the two strongest Russian models are obvious. In the llmarena.ru snapshot from mid-November 2026, GigaChat 3.5 holds seventh place in the world with an Elo of 1121 – close to GPT-5.2 (1122) and DeepSeek V3.2 (1119), and its license is marked as open in the table. Alice AI LLM is in twentieth place with 1069 points, next to Gemma 3 27B and Claude Sonnet 4.

It's important to be cautious with the pursuers: T‑pro‑it-2.0 in the September 2026 snapshot is in 36th place with an Elo of 1024, and Cotype models are not represented in the public leaderboard at all – MWS currently positions them as a B2B product.

For scale, here's the global lineup from the same snapshot: Gemini 3 Pro Preview – 1199, Qwen3.6 Max – 1165, GPT-5.4 – 1161, Claude Opus 4.7 – 1149. GigaChat's lag behind the leader is 78 Elo points, Alice's is 130, and T‑pro's is 175 (the diagram at the beginning of the article visually shows this gap). Since the rating is dynamic, positions change: in the September snapshot, Alice, for example, was nineteenth with the same 1069 points – therefore, keep the snapshot date in mind with any figure from this article.

Second echelon: where we are strong and where we are not

An honest answer to the headline's question: both models belong to the second echelon of the world ranking, and there's no need to be ashamed of it. The gap with global leaders is concentrated in areas requiring long agent chains, complex multi-step code, and strict multi-stage logic: in these disciplines, user votes consistently go to Gemini, GPT, and Claude, and the Chinese open models DeepSeek and Qwen also rank higher.

However, on their home turf, our models look convincing. The rating is collected based on Russian-language queries – and GigaChat, in the overall standings, surpasses GPT-5.2, meaning for Russian-language tasks, it's in the world's top-10. Add to this the price and availability, the open weights of GigaChat, T‑Pro, and Alice Foundation (western flagships don't offer this at all), and integration into real services – banks, search engines, taxis, marketplaces. For typical office tasks – writing, translation, summarizing documents – the gap with global leaders is practically negligible.

What's the result

Globally, both our models belong to the second echelon; they lag behind the world leaders (Gemini 4, Claude 5.5, GPT-6, and several Chinese models) in complex programming, multi-step agent scenarios, and logical tasks. But we must understand that regionally and in Russian, these models can indeed show a decent quality of responses, being in the top 20 of the Russian-language rating.
