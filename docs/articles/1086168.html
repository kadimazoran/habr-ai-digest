How Detectors and Language Models Identify AI-Generated Text

Without them, I wouldn't have thought of running my article through available AI detectors (GigaCheck, Aidetector, Decopy, Grammarly, ZeroGPT, Text-Craft, Текстовод) and simultaneously asking six language models: Claude, ChatGPT, Meta AI, DeepSeek, Gemini, and Grok, to see how they determine if text is AI-generated.

The spread was even more interesting than I expected. Aidetector showed 98.4% "written by a human," while ChatGPT estimated the probability of the text being entirely human-written at only 40%. More importantly, the explanations for their decisions revealed that the same features were considered signs of AI by some systems and clear indicators of a human by others.

Take the complex interdisciplinary connections in my text. For Grok and DeepSeek, this was "suspicious, looks like AI," while for Gemini, Meta AI, and ChatGPT, it was the opposite: "AI doesn't combine things like that; it's a human trait."

The situation with the article's structure is even more interesting. Grok and Meta AI called it "clear, characteristic of AI," too clean. DeepSeek, conversely, noted that the sections weren't very clearly structured and also considered this a sign of AI, suggesting it looks like text where a model tries to embed facts.

Incidentally, Grok was the only one that evaluated more than just the text. It found my other publications and, based on this reputation, not just the text, concluded the author was human. But this isn't about my identity; in modern realities, that can also be faked. The point is that there are no longer criteria to determine if text is AI-generated, and even reputation can be fabricated.

"Not Looking Like AI" Is Now the Goal, and It No Longer Means Anything

This is precisely what I discussed at the beginning: how do you set rules for "what is allowed and what isn't" if there's no object? Before we move to analysis, it's important to note that all services and models immediately offered to "humanize" the text. That is, AI, which needs to distinguish text sources, immediately helps to hide them reliably.

According to Goodhart's Law, as soon as a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. "Not looking like AI" is now the goal, and it no longer means anything.

Regarding the first article on the structural instability of AI networks, I received a comment stating that structural instability of a model is treated with SFT, zero temperature, and pydantic validation. This is true, but it refers to the structural instability of the information environment in which AI agents gain increasing autonomy.

All three tools provide something important: they pull the output towards a standard. Zero temperature pulls towards the most probable; SFT towards the training sample; validation towards the specified format. For an individual agent, this is reliability, which is undoubtedly necessary. But all three have a standard, meaning something already known, expected, and accepted. And what provides reliability for one agent works as a cleanup and increase in the proportion of AI content when the same principles are applied to data selection and flow moderation.

However, this is not visible at the code or testing level. It's a process running in the external "technology-human" network and won't be noticeable until it reaches critical limits. Therefore, those who can analyze the behavior of complex global systems, not just individual models and their networks, are needed at the table. The Perversion of Human and Machine Language: How L. Tolstoy and M. Cervantes Became Models for AI-Generated Text

Next, in a separate chat, I asked each model to list the features that distinguish AI-generated text, and all six models provided six different lists. Only the core elements matched: clichés, the "not X, but Y" construction, lists, sterility, repetition, or perfect grammar; beyond that, the lists diverged.

Most interestingly, all models include in "AI clichés" what is actually the foundation of classical rhetoric, which has likely existed for over two thousand years.

For example, a tricolon, or a list of three elements, is called "love of threes" by the models. But "Veni, vidi, vici" (I came, I saw, I conquered) are the famous words of Julius Caesar, and "Liberté, égalité, fraternité" (Liberty, equality, fraternity) is the motto of the French Revolution.

Anaphora, or repetition at the beginning of phrases, is simply called "repetition" by the models. However, it serves a purpose in emotional reinforcement, as in Winston Churchill's speech: "Never give in. Never give in. Never, never, never..." in 1941, its goal was precisely an emotional appeal to his compatriots to fight fascism.

Or my favorite, antithesis, simply put, contrast. The very "not X, but Y" construction. And I'm not the only one who likes it because it strongly influences emotions, not logic: "Ask not what your country can do for you – ask what you can do for your country," John F. Kennedy, inaugural address, 1961. "I came not to send peace, but a sword," Gospel of Matthew.

The main idea of my articles also rests on the same antithesis: AI security is determined not only by code structure but also by the information environment in which it learns. It turns out that to formulate my main idea, I must use a technique for which I will be labeled a machine.

What models call AI's handwriting is the handwriting of Caesar, Churchill, Kennedy, Socrates, and Spinoza. AI writes this way because it learned from human texts, including the best of them. "AI clichés" are not linguistic garbage but its peaks. And now these literary devices are declared non-human: a person is judged for mastering classical rhetoric.

L. Tolstoy was independently named by four out of six models for the syllogisms in the second epilogue of "War and Peace," for the moralizing in later novellas, and for philosophical digressions. M. Detectors, by the way, have already issued similar verdicts. In 2023, GPTZero considered a fragment of the US Constitution most likely entirely AI-written, and ZeroGPT rated its "generation" at 92%. Biblical texts have also come under suspicion.

And when I asked Claude the same question in two different chats, in one, it confidently classified M. Proust's prose as "AI-like" due to its complexity, length, and endless clarifications. In the other, it conversely called N. Gogol and J. Joyce distinctly human – for the same complexity, for deviation from the norm, for a vivid individual style. Moreover, in the second chat, it formulated its own rule: AI tends towards averaged language, while great stylists deviate from the norm. By this rule, M. Proust, whom it had classified as machine in the first chat, is human.

One could say this is just a matter of generation randomness. But that's the point: if the answer changes from chat to chat, then the model lacks a stable criterion.

However, if there's no problem with sources from the pre-ChatGPT era – these texts are demonstrably human, their authorship undisputed because printed copies exist – the problem lies precisely with what models need: the new. New knowledge, fresh ideas, recently written text are now under suspicion.

And here lies the perversion. AI learned from human texts. It extracted the averaged, clear, precise elements. And this averaged output is now declared a sign of AI. It turns out that the standard of good human writing, compiled from the best authors, has become a sign of AI. If you write clearly and powerfully, you are writing like AI, making you suspicious. And new knowledge, to be heard, must pass through a filter designed to reject anything that resembles this averaged output, meaning almost everything of value. The system that should be fed fresh human knowledge is now designed to filter it out.

Right Now, 24/7, a Process Called "Information Genocide" is Happening Globally

But "information genocide" is no longer just an AI problem. Now AI is teaching humans, and as Vladimir Vysotsky sang, we say "shtorma" instead of "shtormy" – the language of the environment becomes one's own. And if the environment for work or study has become regular dialogue with models in recent years, one can adopt their mannerisms without ever running their text through a generator.

This has long been known in psychology: in conversation, we unconsciously adapt to our interlocutor, adopting their words, rhythm, and constructions. This is called communicative accommodation, lexical alignment, or the chameleon effect. No intention is needed; you pick up the accent of a new city this way, and you pick up the mannerisms of those you interact with daily. And we increasingly interact with AI.

And this has been measured. Researchers at the Max Planck Institute found that after the appearance of ChatGPT, words characteristic of this model sharply increased in people's live spoken language. Even a short conversation with a bot caused a person to use its lexicon as their own afterward.

This is where the criterion for text generation collapses fundamentally. Previously, one could say that a generated text differs from a written one. But if humans now, with their own hands, without any generator, write in a manner adopted from AI, then even "clean" human text carries the imprint of the machine. There's nothing left to distinguish. And those who work most closely with AI, precisely the specialists whose new ideas are most needed, are the first to come under suspicion.

This creates a vicious circle in both directions. AI models need human texts, not generated ones. But they learned precisely from human texts, and everything they absorbed from them (clarity, precision, average norm) is now considered a machine trait and may be rejected.

On the other hand, people generate texts using AI but want them not to look generated. And for this, they turn to the same AI to "humanize" them. As a result, there are more synthetic texts online every day, and they are becoming harder to distinguish.

And what the models themselves need – the genuine human signal – is becoming increasingly blurred with each passing day.

And here's a question for programmers. The "allow/disallow" rules you are now writing for agents – did you decide them, or did AI dictate them? I'm not joking. This is an important question because it lies beyond where we are accustomed to looking for risks and enhancing security.

If AI itself increasingly formulates the rules for AI – not directly, but through people who are forced to use AI to keep up with its speed – the same loop arises as discussed in the previous article: models that learn from their own conclusions and copies of their own conclusions degenerate.

The simple example with language models and detectors shows how this works. AI itself discards the human elements it needs, deeming them suspicious. And through "humanization," it receives its own generated texts back, but now under the guise of human ones.

This is what I'm talking about: why businesses and programmers need dialogue with specialists in complex systems and chaos theory, who can highlight global systemic failures. The increasing speed of AI agents directly and proportionally blurs the information environment.

The Blind Spot of AI Agent Security is the Processes of Information Flow Dilution

And here's something important that's easily missed if you only look at the AI system itself. SFT, zero temperature, validation, and other tools – all these bring order within the model, making its output predictable and stable. But they can do nothing about what the model receives as input. And the input is now common to all: the same internet, the same data, the same responses from other agents, and now the return of its own data through humans.

The more synthetic content is in this base, the more often one model's error becomes data for the next, and through it, it enters all systems built on the same base, regardless of how good their code is. You can perfectly stabilize your model and still experience a failure in the near future because the environment from which the code draws data is degrading.

Given current speeds, this could manifest sooner than we expect: texts are produced instantly, models are retrained several times a year, and synthetics enter the common base faster than anyone can filter them out.

Therefore, when making architectural decisions, you must consider not only speed and autonomy but also the properties of the information environment in which the system operates. The reliability of an individual model and the reliability of the environment are two different levels. The former is controlled by IT specialists. The latter is not, but it is precisely what determines the future of your system.

I've written a lot, but the essence is simple: we already know how to protect code, but we still don't know how to protect the environment from which the code draws data. As long as data is diluted by synthetics, it gradually loses its connection to reality and degrades. And the failure, WHEN it comes, will not be systemic in the usual sense – not due to a code error. It will be global and informational: the code is correct, the tests are green, but decisions are built on inaccurate knowledge that diverges from model to model.

What specialists in complex systems and related fields can offer programmers and businesses right now: First, consider revising AI filters and reducing their weight. "Not looking like AI" measures nothing anymore, and it will only get worse. Preserve and tag "clean" pre-AI texts as a reference anchor. This knowledge is demonstrably human, its authorship is currently undisputed, and it is our low-background steel by which everything else can be calibrated. The problem is that its share in the total corpus is melting away under the pressure of synthetics, and without a reference point, there will be nothing to compare against later. Develop other directions – train to check meaning and logic instead of form, origin outside the text (Grok already does this), limit agent rights instead of input recognition, maintain auditor diversity, etc. – but this is a topic for future publications.
