Why Add Computer Vision to Night Vision

The general purpose is clear. But there are nuances. At night, a boat operator needs more than just to see the camera image. They need to distinguish objects on the water, maintain course, and promptly notice things that require attention. The water contains not only buoys and boats but also logs and various other obstacles that are difficult to discern in the dark.

"Night Eye" already solves the problem of obtaining an image in low light conditions. Adding recognition expands its capabilities: the system highlights buoys, boats, and other target objects directly in the frame. For the helmsman, this is additional assistance in monitoring the situation. There's no need to constantly search for each object on the screen independently: the system draws attention to it.

It was precisely for this task that "Night Eye" chose TAPe+ML after conducting test trials and comparisons, heh, with YOLO.

Dataset: RGB, Night Shots, and Thermal Imaging

The initial dataset included RGB images of boats, buoys, and other objects. The team then supplemented the dataset with night recordings, shots from their own cameras, and thermal images.

At one of the later stages, the dataset contained 2205 training and 506 validation images.

It wasn't just about collecting more pictures. It was necessary to represent objects as the device would see them: from different distances, against the background of water and shore, in RGB and thermal channels.

Logs, Small Obstacles, and Fog: What Data Was Missing

During model preparation, it turned out that the availability of images doesn't always match the object's importance to the user. Photos of boats and buoys are easier to find than frames of floating logs or rocks barely emerging from the water. However, it is precisely these small obstacles that interest the "Night Eye" team: they need to be detected in conditions of poor visibility.

For example, for the first attempt at training on logs, Ilya collected about 15 images – ten for training and five for validation (yes, our model needs this many images for training). Therefore, work on this category was выделено as a separate direction, and the first version focused on buoys and watercraft. The next stage is additional shooting and expanding the model to include small obstacles.

Not only the object category was important, but also how a specific camera sees it. Public datasets contained good thermal images, but the team needed more close-up shots from their own equipment. They also needed to expand the variety of boats captured by "Night Eye" cameras in the dark: different types of watercraft, angles, and distances.

A separate challenge was fog. At the model preparation stage, there were no such scenes in the dataset yet. The team planned to replenish the dataset during water excursions to develop recognition on shots from real operating conditions.

Thus, data work became part of product development. For the initial solution, the "Night Eye" team prepared a model for priority objects, and for subsequent versions, they formed a specific image collection program: fog, small obstacles, logs, and additional night and thermal scenes.

Auto-Annotation and Correction of Annotations via API

TAPe+ML auto-annotation accelerated data preparation. Instead of drawing each bounding box from scratch, Ilya reviewed and corrected the system's suggestions.

Concurrently, the team cleaned up the annotations: refined object boundaries, resolved conflicts between classes, and removed duplicate boxes. During the work, 416 duplicate bounding boxes were removed from 282 files.

The API helped automate some dataset operations. Ilya used it not only for recognition but also for data preparation: he removed erroneously placed duplicate boxes of the same class on an object, changed classes of already annotated objects – for example, when merging buoy and marker_buoy (a buoy with a light). Before starting training, he also obtained the number of boxes for each class in the training and validation sets to understand where to add, move, or remove frames. Ilya performed all these operations through API requests.

Buoys and Watercraft – The Main Classes for the First Version

Ilya reviewed the set of categories as training progressed. Similar classes were merged, and priority was given to objects important for the first use case: primarily buoys and watercraft. This allowed focusing on a specific user function rather than on the longest possible list of recognizable entities.

From Trained Model to Operation on Water

After training, Ilya connected the model to the "Night Eye" cameras. During testing, the system received images from the RGB camera and thermal imager, recognized objects through our API, and displayed bounding boxes with class names in the user interface.

In the selected run, the model achieved an F1 score of 0.723 and an mAP50 of 0.735. These metrics characterize the quality of object detection: the balance between misses and false positives, and the accuracy of class and bounding box localization. Behind this result was work with water scenes: collecting frames, checking annotations, refining classes, and adding night and thermal images.

On the water, the team tested how the trained model performed as part of "Night Eye.".

How the Model Learned to Distinguish a Buoy from a Boat

For "Night Eye," it's important not just to find an object in the frame but to determine its category. A buoy and a boat require different interpretations, even though they might look similar in a small thermal image.

This difference is illustrated by one of the model preparation examples. In the same image, the base model labeled small targets on the water as boat, while the "Night Eye" model used the category buoy. Training on domain-specific data allowed us to move from general recognition to categories needed by the product.

Integration with Cameras and Interface via API

The next step was to connect recognition with cameras and the interface. In the test configuration, the RGB camera and thermal imager were connected to an RDBox mini-PC. The internet connection for working with our API was provided by a phone hotspot.

TAPe+ML received frames and returned the coordinates of detected objects. A Python service written by the "Night Eye" team drew bounding boxes on the images and displayed the results in the system's interface.

Thus, the trained model became part of "Night Eye's" proprietary solution. On the screen, the user sees not only the camera image but also the highlighted objects – the result of the joint work of the hardware system, our recognition model, and the partner's software integration.

On-Water Trials: Boats at 500 Meters and a False Positive Under a Bridge

During several field trials, the project team tested the entire chain: image acquisition, frame transmission, recognition, and object display in the interface. The system operated with real cameras in a marine environment.

For instance, TAPe+ML detected boats at a distance of 500 meters – according to the trial participants, at that moment, not even a "dot" was visible in the thermal and regular camera images.

Of course, the system doesn't work perfectly yet: once, while passing under a bridge, it recognized the red glare from its navigation lights as a buoy – most likely because it's trained to recognize buoys with red lights, among other things. This can be resolved by retraining the model – specifically, by adding fresh frames from boats to the dataset and introducing a new class, "bridge."

From Trials to a Local Commercial Version

After the trials, the natural next step was to move towards on-premise recognition. The commercial configuration considers an Android tablet with an RGB camera and a thermal imager.

Local processing means the CV function will not depend on a connection to a cloud API. For the manufacturer, this is an opportunity to supply recognition as part of the equipment package.

Work on the on-premise version for "Night Eye" was one of the stages of optimizing TAPe+ML for a processor core. The initial delivery for the project occupied about 28–30 MB. This version ran on RDBox with a core load of only 30–60%.

Then, we continued optimizing libraries and execution. The current on-premise delivery occupies about 4.2 MB. The model fits into the processor's internal memory and, after initial loading, runs there – without constantly transferring its parameters from RAM. How to Train a Model for Your Task

With TAPe ML, you can train a model on your own data and integrate recognition into your own product – a surveillance system, industrial equipment, or a user service. The platform at ml.comexp.net provides tools for the entire cycle: image upload, manual and automatic annotation, training, results verification, and integration. Detection, segmentation, classification, object tracking. Everything is there.

You can start with a ready-made model: upload an image and see what objects it detects. The base model is trained on COCO and recognizes 80 classes. For your own objects and categories, you can create and train a separate model.

Further work involves several steps:

  1. Upload your task data. The platform supports uploading training and validation images, preparing annotations, and training. In "Night Eye," the team used RGB frames, night recordings, and thermal images – data from the cameras and under the conditions the system was expected to operate in.

  2. Annotate the first examples and enable auto-annotation. You don't have to manually annotate the entire set before the first training. After initial annotation, the model suggests objects in the remaining images, and the user confirms or corrects the result. This cycle allows you to expand the dataset by reviewing the system's suggestions, rather than drawing each bounding box from scratch.

  3. Train the model and check the results. The platform provides training and results analysis tools. The "Night Eye" team used them to review the set of classes, correct annotations, and supplement data with night and thermal scenes.

  4. Integrate recognition into your product. Via the API, you can get object detection results and use them in your own application. The API also allows automating data preparation: in "Night Eye," it was used to remove duplicate bounding boxes, merge classes, and check the number of annotated objects in the training and validation sets.

  5. Obtain an on-premise version for your equipment. After training, we can prepare an execution package for the customer's processor. The current on-premise delivery occupies about 4.2 MB and runs on a single CPU core – without requiring a dedicated graphics card (as we promised) and without transmitting frames to a cloud API.
