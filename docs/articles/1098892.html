Teach me bad, or why I'm against prompting

Recently I had to explain Hegel's laws of dialectics to a 17-year-old guy. To be precise: not just retell them, but explain them so that he would definitely understand. And understand so well that he would immediately become rich and successful.

"The neural network can explain to everyone simply" — they said

In AI advertisements, you can often see that AI can explain any complex information so that everyone understands it: a schoolboy, a baby, Grandpa Vova from the village, for whom AI is just a stuck sound. In general, depending on his age, tasks, and level of education.

As if!

Hegel in a vacuum. The task

Let's imagine the initial task:

"Explain Hegel's laws of dialectics to a 17-year-old guy."

It would seem, what could be simpler. It knows who Hegel is. It knows the main tenets of dialectics. It knows examples. It can write a perfectly competent text.

First result: formally correct, practically bad

The first version turns out to be something like this:

Dialectics considers development through the interaction of opposites. Contradiction is the source of movement and development. Quantitative changes under certain conditions lead to qualitative leaps. Development occurs through the negation of the previous state and the emergence of a new one...

Everything seems correct. But there is one problem. Our guy might ask:

What does contradiction mean? Dialectics? Interaction of opposites?

And these would be perfectly normal questions.

The problem was not that the neural network knew Hegel poorly. The problem was that I asked it to explain to a person who does not exist....

Reformulating the task

This is where I realized that I hadn't given the AI a real person to understand who it was explaining to. Age is not yet a description of a person.

So I added context to the model.

The guy studies in college, practices martial arts, is interested in the biographies of successful people, asks a lot about life, and wants to achieve something himself.

He has little life experience, but a lot of curiosity.

And there is one peculiarity: he tends to perceive information quite literally.

And I added one more important feature:

He perceives information quite literally.

That is, if you say:

"Contradictions are the engine of development,"

do not expect him to understand on his own what "contradiction," "movement," and "development" mean in a philosophical sense.

First, you need to explain the words themselves.

For example:

Opposite — these are two things that are on different sides. For example: strong and weak, many and few, hot and cold.

Then show that a philosophical contradiction is not necessarily a quarrel between two people. You can take a familiar situation: you want to train more to get stronger faster, but the body needs recovery. You have to consider both sides.

And only then use the word "opposite" further.

This seems like a small thing. But it is precisely these small things that determine whether a person will understand the text or not.

But another problem arose

After the first clarification, I looked at the new result and discovered another error.

The neural network explained several of Hegel's ideas well, but missed one of the main laws — the transition of quantity into quality.

And I wrote to it:

"WHAT THE F*CK DID YOU MISS........"

However, these are my own developments in communicating with AI. So I'll write differently:

"You missed one of the main laws of dialectics — quantity into quality."

And I asked it to redo the explanation. It turned out to be a different text.

For example, quantity and quality

Instead of a philosophical definition, you can start with training.

You did one workout. It changed almost nothing. You did ten. Then fifty. Then a hundred. Gradually, you become a different athlete. And here the idea appears:

Many small changes can gradually lead to a big change.

Even simpler — water.

20 degrees.
30 → 31 → 32 → 33....... 100° boiling

It wasn't just another small temperature change.

The state of the water changed.

That is:

the quantity of changes gradually accumulated → a qualitative change occurred.

For a specific person, such an example is much clearer than a textbook definition.

A good result came from a bad one

Let's see what happened. First, I asked to explain dialectics to a 17-year-old person.

Then I clarified, to which specific person. Then I indicated what was missing in the explanation.

And I asked to correct the result.

It turned out to be a fairly simple working cycle:

TASK
↓
RESULT
↓
I see what's wrong
↓
FEEDBACK
↓
ADDING CONTEXT
↓
NEW RESULT
↓
CHECKING

And the cycle repeats. This is different from the approach:

"Write me the perfect prompt"

I don't try to come up with a request in advance that will somehow magically make the model produce the ideal result immediately. I let it make the first attempt.

And then I use the first attempt to better formulate the task itself.

Why it works

Because sometimes a person doesn't know in advance exactly what they need from the neural network. They only understand it after seeing the first result. For example, before the answer, I could have said:

"Explain it simply."

And after the answer, I realized:

"No. Not simply. You need to explain it to a person who understands words literally, doesn't know complex terms, and has no philosophical education."

That is, the first version of the answer becomes a kind of task diagnosis. It shows me what is missing in my own description.

The neural network won't do everything for you

There is an important point here that is sometimes lost in conversations about AI. You can discuss prompts, context windows, and new models as much as you want. But if a person doesn't understand the problem at all, the neural network won't necessarily save them.

Suppose I know nothing about philosophy. Although I personally studied it in two institutes.

I ask:

"Explain Hegel to me"

The neural network produces text. I read it. And how do I check if everything is correct? I don't know.

If I don't understand the topic, I can't properly distinguish:

  • an important thought from a secondary one;

  • a correct explanation from beautiful nonsense;

  • a missed part from an unnecessary one;

  • an error from normal simplification.

Therefore, working with a neural network requires at least a minimal understanding of the subject. You don't have to be a professor. But you need to understand:

what I want to get and how I will know if the result is incorrect

In the Hegel example, I was able to notice the missed law of quantity and quality precisely because I had an idea of what should be in the result.

It turns out a strange thing

The neural network simultaneously:

saves you work

and

makes you better understand your own task.

Because before, you could say:

"Well, it seems okay."

But when working with the model, there is an opportunity to constantly ask:

"What exactly is wrong here?"

And the better you can answer this question, the better the next result will be.

Therefore, I stopped thinking of a prompt as a command

For me, a good request is no longer:

"Do X in the style of Y."

It's more of a description of the situation:

Here's what I want to get. Here's who it's for. Here's what this person already knows. Here's what they don't know. Here are their characteristics. Here's what I didn't like about the previous result. Here's what must remain. Here's what needs to be changed.

That is, I'm giving the neural network a model of the task, not just a command.

And everyone talks more about how AI will change humans, implying more physical transformation, but changes are already happening: individuals are becoming smarter and can do things they couldn't before.

For example

I've been with neural networks since their inception. I've gone from bots in Telegram to creating my own Windows application. From idea to packaging into a ready-made build, you can read about it here.

Perhaps, therefore, it is more useful to talk not about "prompting," but about "working with a neural network."

Prompting creates the impression that there is a correct magical formulation.

Write it — get the ideal result. My experience so far shows a different picture.

A good result often comes not from an ideal first request, but from several cycles of normal joint work.

You set a task. Get a version. Criticize. Clarify. Provide additional context. Check. Repeat. And gradually you get exactly what you needed.

This is exactly what, and not only this, I teach people who work with AI and don't get results.

Instead of a conclusion

It seems to me that one of the most interesting possibilities of modern neural networks is not that they can replace a specialist.

But that a specialist can get a second brain to work on their task.

You bring your knowledge, experience, requirements, and mistakes.

The neural network helps to process, check, reformulate, and develop all this.

But if you yourself don't understand what you are doing and why, a magic prompt likely doesn't exist.

Therefore, I now approach working with a neural network:

Don't try to write the perfect request the first time. Give the task, look at the result, and use it to better explain the next task

Sometimes the best way to teach a neural network to understand you is to first let it misunderstand you. And talk to it, pour out everything that's on your mind. As silly as it may sound.

Teach it bad — so that it learns good.
