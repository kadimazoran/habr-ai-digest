Zeta and Beyond

To understand what the Riemann quasi-hypothesis is about, one must first understand the Riemann hypothesis itself. When the Clay Mathematics Institute formulated its seven Millennium Prize Problems at the turn of the century, the Riemann hypothesis stood out even among the other six. It was the only problem carried over from the famous list of crucial mathematical problems that David Hilbert had presented a century earlier.

At first glance, the hypothesis concerns the behavior of a particular function. Start with the sum
1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/4 + .... Now use this sum (along with a special mathematical trick that extends its domain) to construct a more general function called the Riemann zeta function (ζ):

Here, the argument s is a complex number. Complex numbers consist of two parts: a "real" part and an "imaginary" part, the latter being a multiple of the square root of −1 (denoted by the letter i). Any complex number can be represented as a point on a plane:

The Riemann hypothesis is about for which values of s the zeta function equals zero.

But it actually touches on much more than just the behavior of this particular function. As Bernhard Riemann, who first formulated the hypothesis in 1859, understood, the function is connected to the patterns of distribution of prime numbers. These are the integers divisible only by 1 and themselves, which many mathematicians call the "atoms of arithmetic."

It has long been known that as one moves along the number line, primes become rarer. Riemann showed that the nature of this thinning is, in a certain sense, determined by the zeta function—specifically, by the values of its argument for which it equals zero.

It is easy to prove that if s is a negative even integer (−2, −4, −6, . . .), then ζ(s) = 0. These are called the trivial zeros. The Riemann hypothesis states that all other zeros of the zeta function, called nontrivial zeros, occur only when the real part of s is 1/2. In other words, they should all lie on a single vertical line:

If even one nontrivial zero were found off this line, it would mean that primes must follow an extremely unexpected pattern of distribution, one that no one has observed before.

Even simply narrowing the region where nontrivial zeros can lie has enormous consequences. Riemann himself proved that the nontrivial zeros of the zeta function can only lie within the vertical strip between 0 and 1. Then, in 1896, two mathematicians showed that the zeros cannot lie on the boundaries of this strip. A direct consequence was the proof of an important result known as the prime number theorem. It provides a formula describing how quickly, on average, the density of primes decreases.

If the Riemann hypothesis is true, mathematicians can guarantee that any deviations from the average distribution described by the prime number theorem will always be small.

Mathematicians have numerically verified that the first 12 trillion nontrivial zeros lie on the 1/2 line. However, they cannot be sure that the hypothesis holds for all zeros. Even a single exception would have the effect of a bombshell. As Enrico Bombieri wrote in a paper on the formulation of the problem for the Millennium Prize: "A disproof of the Riemann hypothesis would cause chaos in the distribution of primes."

Diamonds in the Rough

And here we come to the Riemann quasi-hypothesis. If the Riemann hypothesis states that all nontrivial zeros lie on the vertical line 1/2, then its "quasi" version only requires proving that they all lie within a vertical strip narrower than the already known strip between 0 and 1. The narrower this strip, the more can be said about the distribution of primes.

OpenAI presented two different versions of the proof of the Riemann quasi-hypothesis. The first version, dated September 30, 2026, claimed to prove that all nontrivial zeros must lie between 1/8 and 7/8. It underwent formal verification using the Lean programming language. The second version, completed almost a week later (and not yet formally verified), claimed to prove a weaker result: the real part of all zeros must lie between 1/12 and 11/12. However, while the first paper is 199 pages long, the second is comparatively short: only 49 pages.

Like many other recent AI-generated results, both papers "are written terribly (so much so that any math journal would immediately reject them)," Radziwill wrote in an email. "In terms of presentation and clarity, they fall far short of the standards in our field." Therefore, mathematicians have set aside the 199-page work with the 7/8 result for now and are focusing on the proof with the 11/12 result, which appears to use similar methods and have similar consequences. "The reason people are mostly studying the 11/12 result is that the work is shorter," Radziwill explained.

Interestingly, the result with the 11/12 bound was the only one of OpenAI's 722 papers released on October 6 that was edited by a human. In response to questions from Quanta, the company stated, "This paper was the result of the work of all OpenAI employees."

Within two weeks of its publication, mathematicians were intensely studying the paper. Experts generally agree that the result is correct, and its novelty lies in the unification of ideas from number theory that have been known for quite some time.

For decades, Sarnak has argued that mathematicians should approach the Riemann hypothesis by viewing the zeta function as just one element of a broader family of functions and then proving statements about the entire family. "However, it was unclear exactly how to do this and which family to consider," said Michael Rubinstein of the University of Waterloo.

As it turned out, Sarnak's intuition was correct. However, the family discovered by the OpenAI model had previously gone unnoticed. Metaplectic forms have been used relatively rarely in number theory until now, he added, "so I hope this beautiful and esoteric theory will receive the attention it deserves."
