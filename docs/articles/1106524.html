Operator-Side Noise Suppression

The first presentation was given by Maxim Maslennikov, Cluster Lead Architect at MWS. He discussed a system that removes extraneous sounds during phone calls directly within the operator's network. Unlike conventional noise suppression in smartphones and headphones, this system does not require special applications or support from the phone itself.

The idea arose after an incident in the metro. One of the company's executives couldn't have a phone conversation due to the surrounding noise, prompting the team to investigate whether voice could be cleaned not on the device itself, but before it reached the recipient.

The implementation proved more complex than the idea itself. In 2G and 3G networks, voice is transmitted over a dedicated channel, while in LTE, calls use IMS – an IP-based multimedia subsystem. This allows voice to be transmitted as digital packets over the data network. Since older phones and previous generation networks continue to operate, the solution had to be adapted for two different voice transmission methods.

For 2G and 3G networks, the team considered routing calls through a special prefix. For LTE, they took a different approach: they integrated their own media service into the operator's network. The voice stream passes through this service, and the subscriber doesn't need to change their number or install any additional software.

The main limitation of such a system is processing time. Voice is transmitted in small packets, and each packet must be received, converted into a format suitable for the neural network, cleaned of noise, and then sent on. The entire process has about 20 ms. If the delay exceeds this, the interlocutors will start hearing clicks, disconnections, or other communication distortions.

Furthermore, the neural network must operate not on a separate recording, but on a continuous conversation. The model considers previous speech fragments, so it cannot simply be restarted for each new packet. Developers had to adapt the processing to maintain state between fragments and avoid disrupting the conversation flow.

Off-the-shelf models also required modification. The team took several open-source solutions for speech enhancement and began training them on recordings of real-world noise. Ultimately, the system was tested in various conditions, including heavy background noise and near an operating compressor.

Another challenge was to avoid consuming excessive GPU resources for processing each conversation with the same intensity. To address this, developers added a small model that continuously analyzes the voice stream and determines when more intensive noise suppression is needed. If conditions improve, the main model can be deactivated, and only the lightweight algorithm remains active. When scaling, it's also important to consider equipment placement: the further the processing node is from the subscriber, the harder it is to stay within the acceptable delay.

However, the method has its drawbacks: after processing, the voice changed slightly but remained understandable. According to Maxim Maslennikov, the solution is already in industrial use. MWS is considering offering it to other operators, but the company will not disclose the models or their weights. Among the next tasks, the speaker mentioned restoring voice data lost during network transmission.

AI Agents for Architectural Documentation

The second presentation was given by Evgeny Artemyev, Stream Lead Architect at MWS. He demonstrated a tool based on AI agents and the Architecture-as-a-Code approach, designed to keep architectural documentation up-to-date.

The problem arises when actual development deviates from the initial design. Teams change APIs, add microservices, and introduce new integrations, but they don't always manage to update the architectural documents accordingly. Over time, the system's description ceases to reflect its actual state.

The MWS tool collects information from development repositories and DevOps, Jira tasks, and architectural materials in Confluence. One agent analyzes changes and gathers relevant information, while another consolidates it and prepares proposals for documentation updates. An orchestrator manages task distribution, and a separate layer removes sensitive data before sending it to the model.

Evgeny Artemyev compared two approaches to organizing this work. In the first case, developers predefine the sequence of actions (deterministic approach) and available tools, making the system's behavior predictable at each step. In the non-deterministic approach, the system itself decides which steps to take. In an experiment with Claude, this latter option proved less predictable: the model consumed more tokens, performed unnecessary actions, and adhered less strictly to the specified structure.

LangGraph is used to manage the process. It defines the sequence of stages and the allowed transitions between them. Multiple agents can work in parallel, reducing task completion time. The downside of this approach is more complex debugging: developers need to monitor the state of each agent and save intermediate results.

It is equally important to control the amount of information the model processes. This can be done using regular expressions or a separate classifier model. The latter option requires additional computation. In one project, checking on a CPU took three to four seconds, even though no more than 500 ms was allocated for it.

The result of the work is YAML and Markdown files. These files allow for identifying outdated technologies, analyzing inter-system dependencies, and assessing risks. The architect then only needs to review the proposed changes and decide whether to implement them. The tool handles the collection of information from various sources and the preparation of updates.

The "Hypothesis Map" and Business Goals

The third part of the meeting was led by Alexander Byndyu, an entrepreneur, methodologist, and writer. He discussed the "Hypothesis Map" – a methodology that helps connect a company's goals, internal capabilities, and external conditions with specific strategic bets.

Alexander emphasized that simply accelerating work processes does not guarantee an increase in business efficiency. For example, if an employee starts spending five minutes preparing an email instead of two hours, it saves time but doesn't necessarily increase the company's revenue. In his opinion, a significant economic effect emerges when technologies change not individual operations, but the very approach to doing business.

Examples included remote control of multiple cranes by a single operator and fields as a service, where machinery operates along a route using GPS and AI.

The "Hypothesis Map" is based on a linked SWOT analysis. The company assesses its own strengths and weaknesses, considers external trends and potential threats, and then formulates strategic decisions based on this. It is important that each idea is supported not only by market opportunities but also by the organization's actual resources. This approach helps avoid wasting time on seemingly attractive initiatives that the company is not yet capable of implementing.

Work begins with setting a goal and defining metrics that reflect the current situation and the desired outcome. According to Alexander, a strategy should help achieve complex goals under conditions of limited resources and uncertainty.

The practical application was demonstrated using the example of Vasilisa-Victoria Shikerina, who specializes in solution architecture for business process transformation. Her task involved identifying the competitor market and working with contracts. The case showed that AI can create a "Hypothesis Map" from a small PDF file and get close to reality, and with full data input, the analysis will be more accurate. However, the quality of the result directly depends on how precisely the goal is formulated. An error in the goal leads to incorrect metrics, which is immediately apparent when compared to a benchmark result. The practical benefit here is that such a tool quickly highlights weaknesses in task formulation. It doesn't hide errors but makes them noticeable, and corrections take minutes instead of days.

Byndyu then demonstrated how AI works with GitHub skills. The model asked clarifying questions, generated a "Map," and a prototype interface. At one point, the system made an error in identifying the subject, and it had to be corrected manually. When participants discovered that one of the trends didn't actually exist, they changed the input data and, in about ten minutes, obtained a new prototype version based on the revised input. The value of the prototype was not in the quality of its implementation, but in the speed of feedback. From an IT perspective, a prototype built in fifteen minutes might not be perfect, but from a business perspective, it allows for quickly testing an idea and immediately adjusting the underlying assumptions.

A complete "Map" can include overlapping goals, departmental strategies, and negative actors for whom counter-strategies are needed. Some connections are still configured manually. The user must understand the methodology to guide the model and evaluate its suggestions.

Ideas for New Products

In the final part of the meetup, participants proposed business scenarios based on MWS technologies. Among the ideas were a manager's assistant that monitors conversations and reminds about their goals, a voice pseudo-notary for clarifying and recording agreement terms, an advanced answering machine, a voice concierge, and a translator. They also discussed call routing based on conversation topic and client's emotional state. A separate idea was an assistant for interacting with government services: it could independently make calls and wait on hold, connecting the person only when their participation is required.

The common goal of these solutions is to relieve people of some routine work during phone communication. Participants also had to determine how new services would be more useful than existing alternatives and in which situations automation would genuinely simplify interaction rather than adding another intermediate tool. For some scenarios, such as recording agreements, the question of legal validity of the outcome also remains open.

Conclusions

The three main themes of the meeting demonstrated that the application of AI depends on the specific environment. In mobile networks, latency and computational resource availability are critical. In architectural work, context quality and agent controllability are key. In strategic planning, the accuracy of goals and initial hypotheses are paramount.

Moreover, in none of the scenarios discussed does the model operate in isolation. It must be integrated into existing processes, have its available actions constrained, and its results verified. The more autonomy a neural network is given, the more important control and clear evaluation criteria become.

Therefore, the practical value of such solutions is determined not by the number of models and agents involved, but by the task they help solve and how reliably they perform it in real-world conditions.
