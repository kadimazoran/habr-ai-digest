Kumquat is an open-source (MIT) file converter that lives in the macOS menu bar. It runs on Apple Silicon with macOS 14 and newer, with an interface in Russian and English.

Let's talk about co-authorship right away. The application was made by me (kipperstudio) together with Claude, an AI model from Anthropic. Claude wrote most of the code under my guidance, and I came up with ideas, set tasks, manually checked, and decided what to release. At the end of the article, there's a separate section on how it was done and where the AI made mistakes.

The Problem

For me, it looked like this: several times a day, I need not just to open a file, but to do something with it. Convert a photo from my iPhone to a format the website understands. Compress a screen recording to an email limit. Extract text from a screenshot with an error. Merge scans of a contract into a single document. Remove geotags before uploading photos.

Each of these small tasks turned into a mini-quest: find a suitable utility or web service, upload the file there (often quite personal), set the settings, and download the result back. I wanted the action to be where the file already is: under the cursor, at the moment I'm dragging it.

The Gesture Idea: Drag-and-Drop Plus a Short Shift Press

Drag-and-drop on macOS already means 'I want to do something with this file.' All that's missing is the question 'what exactly.' Therefore, the gesture is as follows:

  1. Start dragging files from anywhere: from Finder, from the desktop, from email.

  2. Without releasing the mouse button, briefly press Shift.

  3. A wheel appears under the cursor. Holding over a category expands it, holding over the center returns you back.

  4. Drop the files onto a petal. The result is saved next to the original; the original is not changed.

Why a wheel instead of a regular menu? In a radial menu, all items are equidistant from the cursor, and the direction is remembered by muscle memory: after a week, your hand automatically goes 'up' to convert. Moreover, you can't click while dragging, only move and release, and the wheel is perfect for 'point and drop.'

The wheel only shows options applicable to all dragged files simultaneously. If you drag HEIC and MOV together, the 'to MP3' option won't appear.

How to Catch the Gesture Without Accessibility

The most obvious approach is a global CGEventTap or NSEvent.addGlobalMonitorForEvents. However, monitoring keyboard input in other applications requires Accessibility or Input Monitoring permissions. For a file converter, asking the user for access to all keystrokes is overkill, and I wouldn't install such an app myself.

It turned out that everything needed can be obtained by polling the public state, without any special permissions:

  • NSEvent.pressedMouseButtons – whether the mouse button is currently pressed;

  • NSEvent.modifierFlags – which modifiers are held down;

  • NSPasteboard(name: .drag) – the system drag-and-drop pasteboard. When a drag starts, its changeCount changes, and it contains file URLs.

The timer ticks infrequently (every 120 ms) while the mouse button is released and switches to 60 Hz as soon as the button is pressed. Here's the core of DragGestureMonitor.swift:

The key detail is comparing the changeCount with the moment the button was pressed. Simply checking 'there are files in the pasteboard' is not enough: data from previous drag-and-drop operations remains there.

A Tap, Not a Hold

Second point: people press Shift during drag-and-drop for other reasons too. Therefore, the wheel only opens on a short press (up to 0.6 seconds) of exactly one modifier. If you press Shift+Option during the process, it's not our gesture. Which modifier opens the wheel is configurable.

pressedUnion accumulates all modifiers that were held down during a single press, so 'Shift, then added Cmd, then released everything' is correctly discarded.

A Panel That Accepts Drops and Doesn't Steal Focus

The wheel is an NSPanel with the style [.borderless, .nonactivatingPanel] at the .popUpMenu level. Non-activating means Finder remains the active application, and the drag-and-drop operation is not interrupted. The panel itself is registered as a drag-and-drop receiver, so files drop directly onto the petal, not 'somewhere below' the window. It only becomes the key window in click mode (when the wheel is opened from the menu or via a hotkey) so that Esc works.

There are other entry points: a menu bar item, Finder › Services, a hotkey ⌃⌥K for selected files in Finder, observed folders, and the command line. But drag-and-drop with Shift is the main scenario for which everything was conceived.

The Wheel: Geometry and Glass

The geometry lives in a separate, clean FanLayout structure without any dependencies on AppKit views, making it easy to test. Each petal is a circular sector. Several decisions grew out of practice:

  • Minimum of three slots. If there's one or two actions, they are still drawn as petals in a three-slot ring, not a full donut. Two petals are placed symmetrically relative to the top.

  • Two rings. When there are more than ten items, they are divided between an inner and outer ring proportionally to the circumference, so that the petal arcs are roughly equal. The outer ring is rotated by half a petal to avoid matching seams.

  • Even gap. There's a constant gap of 5 pt between adjacent petals, not a constant angle. For this, the sector edges are shifted by asin(gap / 2r) separately for the inner and outer arcs, otherwise the gaps at the center collapse.

  • Rounded corners. The sector is first reduced by the rounding radius, then its 2r-wide stroke with round joins is added. This results in a neat rounded petal without manual arc manipulation.

  • Delays. A category expands after 0.32s of hovering, returning via the center triggers after 0.45s. Less causes accidental triggers when the cursor passes over, more causes a feeling of lag.

The glass effect is achieved using NSVisualEffectView with the .hudWindow material and blendingMode = .behindWindow. To ensure the blur is only under the petals, all paths are combined and drawn into a maskImage. The shadow is a separate view with a shadowPath of the same shape. The wheel is always in dark mode, and each category has its own color from a palette: conversion – kumquat colors, optimization – leaf, editing – honey, and so on.

Engines

Inside Kumquat is a set of engines under the common ConversionEngine protocol. What can be converted to what is described in a single switch in ConversionCatalog.swift, and the wheel is built from it. For example, images can be converted to JPG, PNG, HEIC, WebP, AVIF, TIFF, BMP, GIF, and PDF, and if the build lacks a WebP encoder, that option simply disappears.

For multiple files, commonOutputs(for:) takes the intersection of the lists. The main principle is to use system frameworks as much as possible, and third-party code only where absolutely necessary.

Images are processed via ImageIO and Core Image. A particular pain point is EXIF orientation: a photo from a phone is stored as 400x300 with a 'rotate 90°' tag, but displayed as 300x400. If you forget about this, the converted image will be sideways. The tests include a specific fixture for this exact case.

Vision is responsible for text recognition (Russian and English), face detection for blurring, and background removal using VNGenerateForegroundInstanceMaskRequest. Everything is local; nothing is sent over the network.

Keynote, Pages, Numbers are converted without the iWork applications themselves. Old iWork '09 documents contain a ready-made QuickLook/Preview.pdf. Newer ones only store a preview of the first page, so Kumquat requests the system Quick Look generator (qlmanage -p -o) to render all pages into a vector PDF and merges them.

EPUB and office formats are rendered to PDF using an offscreen WKWebView with JavaScript disabled and all non-local requests blocked. EPUB 3 is assembled manually on output: one chapter per <h1>, the mimetype as the first file without compression, plus NCX for older readers.

XLSX tables are read and written without third-party libraries: OOXML is a ZIP archive containing XML, and the system /usr/bin/zip and unzip are sufficient for packaging. It was also necessary to learn how to understand which cell number formats are actually dates.

Plus interactive editors: screenshot markup, coloring, photo correction, PDF pages, video and audio trimming on a timeline, 'beeping' and blurring areas in videos.

Video Twice as Fast: VideoToolbox vs. libx264

The built-in ffmpeg is compiled under LGPL, which means it doesn't include libx264 and libx265 (they are GPL). H.264 and HEVC are encoded hardware-accelerated via VideoToolbox, Apple Silicon's media engine. The question was how to compare quality: x264 has the familiar CRF, while h264_videotoolbox uses a constant quality -q:v from 1 to 100 with a completely different scale.

Conclusions:

  • VideoToolbox is 1.5–2.7 times faster, and its time is almost independent of quality: about 7 seconds for any preset.

  • You pay with bytes: at equal VMAF, a file from VideoToolbox is 0–40% larger. It's worst at low bitrates, where x264 with its motion analysis wins noticeably.

  • For compression to a target size ('no more than 25 MB'), a limited average bitrate is used. Files are within 96–98.5% of the target, meaning they don't exceed the limit.

These six pairs were incorporated into the code as reference points, between which vtQuality(crf:hevc:) interpolates linearly. For HEVC, +5 is added to the quality: at the same -q:v, HEVC via VideoToolbox yields a lower VMAF than H.264.

The hardware encoder is not omnipotent. H.264 in VideoToolbox does not support frames wider than 4096 px, and the encoder session may fail to create.
