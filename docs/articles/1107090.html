Let's start with a small experiment. Here are three pictures: a mousetrap, a matchbox, and an aardvark. Which one is the odd one out? A task for a couple of seconds: the first two items were made by humans, and the third is a living creature.

Now let's try to offer the same to a multimodal neural network. The chances of getting an answer that matches a human's will not be that high. In the researchers' experiment, Qwen3-VL family models showed a result of 37% to 41% when directly queried. For comparison, random choice in such a task gives 33.3%.

You will, of course, object: the neural network was not created to play 'Third Wheel'. It analyzes images, recognizes objects, works with text, and handles more complex tasks. And the results of popular multimodal benchmarks for modern models are quite decent.

It's just that the ability to answer questions correctly doesn't mean the model sees the world the same way we do. It recognizes the mousetrap, the matchbox, and the aardvark, but it might choose the odd one out based on a completely different criterion. And it's not a given that this criterion will seem logical to us.

This is what the authors of the study Aligning MLLMs with the Latent Structure of Human Cognition are exploring. Usually, multimodal large language models (MLLM) are trained to produce answers that people like. Scientists propose embedding the real patterns of human perception into the training process.

To do this, the researchers took data from a large-scale psychophysical experiment, where millions of human judgments are reduced to 66 semantic dimensions, and built the CogAlign framework on them. Its task is to change the very approach to training neural networks: to teach the model the grounds on which a human chooses. Let's figure out how it works. 

Why standard alignment boils down to answer training

When developers talk about alignment, they almost always mean methods like RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) or DPO (Direct Preference Optimization). Both approaches work well when you need to stop a chatbot from being rude to a user, make it produce neat lists, and follow an instruction format. But from a cognitive science perspective, this is just superficial mimicry.

In RLHF, humans compare model responses and choose the good ones. A separate reward model is trained on these ratings, which then fine-tunes the neural network. DPO bypasses the intermediary: the model learns directly from pairs of good and rejected responses.

Both methods are useful, no doubt. But they have a peculiarity that the authors of CogAlign point out: the final answer matching human preferences doesn't say anything about the path the model took to reach that result.

Imagine we show a neural network three objects and ask it to pick the odd one out. It answers correctly. Great, check the box. But why did it choose that object? Because it noticed that the other two were made of metal? Or because similar images in its training data were more often encountered in a specific context?

In the first case, the model thinks like us. In the second, it guessed, and we'll never know if it got lucky or latched onto something we don't see in the picture.

Hence the idea of CogAlign: to teach the neural network, in addition to correct answers, also what a human looks at when choosing the odd one out.

However, don't conclude that RLHF or DPO are incapable of teaching models to reason. This is about something else: standard preference tuning doesn't guarantee that the neural network will learn the human grounds for choice. CogAlign is precisely trying to fill this gap.

A map of human perception in 66 dimensions

A logical question arises: where did these 66 dimensions come from, and why did the researchers decide they were relevant to human thinking?

The work is based on the THINGS research project, which studies how people perceive and classify objects in their environment. It collected 1854 concepts of real objects and conducted a large-scale psychophysical experiment.

Participants were asked to play the 'Third Wheel' game. They were shown three images and asked to choose the object that was least like the other two. The task is as simple as possible, and it can be repeated a huge number of times with different combinations of objects.

As a result, the researchers obtained about 4.7 million human judgments. And this is a completely different story: instead of isolated and unambiguous answers, there was a large database of what features people use when comparing objects. But how to turn these answers into an understandable model of human perception?

The researchers assigned this task to the SPoSE (Sparse Positive Similarity Embedding) algorithm. It places objects in a multidimensional space, where each object has a set of semantic features. A mousetrap, for example, will receive a high score on the 'metallic or artificial' dimension, while an aardvark will score high on the 'animal' dimension.

SPoSE produced 66 dimensions, each with a human-understandable meaning. Many are expected: food, animals, textiles, electronics, transport, plants. Others sound more unusual: round objects, fluffy and soft things, weapons and danger, medicine.

Moreover, some dimensions are quite unexpected. For example, the list includes stereotypically feminine and stereotypically masculine features. We will return to this point later.

An important point: this is not to say that scientists have discovered exactly 66 universal switches in the human brain responsible for perceiving the world. It is a mathematical model derived from human responses. It describes which semantic and visual properties help people compare objects.

The researchers decided to use this map to train multimodal models. Why one object is similar to another is no longer guessed by the AI: it has ready-made criteria.

How CogAlign turns dry vectors into chains of reasoning

CogAlign consists of three stages. The first stage doesn't require neural networks at all: everything is done by the mathematical model of human perception.

If we break it down step by step, it looks like this.

Stage 1: Identifying the main feature

Let's take any triplet from 'Third Wheel', for example, ours: a mousetrap, a matchbox, and an aardvark. Each object already has 66 scores in the SPoSE space, one for each dimension.

Then, the Jackknife method comes into play. The algorithm sequentially removes one dimension at a time from the calculation and observes how the probability that the remaining pair will be the most similar changes.

If removing a certain feature changes the result the most, it means that feature played the most important role in the choice. It is then taken as the basis for further explanation.

For example, when comparing two artificial objects and an animal, the 'man-made' feature might be important. In another triplet, the deciding factor might be that one object is related to food, and the other two are not.

Stage 2: Translating the mathematical feature into human language

Getting a label like 'belongs to animals' or 'metallic' is not enough. It's just a short designation that doesn't explain anything on its own.

Therefore, the authors use a regular LLM, to which they provide a description of the three objects and the identified semantic dimension. The model's task is to create a coherent explanation showing how this feature influences the human choice.

This is how Chain-of-Thought (CoT) textual reasoning chains are created. The generative model at this stage should not independently determine the correct answer. It receives the already identified cognitive criterion and turns it into an understandable justification.

Stage 3: Fine-tuning the multimodal model

Now the researchers have everything they need: images, the correct answer, and an explanation of the feature used for selection. They fine-tune the model on this data using Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), i.e., on ready-made examples with explained answers.

The resulting dataset contains 309,329 examples. Of these, about 80,000 relate directly to the cognitive tasks of THINGS, and the rest are needed to preserve general multimodal skills.

The latter point, by the way, is the most important. If you endlessly train an AI to solve one specific task, you can get an excellent specialist in finding the odd object, who will perform significantly worse at everything else. Therefore, tasks such as image recognition, storytelling, finding differences, and other multimodal tasks were added to the training set.

In other words, the authors want to preserve the existing skills of the model and add another way of processing information to them — relying on human semantic features.

Why rules cannot be set in a prompt

Frankly, all this might seem excessive, and a lot of hassle. Why fine-tune when you can load a list of 66 dimensions into the system prompt and ask the neural network to consider them when answering?

In fact, the researchers asked themselves the same question and tested it. They were not pleased with the result.

The Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct model was used. With a standard chain of reasoning, it achieved 53.37% accuracy. When forced to reason through one selected semantic dimension, accuracy dropped to 49.01%, and with all 66 dimensions at once — to 52.70%.

This means that listing cognitive features in the prompt did not help. And in one of the variants, it significantly worsened the result.

The generation itself also faltered. Researchers found that with such prompts, models periodically entered repetitive cycles. Instead of choosing a suitable criterion and finishing the reasoning, they began to list all sorts of positive and negative characteristics of the objects. And they continued to do so until the tokens ran out.

This glitch looks particularly amusing with the aardvark. The model lists: the animal is not metallic, not artificial, not flat, not green, and so on. Each fact individually is correct, but almost none of it relates to the task. The neural network has more information, but it hasn't learned to use it.

This is the difference between a prompt and fine-tuning. With a prompt, we inform the model about the existing features and hope it applies them appropriately. Fine-tuning shows through numerous examples how a feature is related to an explanation and the final answer.

The authors specifically checked where the increase comes from: from training on explanations or from the fact that there are more chains of reasoning. When full explanations were replaced with short semantic dimension labels, the accuracy of Qwen3-VL-4B dropped to 42.81%. I don't know how else to more clearly show that a list of features is not enough.

What happened in practice

The main experiments were conducted with the Qwen3-VL family in three configurations: 4B, 8B, and 30B-A3B. The latter is built on the Mixture of Experts architecture.

The difference is obvious. Even a standard chain of reasoning significantly increases the match with human choices, and CogAlign provides an additional boost for all three models.

Separately, researchers compared the results with Centaur — a special 70 billion parameter model trained on a large dataset of human behavior. It showed 51.96%. All three versions of Qwen3-VL after CogAlign were ahead.

Other architectures were also tested. On a dataset of approximately 75.6 thousand tasks, Qwen2.5-VL-7B improved its result from 56.63% to 59.29%, and Gemma3-12B — from 55.77% to 57.28%.

But it would be wrong to say that after such training, the models became better at absolutely everything.

The authors separately tested MMMU and MMBench — two popular multimodal benchmarks. On MMMU, the results improved for all three versions of Qwen3-VL. However, on MMBench, a slight decrease was observed: for example, the 4B model's score changed from 82.96% to 81.39%.

On the other hand, the results on tasks requiring understanding of physical properties of objects are particularly interesting. In the corresponding MMBench category, the Qwen3-VL-4B model improved from 67% to 75%, and the 8B version — from 61% to 77%. This is a good argument for the fact that working with human semantic features can be helpful beyond the 'Third Wheel' game.

But the most unusual experiment was the fMRI check.

For this, researchers used an independent dataset NSD (Natural Scenes Dataset), which records the brain activity of people looking at images. The model and the brain were compared using the searchlight RSA (Representational Similarity Analysis) method. It shows how similarly the neural network and individual brain regions group and differentiate the same images.

After CogAlign, researchers observed a positive shift in similarity between the internal representations of Qwen3-VL and human neural activity. Moreover, changes were observed in all four study participants for whom the corresponding analysis was conducted.

However, one point must be considered. No one connected the model to a tomograph and discovered human thoughts in its layers. I am speaking solely about the mathematical similarity of representations, which was measured by an independent method.

But the very fact that such similarity increased after training is interesting.

Stereotypes come as a package

And finally. The CogAlign work has an interesting feature: to improve multimodal models, researchers used data from a psychological experiment, although usually in such cases, another huge dataset with pictures and captions is collected. And even a simple odd-one-out game helped the models answer more often like humans.

At the same time, I would not consider CogAlign a ready-made way to make AI think exactly like us. The study itself has limitations: the authors choose only one main dimension for each triplet, although in real life, several features can influence our decision simultaneously. Furthermore, these 66 dimensions primarily describe objects and their properties. This approach still needs to be tested for abstract mathematical or geometric tasks.

There is another problem. Remember the stereotypically feminine and masculine features from the list of dimensions? Along with useful object physics, the neural network learns stereotypes from the answers of thousands of people — for example, gender axes when evaluating clothing or household items. Therefore, cognitive datasets will likely need to be carefully filtered and checked before being used as the basis for base models.

This means we first need to decide which human thinking habits we pass on to AI, and which are better left out of the training set.

We are used to evaluating neural networks by how well they answer questions. CogAlign suggests looking at the grounds on which the model chooses an answer. I think it will be a long time before we see clear results in this direction, and what emerges in the end might truly frighten us with a machine uprising.
