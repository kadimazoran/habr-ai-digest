Where Can You Grow?

To avoid a terminology war in the comments, let's synchronize our dictionaries.

There are 3 ways of career growth: horizontal, vertical, and diagonal. However, they are presented differently in various sources. I differentiate them based on three criteria:

  • Does the function change, e.g., from marketing to analytics or development?

  • Does the domain change, e.g., from fintech to edtech or medtech?

  • Does the level, grade, or position increase?

Vertical growth is the classic climb up the hierarchical ladder. Within it, there are:

  • Expert track: analyst → senior → lead → expert. Or trainee → junior → middle → senior → lead. With each new level, the complexity of tasks, autonomy, and the weight of your opinion in disputed decisions increase. You still perform analytics hands-on, you just do it better than the previous levels.

  • Management track: you are no longer responsible for your own tasks, but for how others work. After senior comes lead, then head of a department, then director, and so on.

Horizontal growth means you expand your arsenal of skills and knowledge without vertical advancement, and with a comparable level of responsibility. For example, a business analyst switches to a system analyst role, learns data analysis, or changes domains.

Diagonal growth involves changes in both function and position. For instance, you move to a different role with a promotion.

By the way, I wrote in detail on Habr about our approach to horizontal and diagonal growth, the system analyst academy. Read it if you're interested in how colleagues from other roles transition into analytics and gain a new development track.

The Vertical Growth Ladder

Vertical growth in an analytical role at RT Labs consists of 7 steps:

  • Analyst

  • Senior Analyst

  • Lead Analyst

  • Expert Analyst

  • Head of Analytics Department

  • Director of Analytics

  • Deputy Technical Director for Analytics

As you grow from junior to senior, you perform your job better. Let's take the typical work of a system analyst as an example — designing an IT product. For instance, an online store.

If you are a junior. You can design a separate feature like "Order Placement" but you need help. For example, with a full task description: what exactly is expected of you, in what sequence to perform it, in what formats, and using which tools.

The task assignment for you might look like this:

  • "Gather requirements from these sources"

  • "Format according to this template"

  • "Check against these criteria"

  • "Submit for review to this employee"

You don't make architectural decisions yourself. Your work results are always reviewed. You follow instructions and the example of more experienced colleagues. You cannot make decisions independently because you lack experience and the ability to assess risks.

The key to the next level is experience and that very "exposure." Do as many diverse tasks as possible, observe the decisions made, the reasons behind them, and their consequences, study how everything is structured and interconnected.

If you are a middle. You already know the principles and methodology of analytical work; you don't need algorithms and templates, but you adhere to them. You can design a feature or system module from start to finish. You are more autonomous, you offer good ideas, but you still cannot make decisions independently. Your work still requires a full review by more experienced colleagues.

The key to the next level is initiative and responsibility. Take ownership of parts of the product and build expertise. Maintain a knowledge base for them and share your experience and knowledge with colleagues. Continue to broaden your IT horizons by trying different technologies and approaches.

If you are a senior. You are already responsible for both the decision and the outcome, and you must consider risks and dependencies. You master several areas of responsibility within the product, where your opinion is crucial. You don't need much information at the start of a task; you can obtain it yourself and structure your work to satisfy the needs of all stakeholders. You train colleagues, help them make decisions, and review their work.

The key to the next level is the effective organization of your own work and the work of colleagues. To achieve this, propose your ideas regarding rules, methodologies, and standards.

What Changes at Management Positions

At the management level, you start creating the rules by which other people work. I'll share my experience at RT Labs, but it's important to understand that differences are company-specific.

We have three levels of analytics function managers. There are fewer fundamental differences between them than it might seem. All are responsible for the quality and efficiency of analytics, but on different scales — within a team, a block, or the entire company. Consequently, the level of decisions made differs.

Our company produces IT products. Therefore, there are production processes involving various roles, including analysts. How analysts work, what we consider quality analytics, and how we achieve efficiency in our processes — this is the responsibility of the function managers.

The tools of such a manager include standards, rules, methodologies, and instructions in the spirit of "how employees in specific roles should do their work to achieve our goals."

However, a company doesn't exist in a vacuum: market rules are constantly changing, and the company must adapt quickly. Therefore, we cannot create all work laws once and enshrine them for decades. Flexibility is achieved through iterative improvements and reflection on results. We implement something in one process, evaluate the outcome, think about what to do next, and check against circumstances.

Thus, a manager's work can be broadly divided into two layers:

  • The first layer is about developing the analytics function. It requires developing and implementing initiatives that help the company achieve its goals. For example, designing and launching a review process for work artifacts, organizing a high-quality product knowledge base and its updates, and introducing new technologies into the role's workflow.

  • The second layer is about supporting existing processes and standards. This is what helps the analytics function operate daily. For example, hiring and onboarding employees, conducting 1-on-1s, organizing professional development and employee growth, and monitoring and evaluating the effectiveness of implemented initiatives.

We've covered what function managers generally do. Now, let's differentiate by levels.

Team Lead in a Cross-Functional Team

Who they are. A player-coach. In most cases, they are not formally managers and have no direct reports. They are line employees in a production team: they do analytics themselves and help others. Usually, they are the most experienced person on the team — the one people go to for advice first. However, this is a transitional role from a line employee to a manager.

Responsibilities. Formally — nothing beyond their analytical tasks. They are not responsible for the team's analytics. In practice, they help the cross-functional team lead manage everything related to analytics: decomposing incoming tasks, distributing workload, assessing documentation quality, conducting reviews, participating in interviews, onboarding new hires, training colleagues, and writing instructions. The exact list of tasks varies from team to team, and everything is decided based on current needs.

Planning Horizon. Sprint. Issues are resolved in the moment and on demand: they don't plan anything themselves and are not directly responsible for the results of other analysts.

How they become one. Such a lead is not appointed from above: the person grows within the team and, with the manager's approval, takes on additional responsibility. The moment this happens is usually recognized retroactively: people have been coming to them with questions for six months, and only then is it given a title.

The key to the next level. Delegation and sharing expertise. It's often difficult for an expert to adapt; they continue to take on complex tasks themselves. This prevents the team from growing, the analytics lead becomes a bottleneck, and all the context resides in one person's head. Often, such a specialist is not promoted precisely because they are indispensable as an expert. Therefore, it's important to let go of the self-positioning as the only one who knows everything and can solve any problem. But this is where the difficulty of growth lies.

Block Lead

Who they are. Head of the Analytics Department. This is where "helping" ends and "being responsible" begins.

Responsibilities. For results achieved by others, and not just in words: KPIs are tied to how the analytics function operates within the entire block. Adherence to standards, quality, and efficiency metrics are their numbers. They have about 15-20 people under administrative supervision, analysts from various product teams. The fundamental difference from a team lead is that they are no longer brought tasks — they decide what and how to do to achieve target indicators.

Planning Horizon. From a quarter to a year. This is the first level where you need to think beyond the sprint.

How they become one. In various ways. We have an internal competition — preference is always given to an internal expert. If a suitable candidate is not found within the company, we look externally.

The key to the next level. Thinking in terms of company results — I'll talk more about this below. What you do should positively impact the company's metrics that management values.


The main challenge is that you are responsible for the department's metrics, but you no longer directly influence them, as you are not the one doing the analytics. Your job now is to create an environment and set up tools so that others can do their jobs well.

However, you will be held accountable if something is wrong with the analytics and someone is unhappy with the result. Therefore, the crucial skills to develop are: delegation, stopping micromanagement, building a system from individual decisions, and learning to work with goals — setting them, defending them, and understanding how you will measure whether they have been achieved.

Company Lead

Who they are. Head of the analytics function for the entire company. At RT Labs, this position is called "Deputy Technical Director for Analytics."

Responsibilities. For everything related to analytics in the company. In short: company leadership shouldn't have to worry about analytics — that's my responsibility.

I've already mentioned that the company has a global development vector set by the CEO and CTO. Based on this, as well as our function's efficiency and quality metrics and feedback from block managers, I develop the annual strategy for the function's development. Then, together with the analytics steering committee — which includes heads of analytics departments from each block — we develop initiatives to achieve our goals. For example, we look at how processes and standards are changing, how analytics is integrated into the overall production process. These initiatives are implemented in the blocks through the heads of analytics departments — employees from the previous vertical role.

Planning Horizon. A year or more.

How they become one. Appointed by leadership. This can be either an internal or external candidate — it depends on the company's current goals and plans.

Challenges of the step. I'll outline them because there is no next level in this vertical.

Two years ago, when I took on this role, the CEO told me: "Anya, now you have to think like C-level."

Therefore, I've spent these two years identifying what makes C-level thinking so different. Here are a few of my conclusions:

  • I've already mentioned thinking in terms of company results, now I'll add specifics. Any business exists as long as it is economically justified. Consequently, company leadership aims to increase net profit. This can be done in two ways:

  • By increasing revenue — selling more or at a higher price.

  • By reducing costs — producing what you sell more efficiently or lowering fixed costs to maintain company operations.

Function managers are often focused on cost reduction because they are not directly involved in sales, but they are involved in process efficiency. An example of an initiative to optimize one of the processes in my portfolio was described in the article on employee development.

  • From the point above, it follows that if at the previous level, goals like "make / create / develop / configure something" were acceptable, now the mere fact of completion is not enough. At this level, you must measure what has been done and prove that it has positively impacted the company's financial results.

    My first presentations contained many phrases like "we will act as follows" — as if I wanted to hear approval of the tactics. But at this level, I choose the tactics: no one will validate my actions or brainstorm with me on how I will achieve the desired indicators. What matters is the direction and specific goals with measurable results, linked to what the company needs at the moment.

  • You have to think in terms of at least annual results. Meanwhile, everyone who forms this result lives in sprints, at best, in quarters. You keep the horizon in mind, which almost no one around you sees, and translate it into the language of the nearest sprint — each time anew. Because of this, you have to keep several backup plans in mind in case the company's direction changes sharply due to external circumstances. At previous levels, a backup plan is needed for a project. Here, it's for the entire strategy.

Ready for the Next Step? Checklist ↓

From Analyst to Team Lead. Ask yourself:

  • When a colleague brings you a complex task, do you explain it or do it yourself because it's faster?

  • Do people already come to you for advice first — or are you just hoping they will?

  • Are you happy when someone else's specification becomes better after your review, or only when your own turns out perfect?

If you choose the first options, you are likely ready for the next step.

From Team Lead to Department Head. The questions here are tougher:

  • Are you ready to be responsible for results that you don't personally achieve?

  • Can you calmly accept that a task is done differently than you would do it — as long as the result is acceptable?

  • Can you explain to management why you need a resource, using numbers rather than phrases like "it's inconvenient"?

  • Do you have at least one goal for which you already know how you will measure its achievement?

From Department Head to Head of Function. A check on your horizon:

  • Can you articulate what should change in your function in a year, and how it relates to the company's direction or market trends?

  • Are you ready to be solely responsible for the chosen path to achieving goals and for its consequences?

  • Are you prepared to drastically change the entire year's work plan, even if you're halfway through, because the company's direction has changed?

  • Can you identify risks on an annual horizon, determine early indicators, and plan mitigation strategies long before these risks materialize?

  • Can you quantify key results and build numerical forecasts on the impact of your work on the company's business metrics?

If the answer to most questions here and in the previous checklist is "no" — it's not a verdict, but a list of things to focus on at your current level. These tasks will become your ticket to the next level.

Looking Ahead: What AI Changes

It's clear that the IT industry is on the verge of global changes. We can say we've already stepped into a new era (I'm talking about AI implementation). In this regard, you might have a reasonable question about how this will affect the ladder. Let's see what realistically has the potential for change, based on the data we already have.

Shift One: AI Replaces Not the Role, but the Executional Part of the Work

Mechanical routine is shifting to AI — and this is not a prediction, but a visible trend. According to a recent developer survey (Stack Overflow, 2026), 83% of specialists already use AI tools, and coding assistants and agents have become the most widespread application scenario — 66%. Almost three-quarters use them daily, and nearly a third of active users use them for more than 4 hours a day. This means it's not an experimental "try it out" phase, but a working reality.

Shift Two: Those Who Rebuild Processes Win

In the latest McKinsey survey "State of AI 2026", 80% of respondents report increased personal efficiency using AI, but only 37% of companies see a positive contribution to EBIT. This means individual employee successes don't automatically translate into money — until processes are restructured.

The Google Cloud DORA report "ROI of AI-assisted Software Development" also highlights the importance of rebuilding the environment: the return from AI tools is determined not by the model itself, but by the quality of the environment in which it operates. The report's authors advise factoring in a temporary dip in productivity at the start of implementation — this is normal and temporary.

We see that roles inevitably shift focus, and this happens differently at each step of the ladder.

For those who execute, from junior to senior analysts: there is less routine work, and the freed-up time can be reallocated to areas where machines are still weak — deep thinking about solutions, immersion in business context, communication with stakeholders. Plus, a new skill emerges: instructing an agent and accepting its results. The ability to set criteria for accepting or rejecting results is important.

For those who write the rules — leads, department heads, heads of functions: the focus doesn't shift but rather expands, adding management of agents to the management of people. Processes need to be reconfigured for agents, the environment that produces quality results needs to be set up, and risks created by AI need to be managed.

For the head of the function, this translates into three specific tasks:

  1. Transferring the profession to agents. For decades, experts have accumulated knowledge about what good analytics looks like. Previously, we transferred this through courses, mentorship, and reviews. Now, this same knowledge needs to be transferred to the agent, explicitly defined: set constraints, communication and thinking styles, the ideal outcome, describe work standards, and teach them to use analytics tools.

  2. Not losing quality. AI speeds up the execution part but doesn't eliminate the question, "Is the result good?" Our own tools work here: standards, templates, acceptance criteria, artifact reviews, but now they are integrated into the agent's behavior. No blind faith in quality results; processes must be built with a focus on acceptance.

  3. How to develop analysts. If the lower rung ceases to be a place for gaining experience, in a few years, there will be no one to fill senior and management positions. There is also confirmation of this trend: 17% of surveyed developers cited fear of losing skills as a reason for avoiding AI in their work. This fear is real and must be addressed with a conscious development track. Education is changing, but not disappearing: analytics now requires teaching not templates, but the design of business chains with built-in LLMs and agent systems, and managing AI risks.

The main takeaway: the changes have already begun, but there's no need to fear them; you need to keep pace with them or drive them yourself. The core management skills — setting boundaries and seeing risks on the horizon — remain the same, but the object of management has become broader, with boundaries now set not only for people but also for agents.
