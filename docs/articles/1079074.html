When I started programming, my whole day revolved around development. I would wake up, write code, go to sleep. And it was like that day after day. My entire social circle consisted of people who also write code. I studied frameworks, libraries, different programming languages.

I came into development because I was fascinated by the idea that by writing code, I could create new programs. All I needed to do was learn to program, and I could create anything. If I learned PHP, I could do backend. If I learned HTML and CSS, I could create websites.

I could choose what I wanted to create, learn the necessary technologies for it, and start creating. You open an IDE, create a new project, and implement it from scratch. You choose patterns, libraries, frameworks, read documentation, delve into source code. You get so immersed in the process that you don't notice days passing. Over time, you develop your own development style.

I wrote code, and I enjoyed writing code, but it wasn't the activity I wanted to do my whole life. I preferred creating something new, not the process of writing code itself. I enjoyed creating products from scratch, with just an idea, that people use. For this reason, I always worked in startups and never in large companies.

I always dreamed of career growth, becoming a team lead, a manager, or taking on another role that didn't involve writing code. And whenever someone else could write the code, I moved away from writing code to managerial tasks. Whether I wrote the code myself or delegated it to others didn't matter to me.

Although I got into development out of interest in creation itself, later I only did it because I was paid for it. For me, it was precisely a profession. Even though I wrote code in my free time, I was still building my startups in that way, from which I planned to earn money. Writing code and money were inseparable. Over time, the main focus became income from projects, not just the desire to create projects.

But there is another type of person. These are true programmers who engage in writing code regardless of income. They write code simply because they cannot not write it. It doesn't matter to them how much money they earn from it. They can create open-source projects that will never bring them any income.

They enjoy writing code, solving development tasks, designing, thinking through logic, fixing bugs, and understanding the entire system. They enjoy the process itself. And they will never give this process to AI agents.

I love creating startups, and they love writing code. For me, creating projects is a way to earn money. For them, programming is an art.

Therefore, all my arguments about how AI helps me in creating projects are absolutely not applicable to artists. They don't need AI to help them with anything.

And that's why there's often a negative reaction to the assertion that AI will soon replace all programmers. For artists, this assertion itself is illogical. Even if some technological breakthrough occurs and AI can truly work autonomously and solve tasks hundreds of times faster than humans, people will still continue to write code by hand, every line of code. AI will never be able to take away their ability and desire to write code.

I don't use AI when writing texts. It writes soulless, bland text. AI is a mess you don't want to read. I want to come up with the wording myself, proofread and edit it, come up with a title, delete unnecessary sentences and entire paragraphs. I like it when new ideas for the text emerge during the writing process. The process of writing itself is important to me, the thinking at that moment, not just the result. If I asked AI to write a text on the topic "Programming is an art," this thinking process wouldn't happen. I wouldn't get the same emotions that come with manual text writing. For me, writing texts is an art that I engage in regardless of money. I think people who enjoy writing code have the same feelings.
