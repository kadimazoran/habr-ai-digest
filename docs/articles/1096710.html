This text is a translation of Eliezer Yudkowsky's essay "Cat-Belling Problems," published on September 4th following his debate with Jeffrey Irving, which took place from August 3rd to 7th at the ILIAD-2026 international conference, dedicated to theoretical and mathematical problems of artificial intelligence safety and control.

The text was originally written mostly in 2021 and only completed and published now. Today, this discussion of artificial intelligence might seem strange—it was written at a time when people were still trying to solve what would now be called the "superalignment problem" with their own minds, using clever plans they devised themselves, instead of saying, "Oh, we'll ask Fable to solve this problem."

This is an essay about a children's fable I read long ago, and a lesson I took from it that has stayed with me my entire life.

This is an essay about why I seem to be utterly uninterested in your clever scheme for solving the ASI alignment problem, and start looking bored and annoyed when you explain it to me.

And this, though not quite an essay, is the story of one guy from an online mailing list in 1996 who devised a reaction engine. It's easier to start by laying out the general idea, so we'll start with that.

Mr. L's Reactionless Drive

Back on the Extropians mailing list, which I was subscribed to back in the mists of time when I was only sixteen, there was a guy whose last name started with L. He had a design for a reactionless drive for a spaceship, which was supposed to generate thrust without expelling mass backward. He named it after himself.*

*) To my mind, some readers might reason: "How dare Mr. L. name the drive after himself?! How conceited! How arrogant! This project smells bad, I already have a bad feeling about it, so it probably doesn't work." First, this was around 1997, and people were less status-conscious about inventors back then. It was a fairly common practice to name inventions after oneself. Second, in my opinion, if the drive worked, Mr. L. would have been absolutely right to name it after himself; so the only question is whether the drive works or not. Or, in other words—reasoning "the drive probably doesn't work because Mr. L. named it after himself"—is a bad Bayesian approach. In a hypothetical world where this drive works, we can certainly allow that Mr. L. has every right to name it after himself, especially if it's 1997, and we're not that far removed from an older world where many inventors did so.

At first glance, this looks like a violation of the law of conservation of momentum. In traditional physics, if a spaceship accelerates in one direction, there must be something else accelerating in the opposite direction, and the total change in momentum must be zero. In science fiction, when alternative physical laws are postulated that violate this rule, a drive like the one Mr. L. invented is called a "reactionless drive," in the sense that such a drive violates Newton's third law of action and reaction.

As a result, as one might expect, many members of the Extropians mailing list pressed Mr. L. on how his L-drive could possibly work?

In fact (though I don't think L. fully grasped this), the problem was so "difficult" in quotes that those who bothered to ask Mr. L. to explain it at all showed immense open-mindedness toward Mr. L. and his ideas—it was a great act of charity and adherence to the ethics of scientific discourse.

Mr. L. showed us all an animated GIF he had created himself, depicting a circle with several small masses rotating clockwise around its axis. At the top of the circle, the moving masses accelerated; at the bottom, they decelerated. Thus, the masses moved faster on the right side of the cylinder than on the left, which should create a net thrust to the right due to greater centrifugal force.
