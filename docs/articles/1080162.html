Your product is not a model. Don't price it in tokens.

Initially, tokens were a good fit for calculating the cost of using LLMs: when OpenAI launched its API in 2020, it made sense to charge for the amount of computation required for the model to generate a response.

But two years later, the launch of ChatGPT sparked a wave of new products built on top of these models: and these products no longer just use LLMs, but perform a whole set of tasks for the client. They combine proprietary data, various tools, and integrations into a unified workflow.

When such a product charges by the token, its price reflects the model's costs, not the value the client receives. At the same time, the product's own value becomes tied to a unit whose cost is constantly decreasing. According to the authors, AI applications should not charge on the same principle as model developers.

The authors suggest tying the price to the most understandable result for the client, which can be reliably measured and linked to the product's performance.

  • If you sell access to a model, charge by the token.

  • If the product performs specific tasks, price it based on a client-understandable amount of work — for example, in credits, which are internal units debited for completing various tasks.

  • If the product delivers a specific and measurable business outcome, charge for that outcome.

An incorrectly chosen pricing model is difficult to change later. If you charge by the token, the client starts comparing the AI product to the cost of the model itself. At the same time, the price does not reflect the value of the data, tools, and automation built into it. It's difficult for the client to estimate costs in advance, and the company risks operating with low margins.

Switching to credits doesn't solve the problem on its own. If the number of credits is calculated solely based on the cost of tokens used, the company is still charging for its expenses, not for the benefit the client receives.

If the price is tied to specific work performed, the client understands what they are paying for and can estimate costs in advance. Moreover, improving the product benefits both parties: the client gets more value, and the company can perform the same work more efficiently.

In a survey conducted by the authors, 50 technical specialists responsible for purchasing AI products participated: 27 preferred task-specific credits, and only 14 chose pay-per-token.

1. Tie the price to what you sell

In some cases, choosing a payment model is quite simple: LLM developers sell access to them and charge by the token. Products that deliver specific, measurable results can charge directly for them.

It's more challenging to determine pricing for most AI applications: they don't just provide access to a model, nor do they always guarantee a result for which a separate charge can be levied.

For example, an AI agent for a sales department sells not search queries and model calls, but ready-made information about a potential client: what the company does, who makes decisions, and what proposal can be made to them. An AI tool for programmers sells not spent tokens, but a ready-made code change. A data processing platform might charge for a completed query, configured data processing, or a finished task. The client doesn't necessarily need to know how many models and individual actions were required to achieve the result.

The value is measured differently for different AI products, so there is no single payment model. A voice AI service might initially charge per minute of conversation, and then switch to charging per interaction that the AI resolves without employee intervention. An AI assistant might charge a fixed fee per user, and then add usage-based charges if different tasks vary significantly in cost and client benefit.

The main question is: "What unit of value does the client already understand, and can we consistently measure it?"

2. Clients need to understand what they are paying for

Clients will still ask about tokens, but they usually care about something else: comparing the cost of different solutions and controlling expenses.

Firstly, buyers need a common benchmark. At first glance, a unified calculation system allows for comparing a specialized AI product with a universal API or a custom development, but such a comparison is incorrect. Each product uses different models, data, tools, and degrees of automation. Therefore, the same amount of computation in different products can yield vastly different results.

Secondly, companies want to understand where their money is going. Finance and IT teams need to allocate costs across departments, projects, and clients. If they have to forecast the consumption of each AI product separately, technologies don't simplify work; they create a new level of complexity.

A customer support manager has a rough idea of how many inquiries the company receives. But they cannot calculate in advance how much information the AI will need to process, how many times it will have to repeat a request, and how long the response will be. As a result, instead of a unified ROI calculation, they have to forecast expenses for each AI product separately.

At the same time, the client needs transparency one way or another. Show what tasks the product performed, what resources were used, and why complex tasks cost more than simple ones. But it's not necessary to use internal technical costs as the basis for billing. Finance and IT teams should receive clear reports to plan budgets and allocate expenses.

For pricing to be transparent, it's not necessary to calculate payment in the same units that the company uses for its expenses.

In some cases, the cost of tokens can still be included separately in the bill. For example, if model requests are particularly expensive or their cost is difficult to predict. But this should be an additional part of the tariff, not the primary unit of payment for the product.

3. Credits should reflect what the client is paying for

For most AI products, credits can be a convenient way to account for tasks of varying complexity and cost. But credits themselves don't make pricing more transparent: what the client actually gets for them is important.

A bad system simply converts tokens into its own, even less understandable currency. The name changes, but the client still pays for the product's technical costs.

A good system ties credits to understandable tasks and their complexity. For example, fixing a minor bug requires fewer credits than developing a new feature. A brief analysis of one contract clause requires fewer credits than reviewing the entire document. Adding basic information about a potential client requires fewer credits than a comprehensive investigation.

A good credit system solves three problems:

  • It hides technical complexity. The client pays for the work done, not for the models, requests, and computations within the product.

  • It clearly reflects task complexity. A simple task requires few credits, a standard one a predetermined amount, and a complex one more.

  • It provides flexibility. One credit package can be used for different tasks and AI tools within the same contract.

The main thing is that the client immediately understands how the system works. Usually, companies can estimate how many tasks they need to perform, but they don't know how much computational resource will be required. If the credit deduction principle cannot be explained in a few sentences, the system is too complex.

4. Credits help maintain profitability

A well-thought-out credit system not only simplifies payment for the client but also helps the company maintain profitability.

In traditional SaaS, each new user adds almost no extra cost to the company. In AI products, it's different: each user's activity requires new model calls, data retrieval and processing, use of third-party services, and sometimes image and video generation. Additionally, money is spent on repeated requests and tasks that the AI fails to complete.

Therefore, rapid revenue growth doesn't necessarily mean the business is profitable. A significant portion of every dollar earned can immediately go to model providers, cloud services, and data suppliers.

When setting prices, it's important to separate two questions:

  1. How much is the client willing to pay for the result? This, along with competitor pricing, determines the cost of a credit package.

  2. How much does it cost your company to perform the task? The cost and complexity determine how many credits will be debited.

This separation helps maintain profitability: if you find a cheaper model, reuse existing data, improve AI prompts, or optimize infrastructure, your costs decrease, but the price for the client doesn't necessarily have to decrease with them.

The company can retain a portion of the savings because the client pays for the work done, not for the cost of computations.

A telling example is Clay, a sales automation and lead generation service. In 2026, the company admitted that in 2022, they had miscalculated the cost of credits in their Pro plan. As a result, this plan incurred losses for several years.

In the new system, Clay separated costs into two parts. Data Credits cover data from third-party sources, and Actions cover the platform's work: information retrieval, processing, and executing a sequence of actions. For models with predictable costs, there's a fixed price. The cost of expensive AI models, whose expenses can vary greatly, is paid separately by the client, based on the actual token count — and without a markup.

In other words, the client separately pays for the platform's value, third-party data, and unpredictable LLM expenses.

5. Move to pay-per-result when its value becomes obvious and easily measurable

Credits are suitable when the product performs a specific task, but its impact on the business is still difficult to quantify precisely: for example, an AI might study a potential client and prepare an initial message, but it's hard to guarantee that this will lead to a sale.

However, if the result can be precisely measured and linked to the product's performance, it makes more sense to charge directly for it. For instance, for a resolved support ticket, a found lead, a scheduled meeting, a processed insurance claim, or money recovered for the company.

If the product's impact on the final outcome cannot be precisely measured, it's better to debit credits for completed tasks. If the client only buys access to the model, you can charge by the token.

Moreover, a single product can combine multiple payment methods: a fixed subscription per user, credits for completed tasks, separate charges for expensive and unpredictable model calls, and a commission for a specific business outcome.

Having several different payment methods is not a problem in itself: the main thing is that the client understands exactly what they are paying for in each case.

Show the client what they are paying for

If you charge by the token, the product's price becomes tied to the cost of computation, which is constantly decreasing. Meanwhile, the product itself should become more useful, reliable, and deeply integrated into the client's workflow over time.

Therefore, it's better to tie the price to the most understandable result that can be measured and linked to the product's performance.
