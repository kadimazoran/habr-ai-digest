AI is actively used not only in commercial but also in scientific programming, for data analysis and visualization. As in other areas, generative AI multiplies both the good and the bad inherent in the established system. It accelerates code creation and calculations, leading to the faster emergence of research results. However, this is followed by a complex chain of verification and dissemination of results, which is not easily scalable with AI.

The prospect is unpleasant: we are trying to produce research results much faster than we can verify which of them actually make sense. A single incorrect piece of code can generate a graph, statistics, conclusion, article, and then dozens of new papers citing the first publication. At each subsequent stage, the result will appear increasingly "scientific" (recall the citation index).

Ultimately, if we do not change the system of scientific knowledge dissemination, we risk increasingly overlooking incorrect conclusions, and may even descend into pseudoscience. This, in the end, will not accelerate but only slow down progress.

What is the problem

"The code works" and "the code correctly solves the given problem" are two different states.

In simple cases of commercial development, these concepts are almost identical: the code runs, produces a result, so everything is fine. But as soon as the task becomes a bit more complex, for example, requiring high loads, not any running code will cope with it.

And in the scientific field, it is quite possible to write a program that will run to completion without a single error – it will generate a beautiful graph or produce the required decimal places. Only it will be solving a completely different problem, using the wrong method, exceeding the model's applicability limits, and so on. The development environment, alas, cannot highlight such errors.

The problem existed long before generative AI. Scientists are not superhuman: they make mistakes, misinterpret results, choose inappropriate methods, and sometimes deliberately falsify data. And verification is complicated by the fact that the correct calculation result is often unknown.

As a control method, the scientific world invented peer review. To put it very simply, a scientific paper has several chances to be sent back for revision on its way to immortality. First, a scientific journal editor might reject it, then reviewers will scrutinize it. After publication, other researchers will pay attention to errors, try to reproduce the results, or find contradictions with their own data.

The system never claimed to be ideal. A reviewer is also human, and therefore, quite capable of missing an error. There are many examples one can recall. The Piltdown Man alone is worth mentioning.

In 1912, paleontologist Arthur Smith Woodward and amateur archaeologist Charles Dawson announced the discovery of a "missing link" between ape and man – the Piltdown Man. The find was studied for decades and effectively entered scientific circulation. Although the authenticity of the remains was questioned from the beginning (bones found later in other regions did not resemble those presented by Dawson), it was only in the 1950s that it was definitively established that it was a forgery: a human skull was combined with an orangutan jaw, the bones were artificially aged, and the teeth were filed down. Modern dating methods helped expose the forgery, and the story is now known as the Piltdown Hoax.

A more recent example from physics is Jan Hendrik Schön. In the late 1990s and early 2000s, he published a large number of papers on the physics of organic materials and superconductivity, even received several scientific awards, and was tipped for a Nobel Prize. Colleagues noted that in 2001, he published one article every eight days – an extraordinary productivity for a scientist. However, he was later exposed for falsifying results: he manipulated experimental data to justify his predictions. Other research groups could not replicate the processes he described, and later, the same graphs were found in publications dedicated to different experiments. The articles were retracted, and the scientist was fired from Bell Labs.

There are many such stories. Each time after such scandals, discussions about how well the peer review system actually copes with its task would flare up with renewed vigor. There was a lot of criticism, but no one could offer anything better, because the main principle still works. It is that a researcher should not be the only person who decides that their result is correct.

Generative AI is changing the rules of the game. It helps write code or analyze data faster, thus reaching the publication stage in a short time. But if the army of editors and reviewers was not coping well with the flow of papers before, who will check all this when the number of publications increases tenfold?

And the number of publications is already actively growing! From 2020 to 2025, their annual number doubled. A surge is noted after the public release of ChatGPT. Due to the shortage of reviewers, this has increased the time lag between submission and publication from several months to a year, and this, it seems, is not the limit.

Let's approach self-checking responsibly? No!

Researchers from the University of Michigan, the University of Tennessee, and Sandia National Laboratories analyzed the use of AI in science. To do this, in 2025, they collected free-text responses from 527 participants (researchers primarily from US universities) on how they applied AI. The responses were not exhaustive – each described only one recent episode of using models: what task they were solving, how they used their main AI tool, and what they did to verify the result. Additionally, the researchers asked about the participant's confidence in their own abilities, the AI's ability to perform the task, and the resulting outcome.

The researchers coded the free-text responses in two directions: what task the scientist delegated to AI and by what method they evaluated the result. After that, they compared the resulting categories with the participants' programming experience, scientific field, and confidence level.

The findings were curious.

What tasks were delegated

The sample turned out to be quite knowledgeable. The median scientific programming experience was six years, and 84% of respondents wrote code at least once a week. The most popular languages were Python and R.

Here are the tasks delegated to AI.

  • Data – 23.9%. Cleaning, transformation, merging tables, working with formats, and similar tasks.

  • Visualization – 19.8%. Graphs, their design, and customization.

  • Debugging – 17.4%. Finding causes of errors and fixing code.

  • Mathematical and scientific computations – 11.7%. Equations, models, simulations, numerical methods.

  • Statistical analysis – 10.9%. Regressions, statistical tests, ML pipelines, and similar tasks.

Together, these five categories accounted for 76% of responses with a clearly specified use case.

How the result was verified

The funniest thing is that almost no one properly checked the code. The most common verification strategy was simply running the code. This was mentioned by 52.8% of participants. Another 22.5% said they looked at the resulting output. 19.4% read the generated code itself. 16.2% checked the visualization. This might seem harmless, but testing and verification by colleagues were almost non-existent. Automated tests or test sets were mentioned in about 2.8% of cases. Verification by a colleague – about 2%. Manual mathematical verification – 1.2%. Comparison with another AI model – 0.4%.

It's too early to sound the alarm loudly. Firstly, verification can differ for different tasks. Testing is indeed inappropriate in some areas. Secondly, the responses included comparison of results with known values, running code on small datasets with a known answer, or comparison with a previous implementation. This is a perfectly valid way to get an independent judgment. However, these mentions were too rare. In most cases, verification remained within the "human – AI" loop.

Does experience help here

The study also showed that the more experienced a developer is, the less they trust AI in general, but the number of described methods for verifying results does not significantly increase.

It's also amusing that the less experienced a developer is, the more they trust AI. But if you think about it, the less a person understands programming, the harder it is for them to independently notice that the AI assistant has done something wrong.

This leads to an unpleasant situation: a significant part of the responsibility for detecting errors that the researcher themselves did not notice falls on subsequent filters, including peer review. The reviewer will have to delve into and understand how correct the chosen method is, how well-founded the conclusions are, and whether the obtained results can be trusted.

Obvious nonsense is easy to filter out, but what about articles whose results look plausible (which is AI's superpower)?

Fantasing about the future

With such an increase in the number of publications, we will very quickly hit the limits of reviewers' capabilities – the number of hours in their day does not change. This means more incorrect results will be published.

They could be filtered out by re-running the experiment, but such resources are also not unlimited. It turns out that the volume of published but not yet refuted incorrect results will only grow.

There are discussions about automating peer review: several research groups are developing agents to speed up article verification. But this task must be approached not as in the work above, but with all caution, training a kind of "reviewer agent" only on verified results. It needs independent verification methods: test data, benchmark results, alternative implementations, reproducible computations, verifiable mathematical properties, and, ideally, access to source code and data. Otherwise, we will get a brilliant review specialist who will very quickly and convincingly confirm what the previous AI has already managed to code. Thus, the entire scientific world will go in the wrong direction.
