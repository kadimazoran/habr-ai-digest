There is a moment that many managers already feel but do not always articulate aloud. Artificial intelligence is ceasing to be a separate technological topic and is becoming a standard work tool within the enterprise. Finance, sales, production, legal, IT — everyone gets the opportunity to quickly assemble their version of the situation, recalculate options, and justify decisions.

We are entering an era of redistribution of managerial initiative.

And here arises a new problem for the director. It is no longer enough for them to simply have access to data or another “smartest chat.” On the contrary, there are increasingly more well-formulated, quickly recalculated, and locally correct answers around them. Each of them can be convincing — and at the same time, not consider the enterprise as a whole.

The book “The Digital Brain of the Firm” grew out of a short continuation of “The Brain of the Firm: 2026”. If the previous book was primarily about how an enterprise maintains its field of viable continuations, then here the question shifts to the director themselves. What happens to their role when the speed of intellectual assembly ceases to be solely a human advantage?

The book’s answer is simple: the director needs not a digital deputy, but their own management loop, connected simultaneously to their professional line and to the actual reality of the enterprise. That is, not an avatar, not an “artificial CEO,” and not another model that answers questions beautifully.

The digital brain of the firm is where the director meets their enterprise.

On one hand, there is the director themselves: their experience, criteria, habit of distinguishing the important from the secondary, attitude towards risk, boundaries of the permissible, and history of decisions. On the other hand, there is the enterprise: ERP, obligations, money, production, documents, events, statuses, and real limitations. The purpose of the digital brain is not to replace one side with the other, but to connect them into a stable professional loop.

This is precisely why the image of a “golden telephone” appears in the book. It is not a magic tube or a fancy gadget for effect. It is an image of a very simple director’s interface to a complex system. The director asks a question by voice, the system reconstructs the context, accesses the enterprise’s memory and facts, launches calculations if necessary, and returns not a stream of data, but a position and options for decision-making.

Another important point is that this construct does not replace ERP or require building an ideal enterprise first. ERP remains the most important source of transactional reality, but in itself, it does not store the director’s entire professional picture. The digital brain appears on top of real systems and gradually reveals which gaps in data, connections, and managerial memory truly hinder decision-making.

The main question now is not whether a digital brain of the firm can be created. The main question is who will be the first to assemble it in their own enterprise.

This book is small — it can be read in one evening. It is written not for AI system developers, but primarily for the director who is responsible for the real enterprise and already sees that the rules are starting to change again. Not because the machine has become “smarter than humans,” but because the speed of decision preparation is becoming widespread. And in this situation, the main deficit is no longer information, but the ability to maintain initiative and a holistic picture.
