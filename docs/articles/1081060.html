Initially, I thought I just couldn't find this information in the settings: how many tokens are included in the subscription? ChatGPT Plus, Claude Pro, and Google AI Pro cost around $20, and the expensive plans also conveniently fall into the same $100 and $200 brackets. So, all that's left is to find out the number of tokens and choose where you get more. But it's not that simple.

As you delve deeper, you encounter five-hour windows, weekly limits, 5x and 20x plans. This is where it seems: 5x of what? From what number? Posts mention hundreds of millions of tokens, and bloggers claim that AI companies are operating at a huge loss with these subscriptions: they take $100 and provide tokens worth $500+.

Subscription usage restrictions also add to the interest. For example, Anthropic does not allow you to serve users of its application through a personal subscription - you need an API for that. This means cheap access is available, but you can't turn it into your own service. Claude Code Rules.

If subscriptions are so generous, a lot of questions arise. Why is this necessary at all? How long will prices remain this way? How much are companies losing on them? Or is it just a myth inflated by the companies themselves?

The first idea, of course, was to check everything locally. But during the very first attempts with ChatGPT and collecting the limit after one of the failures, it became clear that this is not very practical. Therefore, I gathered public research, user benchmarks, and official terms from AI vendors.

When Cache Actually Saves Money

One of the standard scenarios is uploading a large document and asking questions about it. The document is the same, but the questions are different. In such a scenario, the document is cached, saving limits.

With an agent in a repository, it's a similar story: instructions, already read code, and previous steps remain in the context, while the test result and the next query are added at the end. In this mode, cache hits can be frequent.

Now, consider another task: analyzing ten different log dumps one by one. The main text is new each time, so it cannot be reused. Another way to lose cache hits is to change the beginning of the query: the same document or large text will not be cached if there is different text before it each time. How it works at OpenAI, at Anthropic.

For Fable, a repeat query in this example is twice as cheap as for Astra, even though the regular input and response generation cost the same for both. The reason is precisely the cache read rate: $0.25 versus $1 per million. Gemini is cheaper than both, with and without cache.

But that's not all - will the cache persist until the next request? According to the API terms, the differences are as follows:

  • Astra: The cache retains reusability for at least 30 minutes after writing or the last access. If you get distracted for ten minutes and return to the same document, it's more convenient than a short window. OpenAI Docs.

  • Claude: The standard cache lasts 5 minutes and is updated with each access. This is fine for continuous agent operation, but for dialogues with pauses, there's a one-hour option with more expensive storage. Fable 5.1, however, has very cheap repeat reads. Claude Documentation.

  • Gemini: Automatic caching is enabled by default. Google recommends keeping the common part at the beginning of the request and sending repeat requests at short intervals; there's a separate mode in the generateContent API for explicit cache management. In terms of read cost, Gemini 3.8 Flash is the cheapest of the three. Gemini Documentation.

In practice, the agent/harness often determines a lot: how it collects context and how much time passes between calls.

A good example is in the Microsoft study on Copilot data: the average hit rate in the server-side KV cache is about 90% within one agent step and 55% between steps. While the agent performs a sequence of steps itself, reuse is high. When pauses and context changes are added to the process, the benefit decreases. Therefore, a long session on one project and ten independent analyses can result in very different costs with a similar number of tokens - and before starting, it's important to choose the correct option for the current case.

So, Are Companies Operating at a Loss?

I think that at the most active users, companies at current prices can indeed operate at a loss. But to claim that a $20 subscription provides the equivalent of $500 in tokens and the company simply paid an extra $480 itself is not entirely correct.

According to 2025 data, for every dollar of revenue, OpenAI spent approximately $2.60, and Anthropic - $2.16.

Graph from Ars Technica based on OpenAI financial documents.

But the situation is changing: later, Anthropic already forecasted its first quarter with adjusted operating profit.

My opinion is that companies are currently partially subsidizing for growth and habit formation with the product. It's important now for users to transfer their projects, set up agents, and integrate the tool into their workflow, and then prices can be raised (interestingly, the obvious functionality for migrating from a competitor with all its features is currently only available from Gemini). But on the other hand, now seems like the most opportune moment to try all possible tools, understand what works and how for your tasks, and make a choice.

When Will Subscriptions Be Disabled?

I think companies are already trying to figure out a boundary between subscriptions and APIs.

For example, Ars Technica described Anthropic's test where Claude Code was removed from Pro for some new registrations. According to the publication, the experiment affected 2% of new registrations, and then the Claude Code was again indicated as included on the pricing page.

A more specific current example is Fable: on Pro, it's a separate payment; on Max, it's a limited included portion. OpenAI offers additional credits; Google has also announced replenishment of AI credits. These mechanisms already exist. OpenAI Docs, Google AI subscriptions.

I think that basic versions will definitely remain, while more and more expensive agent scenarios will be paid for separately: with credits in the app or via API. As for the x5 and x20 subscriptions, in my forecast, they will either become much more expensive or be replaced by tokens.

It also seems that Google has more room for such a price war: Gemini can be promoted along with the rest of the ecosystem. OpenAI and Anthropic also earn from APIs and corporate products, but their business is much more tied to AI itself. Therefore, I am particularly interested in who will be the first to "give in" - the competition is very fierce. It's either choosing user outflow for profitability, or continuing a loss-making war just to not lose to competitors - and here it seems that Google can hold out the longest.

At the same time, Chinese and open-source models are not that far behind. In my opinion, their lag behind the flagships is already measured in months, and it's not necessary to catch up with the leader in everything: if a cheaper model handles most of the routine, and Anthropic and OpenAI sharply increase prices - then why not try Kimi K3, etc., which, by the way, is also available on electricity bench. So, even a simultaneous price change from American giants seems unlikely.

How to Compare Them?

Perhaps someone will find something more complex relevant - various local benchmarks and so on. This is also a good option, but these are separate resources and require time just to test AI tools. There is a simpler option.

There is a utility called ccusage. It works with agent session logs and shows token consumption and their cost at API prices. With Node.js installed, it runs with a single command:

npx ccusage@latest codex session
