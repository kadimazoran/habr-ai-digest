❯ Who is YOLO

You Only Look Once — YOLO.
A family of computer vision models for detecting objects in images and videos. The first model appeared in 2015 and changed the approach to object detection. Instead of two stages 'find possible regions and classify each,' it predicted object bounding boxes, their classes, and prediction confidence in a single network pass.

The technology is constantly evolving. The very first versions only solved primitive object identification tasks, but over 11 years, many versions have been released, and the technology has come a long way.

YOLO's main advantage is its balance of speed, accuracy, and ease of use. That's why it's often used for cameras, video analytics, retail, sports, and production control. YOLO is good not because it's always the best, but because in many real-world tasks, it's the easiest to get a working result with.

The technology's popularity in production is due to how the surrounding tools have developed. Previously, training a detector required long manual compilation of C++ libraries for a specific graphics card architecture. Modern versions of the family have turned into ready-to-use Python packages with a minimal entry barrier, supporting export to open formats right out of the box.

YOLO in practice:

Retail and Chain Stores

In retail and chain stores, YOLO can be used within queue and shelf space monitoring systems. Cameras above the sales floor detect shopping carts, baskets, and customers, sending their positions to a tracker. This provides the retailer with an accurate heat map of department traffic and automatically notifies the store manager if more than five people have gathered at the self-checkout counters. When integrated with segmentation, the system identifies empty spaces on shelves with high-demand products.

Industry

At industrial quarries and construction sites, the model can be integrated into the onboard computers of quarry trucks and excavators. All-around view cameras with a lightweight YOLO Nano version can detect people in vests, passenger vehicles, and large boulders in the path of heavy machinery in real-time. The onboard terminal emits a loud audible alarm to the driver in case of dangerous proximity, preventing collisions in conditions with blind spots and dense dust.

Public Safety

In public safety systems, video analytics complexes based on YOLO can be installed on buses and other urban transport. During regular routes, cameras analyze the surrounding environment, detecting suspicious actions, unattended items, conflicts, people falling, or intrusions into restricted areas. If the system detects a potentially dangerous event, it saves a fragment of the recording, determines its coordinates, and sends a notification to the city situation center operator for further verification.

All provided examples are just illustrations of how this technology can be used.

Not everything is as cool as it could be

Significant drawbacks of the technology exist despite its strengths

Domain Shift Problem: A model pre-trained on the COCO dataset can perfectly detect people during the day in good weather, but it immediately becomes blind in a metallurgical plant workshop under sodium lamps and smoke, requiring the collection of hundreds of custom labeled frames.

Difficulty with Occlusions: If one object is partially obscured by another (e.g., a pedestrian behind a pole or a box under another box), the network often loses confidence or misclassifies the object.

Dependency on Input Tensor Resolution: To find small details in a wide outdoor panorama, the input image size must be increased from 640 to 1280 pixels, which quadratically reduces the frame rate.

Accumulation of Tracking Errors: In complex scenarios, temporal trackers like DeepSort or ByteTrack can lose a person's ID during prolonged occlusion and assign a new ID after they re-emerge from the blind spot.

Nevertheless, YOLO remains the default standard in the video surveillance industry. It provides a ready-made architectural base around which a developer can build a working pipeline from collecting video streams from an RTSP camera to sending alerts to a database in a few days.

❯ DotEye: A Telegram Security Guard

A Telegram bot that watches over a room.
Turn on security mode, connect a camera, and the bot starts 'guarding your room'. If a person appears, you get a notification that someone entered or left the frame.

Initially, a photo was sent with every movement, but that was a mistake.
There were so many movements that the bot spammed me for no reason.
So, I made it so that motion only wakes up the detector, and YOLO confirms a person's intrusion into the room. After that, an IoU tracker matches bounding boxes between frames and prevents the bot from spamming identical messages.

In the panel, you can enable security, set quiet hours, choose a model, and check the camera status. Each camera can have its own settings.

There's also a voice mode. When an unfamiliar person enters, the bot can sound a siren and say a predefined phrase through the computer's speakers. By default, it's 'Attention, unauthorized person. Please leave the premises!'. During quiet hours, regular greetings can be turned off, but the alarm can remain. If facial recognition is enabled and the camera recognizes me, the alarm is deactivated. Otherwise, it can be dismissed with a button in the chat.

Pixelating Photos

By default, the bot does not send the original photo with a person to Telegram. Each photo is automatically pixelated. To understand that someone entered, I don't need to see their face. The original frame remains on the computer in encrypted form and is not sent anywhere.

❯ Why a Single Frame Isn't Enough

YOLO looks at a single frame. But a notification is needed for an event over time, not just a frame. A person might stand at the door for five seconds, walk past the camera, or leave and return a minute later. If the results from adjacent frames aren't linked, the bot will consider each new bounding box as a new person.

This is where the IoU tracker comes in.
It compares how much the current bounding box overlaps with the previous one. If the overlap is significant, it's the same object with the same ID. If a new bounding box appears, it signifies an entry. If a bounding box disappears for a specified time, it signifies an exit. This is simple logic, but it's what eliminated the stream of almost identical photos from the chat.

This approach has its limits. If a person is completely hidden by a door or leaves the frame and immediately returns from another side, the tracker might assign a new ID. For my project, this isn't critical. It's more important not to miss an event than to perfectly track movement statistics.

The prank worked

To test the functionality in practice, I asked a friend to participate in a small experiment.

In the morning, I turned on the security and left. My friend was supposed to come in without warning.
He also didn't know about the audio alert system that was set up.

At some point, a message arrived: 'Unknown person detected'. Immediately after, a frame with a photo of a bewildered friend. The predefined voice phrase sounded in the room, followed by a siren. My friend clearly got flustered, quickly left the room, and messaged me to turn off the alarm.

The prank worked. And I confirmed that the bot was indeed functioning.

I'll show an example of how to run the project locally on one device.

Local Setup

Don't forget to clone the repository before you start.
Create a virtual environment and install dependencies:

python -m venv .venv
.venv
Scripts
activate
pip install -r requirements.txt

Next, copy the settings template:

cp .env.example .env
python -m doteye.crypto

This command will generate an encryption key. You need to record it in .env along with the bot token and your Telegram ID:

DOTEYE_BOT_TOKEN=<bot token>
DOTEYE_ADMIN_IDS=<my Telegram ID>
DOTEYE_CRYPTO_KEY=<generated key>

Run the bot:

python -m doteye.main

After starting, the bot sends its status to Telegram. You can also open /panel there, connect a camera, enable security, and check that the bot is working.

You can use your phone instead of a camera

You can connect a built-in webcam, an IP camera, or a phone. For Android, an app that provides MJPEG or RTSP is sufficient. For example, after starting a stream via the 'IP Webcam' app, a local address appears on the screen. The phone and the computer with the bot must be on the same network.

You can add a camera through the bot's interface: 'Panel -> Camera -> Edit camera list'. Or specify the source in the settings:

DOTEYE_CAMERA_SOURCE=http://192.168.1.40:8080/video
DOTEYE_ALLOWED_URL_HOSTS=192.168.1.40

The address above is an example. Your phone will have its own IP, which you need to substitute. An allowlist is mandatory; it's one of the basic security measures. If it's not configured, anyone who knows the address and port can connect to the server.

❯ How to Offload Processing to a Server

DotEye works fine on a home computer as long as there are few cameras and the processor is sufficient. However, the camera, Telegram bot, and inference solve different tasks.
I wanted to keep the camera and control at home, and move YOLO to a separate machine.

The home computer continues to read the camera stream, store events, and send notifications to Telegram. An encrypted JPEG frame is sent to the server, and only the bounding box coordinates are returned. A constant video stream is not transmitted to the server.

Create and configure the server

For the first setup, I chose Timeweb Cloud and a public IP address. This is a temporary scenario. For permanent operation, it's better to use a domain and an auto-renewing TLS certificate.

1. Register an account on Timeweb Cloud and confirm your email.

Go to Timeweb Cloud and create an account. After registration, the control panel opens, from which you can create servers, manage IP addresses, SSH keys, and Cloud Firewall rules.

2. Top up your balance with the required amount.
3. In the panel, go to 'Cloud Servers' and click 'Create Server'.
4. Choose the configuration for the future server

In the panel, open the cloud servers section and click 'Create Server'. Choose a clean Ubuntu image. For this guide, we'll use Ubuntu 26.04 and the 'Moscow' location.

It's best to place the server closer to your home or where the camera is located. This won't speed up YOLO itself but will reduce latency between your home DotEye and the VPS.

In the 'Network' tab, make sure to select a public IP; otherwise, we won't be able to connect to the server.

In the 'Security' tab, you can leave the default settings.

Now, click the 'Order' button and wait.

5. Connect to the server

You should receive an email with server login details.

Copy the server password from the dashboard, open your console, and connect to the server via SSH.

If an SSH key was added beforehand, a password won't be needed. For a permanent server, this option is preferable to password login.

6. Prepare the server for remote mode

At this stage, the server is created, the public IP is saved, and the SSH session is open. This step is just preparation. Install Docker and copy the source code. We won't start the remote server yet, nor issue a certificate, and we won't change the home DotEye settings.

Update the package list and install Git and Docker:

apt update
apt install -y git ca-certificates curl
curl -fsSL https://get.docker.com -o get-docker.sh
sh get-docker.sh

Verify that Docker and Docker Compose are available:

docker --version
docker compose version

Clone the DotEye repository:

git clone https://github.com/network-user/DotEye.git
cd DotEye

Server preparation is now complete. The server has Docker, Docker Compose, and the project's source code.

Launching the Remote Server

The remote server uses two routes:

  • GET /health — responds that the service is running;

  • POST /detect — accepts an encrypted frame and returns the coordinates of detected objects.

Plain HTTP is not suitable for external connections. DotEye only allows it for localhost and development mode. Since frames of the room are transmitted via remote, the server must operate over HTTPS.

In the project directory, run the configuration generator:

python3 deploy/deploy_remote.py

The generator will ask questions.

  1. DOTEYE_CRYPTO_KEY: Press Enter to create a new key and save it in your secrets manager. The same key will be needed on your home machine.

  2. Deployment Type: Choose 2 - IP address.

  3. IP: Paste the public IPv4 of the VPS.

  4. Choose device and model: For a standard VPS without an NVIDIA GPU, use cpu and yolov8n.pt.

The script will create deploy/.env.remote and deploy/docker-compose.remote.yml. In IP mode, Compose already contains TLS paths, port 8099, and the selected model. Manual configuration editing is not required.

Now, issue a self-signed certificate. In subjectAltName, you must specify the server's IP; otherwise, the client will correctly refuse to connect to the certificate.

mkdir -p deploy/tls
openssl req -x509 -newkey rsa:4096 -sha256 -nodes -days 365 \
  -keyout deploy/tls/server.key \
  -out deploy/tls/server.crt \
  -subj "/CN=<SERVER_IP>" \
  -addext "subjectAltName = IP:<SERVER_IP>"

Start the remote service:

chown root:1000 deploy/tls/server.key
chmod 640 deploy/tls/server.key
chmod 644 deploy/tls/server.crt

docker compose -f deploy/docker-compose.remote.yml up -d --build
docker compose -f deploy/docker-compose.remote.yml ps

In the Cloud Firewall, add an inbound rule for TCP port 8099. If your home connection uses a static external IP, restrict the source to only that IP. With a dynamic IP, the port will need to be opened more broadly, making it especially important not to disable TLS verification and not to share DOTEYE_CRYPTO_KEY with third parties.

The private server.key remains on the VPS. Only the public certificate is copied to your home computer:

mkdir -p certs
scp root@<SERVER_IP>:/root/DotEye/deploy/tls/server.crt certs/doteye-remote.crt

This command assumes the repository on the server is located at /root/DotEye. If it was cloned into a different directory, change the path after the colon.

On your home computer, add the following to .env:

DOTEYE_REMOTE_PROCESSING=1
DOTEYE_REMOTE_URL=https://<SERVER_IP>:8099
DOTEYE_ALLOWED_URL_HOSTS=<SERVER_IP>,<CAMERA_IP>
DOTEYE_REMOTE_CA_CERT=<absolute path to copied server.crt>
DOTEYE_REMOTE_FALLBACK=1
DOTEYE_CRYPTO_KEY=<THE SAME KEY FROM deploy/.env.remote>

DOTEYE_ALLOWED_URL_HOSTS contains a precise list of network addresses that DotEye can access. If the camera is connected directly to the computer, only the Server IP should be listed. If the source is a phone, RTSP camera, or HTTP stream, add the camera's IP as well.

The path /app/certs/doteye-remote.crt is used when running the main DotEye via Docker Compose. If the project is run directly via Python, specify the absolute path to the certificate on your home computer.

Restart the main service:

docker compose up -d --build doteye
docker compose logs -f doteye

Or when running without Docker:

python -m doteye.main

Test the remote server from your home machine:

curl --cacert certs/doteye-remote.crt https://<SERVER_IP>:8099/health

In PowerShell, it's better to use curl.exe:

curl.exe --cacert certs/doteye-remote.crt https://<SERVER_IP>:8099/health
