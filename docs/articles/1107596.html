ChatGPT, Gemini, and Perplexity can direct shoppers to fake online stores and non-functional websites. This was revealed by a study from Podqi, a company specializing in intellectual property protection. Suspicious addresses were found in 15% of Perplexity's responses, 8% of ChatGPT's, and 4% of Gemini's. The researchers considered recommendations for such sites, as well as neutral mentions and warnings about dangers.

Podqi checked which stores chatbots suggest when searching for products from 151 brands. The selection included small companies and large manufacturers, including Lululemon, Birkenstock, Stüssy, and Le Creuset. Among the study participants was the brand Madhappy, a client of Podqi itself.

The testing took place in two stages in September 2026. Researchers asked the services questions about where to buy the brand's products, which stores to choose, and whether the company has international shipping and regional websites. Podqi formulated the queries; real customer conversations were not used in the study. Responses were collected using an AI search result tracking tool.

In total, the team analyzed 6795 responses. Links to resources that researchers classified as risky were found across all three services. This category included websites imitating official stores or allegedly selling counterfeits, former brand domains re-registered by new owners, and non-functional pages.

The last group differs from active fake stores: a non-functional website by itself does not necessarily mean fraud. However, a released domain can be acquired by a new owner. If a chatbot continues to offer this address to shoppers, the link could lead to a completely different resource.

In terms of the frequency of risky sites, Gemini showed the best result in the test – 4% of responses. ChatGPT had a rate of 8%, and Perplexity – 15%. However, when assessing warnings, the order changed: ChatGPT most often informed users about the risk – in 24% of responses containing such sites.

Therefore, it is important to distinguish between the study's metrics. Mentioning a suspicious address with a warning and recommending a purchase there are different situations, although both were included in the overall statistics. The results also apply to a specific set of queries and September testing, not to all customer inquiries to these services.

Errors were particularly frequent in questions about regional stores. Chatbots sometimes listed supposedly official websites for specific countries, even for brands that did not have such sites. In one example, Perplexity mistook a third-party page for the official Stüssy store and called it a suitable place to buy guaranteed original products.

Smaller brands proved more vulnerable: risky sites appeared about half as often in responses concerning large companies.

The problem arises amid the promotion of chatbots as shopping assistants. Developers suggest using AI to compare products, find sellers, and explore assortments. However, a confident answer can give the shopper the impression that the store's reliability has already been verified. This is especially significant before holiday sales, when people are looking for good deals and available items from unfamiliar sellers.

Trevor West, CEO and co-founder of Podqi, urged in a comment to The Verge to check the source of a recommendation before entering personal data or bank card details. The mere fact that a store appears in a chatbot's response, he said, does not negate this need for verification.

Google, OpenAI, and Perplexity also commented on the findings to the publication. Google representative Jessica Lewis Board noted that Gemini showed risky sites less often than other tested products. She added that the company continues to improve its models and places links to sources alongside responses so users can verify information.

OpenAI representative Taia Christianson stated that the company is improving its protective mechanisms designed to reduce the appearance of potentially deceptive sites in shopping recommendations. She also thanked the researchers for examples that help identify errors.

Perplexity representative Jesse Dwyer referred to the service's high performance in research benchmarks and called accuracy the company's top priority. At the same time, he emphasized that Perplexity does not claim 100% accuracy for its responses.
