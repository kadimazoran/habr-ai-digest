From Agents to Harness

In large companies, many don't understand what a harness is, or they understand it differently. Daniil Malyshev, Product Owner at MWS, first heard this word in August when he encountered DeepSeek Harness. The architect had a task: to check what another developer had done. As usual, he submitted it for review to Opus, and it provided three minor remarks. Then DeepSeek Harness received the same task and provided about 50 remarks, 25 of which required developer attention. Even though DeepSeek was previously considered an average neural network. Since then, they started to figure out why the model, with such a wrapper, started producing excellent results.

Let's start with the fact that a raw LLM is a black box: it receives text and outputs other text. It has no memory, no hands, no up-to-date knowledge, no precise calculations, no self-checking, and so on.

An agent is a model plus a harness. And a harness is everything in the agent except the model itself: prompt, context and memory, tools, action loop, checks and limitations, orchestration.

Now, a brief excursion into how the wrapper around models has evolved. In 2020, GPT-3 already existed, but it wasn't popular. The boom happened in 2022 when GPT got its first harness in the form of a chat: ChatGPT was released on November 30, 2022, and gathered about 100 million users in two months. Around the same time, the first loops and reasoning emerged. In 2023, tools arrived: from July, it was possible to run Python directly in the ChatGPT sandbox. In the same year, external memory, RAG, and autonomous agents appeared. In 2024, context management tools emerged: history compression, MCP standard, and more. In 2025, a full-fledged environment with files, a terminal, tests, skills, and hooks became available. And in 2026, agents learned to self-configure.

During this time, we've moved from prompt engineering, where we learned to formulate requests, through context engineering, where we understood what the model sees and when. Now it's time for harness engineering: everything previous is important, plus tools, loops, checks, limitations, and security.

What is Harness Useful For

The model itself remembers nothing, calculates inaccurately, and doesn't check itself. The wrapper handles all of this, and the meetup covered its four main tasks.

Context Management

First, let's talk about how LLMs work. They have no memory of their own: for each new question, they re-read the entire conversation—all our questions and their answers. This conversation is called the context. It grows with each message, and the model starts to lose what was at the beginning. Early models truncated the beginning of the conversation; modern ones store it but respond worse.

Therefore, experienced vibe-coders, after 20 messages, would open a new chat and restate the task. Many large models have already partially solved this problem: when the context exceeds 200,000 tokens, the conversation is automatically compressed. Let's note: context management is the most important of these tasks.

Before deciding what to do with the context, let's break down what it consists of. Any prompt we send includes a hidden system prompt. You cannot influence it unless the call is made through an API. Even through an API, vendors dislike interference with system prompts and may ban you. Next in the context are rules, skills, limitations, AGENTS.md, and so on. The dialogue history and results, i.e., what the neural network outputted, usually take up the largest part. When the context overflows, something has to be cut. You can compress the history. You can output only what is needed on demand by calling sub-agents. Or you can store information in files and remove it from the dialogue.

Precise Tools

Older models calculated poorly: adding 10 and 10, they might get 22, so they had to be constantly checked. In 2023, when Python appeared in the sandbox, LLMs effectively got a calculator. The neural network understands from the context that a calculation is needed, writes code for it, and runs it. This way, it gets an accurate answer instead of guessing.

How does this work? A neural network can only do one thing: continue text. It cannot run a program or check the weather on its own, so it writes a request. Then the harness works. It checks if such a tool exists and if the model is allowed to use it. If everything is in order, the harness makes the call itself and returns the result to the model.

Chain of Reasoning

Previously, neural networks couldn't build a reasoning loop. For this, ReAct was invented: think, act, observe, repeat. This is the most crucial part of the harness: the quality of the result depends on how well this loop performs.

LangChain company tested its agents with the GPT-5.2 model on 89 tasks in the terminal. They noticed that sometimes tests didn't run or the LLM didn't finish something it intended to. Gradually, through iterations and skill edits, LangChain moved the model from 30th place to 5th.

But many tools don't mean it's good. Another example is from Vercel. They had an agent with 17 tools, and it periodically made mistakes in one out of five tasks. Then Vercel started experimenting and disabling tools. The context decreased, and eventually, with four necessary tools, the tasks were completed 100%. Therefore, an agent that selects tools for other agents is important.

It's also important to understand that errors accumulate with each reasoning step. If the accuracy of one step is 99%, a chain of 10 steps will complete without errors in 90% of cases, and a chain of 50 steps will complete without errors in only 60%. Every tenth task fails. In the shopping cart example, tests catch the error after each edit, before the agent says "done.".

This is solved by a development specification. It outlines goals and acceptance criteria, what and where to read, the output format, prohibitions, and self-checks. Ideally, a separate specification is needed for each task. OpenSpec is a good example of such a tool.

Harness That Improves Itself

Finally, the trend for the second half of 2026 is self-configuration. It's not complicated: the agent works, logs and sessions accumulate, and a configurator looks for recurring problems and fixes skills, scripts, and checks.

Some market players have already set this up for themselves. Data from a day is aggregated and sent for human review, and they decide whether to update the harness. In Google Research Harness 2, this happens automatically.

Here's one case of automatic harness correction at MWS:

The agent fixes bugs in the shopping cart. Its wrapper includes rules (AGENTS.md), skills (bug fixing), and tools (files, terminal), but no hooks. Six sessions occur per day, and in three of them, the agent submits the work without running tests. Logs from all sessions go into the knowledge base. Once a day, the configurator takes the logs, sees that three out of six sessions lacked tests, and adds a hook to the wrapper: "do not submit without tests." The next day, all six sessions pass with tests.

How Does It Work at MWS?

MWS Track is a task tracker where you can assign a person or an agent as the executor. The agent returns the result to the task, and a person checks it during review.

MWS Code is a model proxy with its own wrapper, appearing as a console client. Developers can use both external and internal models. Everything is logged: what is used and how. Statistics help understand how to develop further.

Agent Registry is a pilot project through which you can get pre-configured agents for your tasks.

AI Memory is a pilot project that collects all information from services, libraries, documentation, and infrastructure. It works similarly to RAG: a separate agent searches for information in AI Memory and provides it in the required format to the agent working with it.

Optimizing Harness in a Large Organization

Ekaterina Ivanova, lead developer at MWS, spoke about optimizing AI launch in the "Digital Employee" project.

For the user, the product is a chatbot in Telegram and Max. It's a digital twin for each client that acts as a concierge. The agent remembers data relevant to the client, stores their addresses and preferences. It behaves proactively, remembers the user's behavioral patterns, and works with over 30 skills.

Usually, the user only sees the prompt and the LLM's work. But under the hood, there's the agent process and its Gateway (OpenClaw or Hermes), memory management, tools, browser and scheduler, identification, and agent lifecycle.

Accelerating Launch

When a user types a prompt in the chat, they wait an average of 37.6 seconds. Depending on conditions, the wait can reach 53.5 seconds. If all necessary components are launched and kept constantly running, it reduces to 24 seconds. The AI's response itself takes 2.3–4.5 seconds; the rest of the time is spent on preparation. What are these seconds spent on?

  • 4 s — operator, i.e., the service that transfers the agent's manifest to Kubernetes;

  • 10 s — attaching a disk to the node;

  • 5 s — mounting the disk in the pod;

  • 5 s — initializing containers (browser, sidecar, etc.);

  • 9.5 s — loading the agent's Gateway: instructions, plugin connection;

  • 3.1 s — readiness check;

  • 1 s — delivering files and addresses to the pod.

How to eliminate this waiting time and what architectures are there for this?

  • Own pod for each user. This is convenient to manage, agents are isolated, it's easy to roll back one user or all. But each cold start takes tens of seconds, and the user's first experience is negative. This option is currently in production.

  • MicroVM per session. The strongest isolation. But startup is much slower, operation is much more complex, and memory consumption is even higher.

  • A shared dispatcher for everyone. One process routes users and handles everything through sub-agents. But if it fails, all users are affected. Moreover, you need to decide where to store each user's memory and how to manage the agent's lifecycle.

  • Warmed-up slots. This is the option chosen now. All heavy preparation is done in advance; the user only waits for the agent to start. But the system's cognitive complexity increases: a shared storage, an assignment registry, and protection against data sharing between owners are needed. This option still needs to be finalized and brought to production.

When each user has their own pod, it's linked to all Kubernetes resources using the user's UUID with prefixes and suffixes. Even a database isn't needed for this.

When slots appear, you need a place to store who owns a particular slot. This is where a PostgreSQL registry comes in, with slot statuses and their association with users. There is no more than one active slot per active user. When user A leaves, their slot should not be assigned to user B. A connection between the user, storage, and slot is needed. Also, Kafka needs to send the necessary events for this.

Returning to 37.6 seconds. How to reduce this time? All numbers below are for OpenClaw.

  • Moving pod startup on the relevant node, delivery of settings and keys to warmed-up slots. This saves 4 seconds, leaving 33.6. In return, the system becomes more complex: a pool of warmed-up slots and user assignments in PostgreSQL appear.

  • Addressing disk connection and mounting. Instead of block storage and a disk for each user, we use shared storage on CephFS. This saves 15 seconds, leaving 18.6. Control over what the user sees becomes more complex.

  • Finally, initializing containers and agent modules. They are pre-warmed: from 2 seconds for OpenClaw to 5 seconds for Hermes. Warming saves 5 seconds, but the request adds slot reservation for the client (0.8 s) and agent wake-up with tokens (0.3 s), and Gateway loading increases to 11.5 s. As a result, the gain at this step is 1.8 seconds.

After all steps, the time is reduced from 37.6 to 16.8 seconds, but the goal is a maximum of 6–8 seconds for the entire infrastructure. To achieve this, we'll have to delve into the agent's implementation, for example, by forking it. The team will move in this direction. For now, the time for the initial Gateway load remains, but the next response will be noticeably faster: instead of 9–11 seconds, the Gateway will take less than 0.5 seconds.

With each step, the system becomes more complex, and new problems arise.

Shared Storage Issues

The main problem with the new approach is the shared disk. In a simple model where each user has their own pod, you could mount block storage and have a separate disk for each client. This scheme doesn't work in an architecture with a warm pool: you can't mount a disk to already warmed-up slots. Therefore, shared storage in RWX format appears, and when a slot wakes up, a specific client's folder is given to it. In the current setup, all slots see a common root, and due to permission reassignment via root or group, a user might see their neighbors. To avoid this, access control needs to be moved to the storage level: mount subvolumes instead of folders and grant access to them via keys. This will further complicate the system. Most likely, a separate service will be needed to monitor this.

Next is the agent's workspace, which may contain files from different owners. They are divided into visibility areas:

  • managed — delivered as configs and versioned, updated, and deleted strictly according to the registry;

  • protected — user profiles and addresses. These are collected and maintained by data and ML engineers, so these files are not touched;

  • runtime — agent files, they cannot be touched or overwritten;

  • user-owned — user files, they also cannot be touched or overwritten;

  • seed — files delivered once and then forgotten, for example, during onboarding.

Result: you can only delete and update your own files, and the client's context must survive sleep and updates.

Agent Issues

Another problem is related to the emergence of new runtime frameworks. A new runtime should not break the overall operation or require the entire system to be rewritten for it. Therefore, a common contract is defined that works with any agent, and runtime specifics are moved to an adapter and separate configuration files.

Agents need to be not only started quickly but also put to sleep quickly when a user leaves or is thinking. Currently, this works based on the last activity timestamp. If the last activity was from the user, the slot is shut down after 30–35 minutes. If the slot was initiated by a periodic Cron task, it's shut down after 5 minutes. User activity alone cannot determine if they have finished or are thinking about an LLM question and should be waited for. To solve this, you need to delve into the agent's runtime. There's also Jev, which makes decisions in 0.5 seconds, while ordinary models need 6–8 seconds. There's a hypothesis that Jev, based on client metrics, can determine if they have left or are thinking. If the hypothesis is confirmed, user activity can be determined within 5 minutes.

Metrics from all participants are collected via Prometheus and Grafana. On the agent, traces are viewed in Langfuse: they show when the model started working. Agent logs are also checked to understand how long the Gateway ran and which phases consumed the seconds.

Some problems have already been solved, and work is ongoing on the rest.

What has changed in the processes?

  • Kirill (Sber). We've experimented a lot in recent years. Initially, agents performed part of the intellectual work, and now we've reached a point where they can accelerate any work, including in the operational center. Which models to use: external or internal?

  • Egor (TechVkus). We use both. It's a choice between control and speed. Local models are secure, but you need to find hardware and personnel for them and put them into operation, which takes time. An external model significantly reduces launch time and remains the most advanced at the moment. With external models, we approach sensitive and personal data responsibly. Another issue with external models is that the provider becomes part of the business, which carries separate risks. When you start thinking about risks, you prepare orchestration, fallbacks, a Plan B, and so on. For our product and team, this is acceptable, but it wouldn't work in fintech or medtech.

How do roles function within the team?

  • Valentin (MWS). Currently, roles are inherited from PDLC/SDLC. This is an intermediate stage: we are used to working within a specific process. Therefore, each stage is still led by the corresponding specialist, but together with an agent. The agent is a copilot; it has no responsibility. The person working with it is responsible for the process and the result, while the agent only assists.

  • Egor (TechVkus). There are common practices and personal initiative. Separate knowledge sharing isn't needed yet; team leads and tech leads handle that.

  • Kirill (Sber). Agents have two areas of ownership: context and harness. Context is formed by a person, who is also responsible for it. The context owner is a developer, architect, or a specific agent user. A separate department is responsible for harness development.

What is the impact of agent implementation?

  • Kirill (Sber). Overall, agents help significantly: incidents have halved, stories per developer have increased by 25%. The amount of code has increased multiplicatively, but not all of it reaches production.

  • Valentin (MWS). Agents speed up specific operations. However, to reduce time to market, end-to-end processes need a complete overhaul. In enterprise, they have been established over years and decades, and changing a process can be difficult; everything is very inertial.

  • Egor (TechVkus). A/B testing is necessary: take a team without agents and a team with agents, assign them the same product, and measure. Then you can arrive at a specific number. Product managers are delighted: an agent can examine the entire database and write queries, create prototypes in Figma, and all of this works well.   • Egor (TechVkus). I'll answer from the product managers' perspective. We don't want this. We are strongly against it. We don't like it, but most likely, we'll end up there.

Economics of AI Implementation in Business Overall

So far, we've mainly discussed AI in IT, but the final presentation by Daria Nikitina from IBS was about implementing AI in business as a whole. To sell something to businesses, you need to explain the benefits they will receive.

By 2026, over 80% of large companies in Russia will be using or testing LLMs. However, only 5–10% of them achieve the expected results. Globally, about 90% of companies use neural networks, and only 35% notice the impact of AI on EBIT.

Why isn't AI implementation taking off?

  • The wrong process was chosen for automation. Solution: process audit.

  • There's no way to measure the effect; success criteria are not formulated. Solution: effect planning.

  • Overinflated expectations without considering other costs and risks. Solution: also effect planning.

The good news is that this can be prevented by auditing processes. For a quick audit, all processes are listed, and each is evaluated across six blocks (in parentheses is the block's weight in the final score):

  • Process (25%): how often it is performed and how repetitive it is;

  • Data and Technology (20%): whether electronic data exists for the process;

  • Risks (20%): how significant the cost of an error is;

  • Control (15%): whether humans need to control the process at all stages;

  • Effects (15%): what is expected from implementation, we'll discuss them below;

  • Organizational Readiness (5%): AI implementation is difficult in conservative industries, as well as where management is not interested in such development.

This matrix looks something like this. The first columns show process names, followed by answers to 22 questions for each process, grouped into six blocks. A scoring of business processes is calculated based on the evaluations. A process with a score of 4 to 5 can be implemented; from 3 to 3.99 — implement with human control; from 2 to 2.99 — launch a pilot in assistant mode; below 2 — do not implement.

So, we have selected a few processes for AI implementation. Now we need to plan the effects. For each process, you can choose one or two evaluation approaches. The first three approaches are easy to prove; the last two have the greatest growth potential, although they are harder to prove.

  1. Direct Savings. This is a reduction in payroll costs, calculated by measuring hours before and after implementation.

  2. Business Scalability. Business growth without increasing staff and payroll.

  3. Reduction of Expenses and Costs. Fewer risks, fines, and taxes.

  4. Revenue Growth. Achieved by expanding the sales funnel and accelerating work with it.

  5. Customer Retention. Agents improve service quality and support speed.

While the first approach is easy to prove, the fifth requires calculating intermediate metrics and dividing customers into two groups: with AI and without. To assess the effect, it's sufficient to choose one approach; a second can be added. If you want to prove the effect, use approaches 1–3; if you want to show ambitious plans, use approaches 4 and 5.

Once the decision to implement AI is made, a process audit is conducted using the matrix, and 2–3 processes are selected for the pilot. Then, effects are planned, metrics are chosen, and pilot results are evaluated based on them. Based on the outcomes, a decision is made on whether to scale the implementation, and a refinement plan is developed.

From day one, you need:

  • a process owner interested in implementation;

  • IT and data, because nothing will take off without quality data;

  • risks and information security, without which large B2B clients do not operate;

  • a budget: it's important to understand who is interested in implementation and who will pay for it;

  • key users on whom the implementation is carried out and results are verified.
