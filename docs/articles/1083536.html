For the past few days, I've been watching what's happening around OpenAI, and there's one little thing that's bothering me a bit.

It's not because another powerful model has been released—we're used to that. It's because the people who have been accelerating this race in recent years have suddenly started saying that it would be good to slow down.

On September 3rd, OpenAI released GPT-6 Astra. The model is powerful, there are many benchmarks—everything is as usual. But I was more struck by one line from OpenAI's own materials.

Astra became the company's first model to be assigned a Critical level for cybersecurity capabilities within the Preparedness Framework.

If we remove the complex wording, the meaning is roughly this: with the right tools and access, the model is already capable of finding unknown vulnerabilities and developing ways to exploit them. Moreover, a human doesn't need to hold its hand and tell it what to do at each next step; it's enough to indicate the starting direction.

OpenAI writes that because of this, they even slowed down certain development stages while strengthening defenses.

A little over a week passes, and the heads of the largest AI companies begin to discuss not how to make the next model faster, but how to slow down this race a bit. Dario Amodei says that model capabilities are growing faster than security systems. Musk supports the idea of restrictions. Altman also talks about the need for coordination between labs.

In parallel, OpenAI postpones its IPO, and talks about slowing down the AI race have already managed to shake the stocks of AI-related companies. This creates a somewhat strange picture. For several years, everyone was pressing the gas, and now the people behind the wheel are the first to say that it seems like there's a turn ahead.

I think the problem isn't even that AI has become smarter

We constantly discuss the intelligence of models: how much they scored on benchmarks, how they write code, whether they solve olympiad problems, how much better they are than the previous version.

But over the past couple of years, another change has occurred that seems much more important to me. Previously, the scheme was simple. You ask ChatGPT a question, it answers something, you read it, and then you decide what to do with it yourself. That is, there was always a human between the model and the real action. Even if the neural network confidently wrote complete nonsense, the maximum that happened was that nonsense appeared on the screen.

With new models, you immediately state what you want to get in the end. Then the agent breaks down the task into steps, searches for information, opens files, writes code, runs tools, checks the results, and if it doesn't work, tries again. And here, the human gradually disappears from the middle of this chain. It's convenient. In fact, that's why agents are being developed. But then another question arises.

What if it made a mistake not at the last step, but at the first?

Suppose the agent misunderstood the initial task or someone misinterpreted it. After that, it took twenty more perfectly logical actions based on a false premise. Each subsequent action might be absolutely correct. The only problem is that the entire chain is going in the wrong direction, and the human might not even be watching what's happening during this time. And it seems to me that this is much more interesting than discussions about whether AI has consciousness.

We've always tried to remove humans from the process

It's quite amusing. For years, the human factor was perceived more as a drawback of neural networks.

AI wrote the code, but the developer has to check it. Well, it hasn't matured yet. The agent prepared the payment, but a human has to press confirm. So, the automation isn't complete. AI processed the documents, but an employee checks the result. Again, a human is needed.

And the end goal of development seems obvious: give the system a task and get the finished result without interfering in the process at all.

I myself looked at it this way for a long time, but now I'm no longer sure if this is the right end goal. Perhaps, in certain places in the process, a human is not a crutch at all. Perhaps, it should be this way.

AI can simply read email. Or it can read it and give feedback automatically without your knowledge. It can find a vulnerability. But should it try to exploit it? It can write code, test it, and ensure everything works. But is it necessary for it to deploy it to production independently? And the smarter the models become, the stranger the situation gets.

The more the agent can do, the more important becomes not what it's allowed to do, but where it's forbidden to continue without human intervention.

Of course, it's logical to recall the "Terminator" scenario at this point

Not in the sense of "that's it, Skynet is coming tomorrow." We are still very far from that, if such a scenario is even possible. But I suddenly saw the plot from a slightly different angle.

We usually remember it like this: we created artificial intelligence, it gained consciousness, decided that humans pose a threat, and launched missiles. Then come the Schwarzenegger-esque actions. And therefore, when someone compares modern neural networks to Skynet, it sounds a bit ridiculous.

ChatGPT doesn't hate people. It's not afraid of dying. It doesn't want to take over the world. And besides, the question of whether it has any "wants" at all is more philosophical than technical right now. But if we recall the story itself, there was another important detail. Humans handed over control of systems to Skynet because the machine could make decisions faster than humans. And when they tried to regain control, it was already too late.

And this part of the story, for some reason, doesn't seem so fantastic today. For problems to arise, it's not at all necessary for AI to become evil. It doesn't even particularly need consciousness for that.

Let's imagine a much more boring scenario. There is a very powerful system. It has a goal. It has access to tools. It can independently plan its next actions. And at the very beginning, it misunderstood what was expected of it. That's it. From then on, it can conscientiously and very effectively do the wrong thing. And much faster than a human.

Perhaps it's speed that starts working against us here. While the neural network is simply responding with text, a human has time to look at the response and say, "What nonsense is this?"

When the agent, in the same amount of time, manages to perform twenty actions, this option may no longer be available.

And it turns out to be a rather amusing paradox. For several years, we tried to create AI that we could tell: "Here's the task. Figure it out yourself."

And now, it seems, we will have to learn to create AI that we can tell: "Here's the task. Figure it out yourself. But stop here and definitely ask me."

Perhaps the true frontier of AI agent development is not complete autonomy, but the system's ability to understand the moment when autonomy should end. And if we return to "Terminator," humanity lost, perhaps, not when Skynet became sentient, but a little earlier—when people decided it was smart enough not to watch it anymore. I would really not want to find out someday that precisely this part of the movie turned out to be the most realistic.
