Hello, Habr. This is Nikolay Karasov, lead developer at the content services recommendation department at AI VK. Our team is responsible for recommendations in the VKontakte Feed.

We had a goal to develop a new user scenario within VKontakte: a person comes for content, sees an interesting product from an author, and proceeds to purchase directly from the social network. Thus, a new object type appeared in the Feed – a product post. The first partner in this scenario was Ozon. From now on, we will talk specifically about author product posts within the Ozon referral program, not about any posts with products.

It was clear that a regular recommender was not suitable for this scenario. As a result, we built a separate product contour within the Feed: with its own logging, candidate selectors, ranking model, and a dedicated quota in the final output. I will talk about this.

Why a product cannot simply be added to a regular recommender

The VKontakte Feed has been optimized for content actions for many years: likes, subscriptions, comments, engagement, time spent in the feed. The ranker is good at predicting whether a person will watch a video or give a heart. It knows nothing about purchases – simply because such an event was not in its training data.

The difference here is not in the number of features, but in the objective function. For a regular post, the goal of recommendation is for a person to view it, like it, or stay in the Feed. For a product post, the goal is different – a purchase.

There is a long funnel between a like and a purchase, and it branches into two scenarios. In the first, a person opens the product card, goes into the VKontakte Market, and from there goes to the partner. In the second, they expand the post text and go to the partner via a direct link. Both end the same way – by placing an order. Each step cuts off part of the audience, and optimizing the first step does not guarantee growth in the subsequent ones.

Therefore, for us, a product is not just another type of content, but a recommendation object with a different objective function. This leads to two practical consequences:

  1. Ranking product posts by the same criteria as regular ones is insufficient. A model that maximizes engagement will promote posts with a bright image and a catchy headline. This may not lead to a purchase.

  2. Different data is needed for training. The history of likes does not answer the question of which products and product categories are of interest to a particular person. A regular feed simply does not have such a history.

Formally, the task is as follows: *from the catalog of product posts that can be included in recommendations, select the most relevant ones for a specific user*. To do this, the model estimates for each "user – product" pair the probability that a display will lead to an order. We are not trying to answer the more general question of "is the person ready to buy at all" – such a formulation is too abstract and not very useful for ranking. The candidate catalog itself currently contains about 74 thousand posts with products.

Cold start: nothing to train the model on

The purchase scenario existed in VKontakte even before the launch of the new recommender, but its share in the overall Feed remained small. For the recommendation system, this meant a limited number of product candidates and, consequently, less data for training. But a small catalog was not the main difficulty in itself. The problem turned out to be cyclical:

  1. There are few product posts. Compared to the total volume of the Feed, this is a fraction of a percent of candidates.

  2. The probability of displaying a product post is extremely low. The ranker has no reason to promote an object about which it has no signals.

  3. Almost no interactions are collected. No displays – no clicks, no transitions, let alone orders.

  4. There is no training data. And without data, the model will not learn to promote product posts.

The circle is closed: for the model to learn to show products, it needs data on product displays.

100% logging for one type of candidate

The first solution was infrastructural. In a regular feed, we log about 2% of events. This is a conscious compromise: collecting 100% of the activity of a multi-million audience is too expensive in terms of both data volume and infrastructure, and for training content models, a 2% sample is more than enough – there are still a lot of events.

For product content, the arithmetic breaks down. Displays are already rare, and 2% of rare displays is a statistical zero. A training set will never appear in this mode.

We immediately rejected the obvious solution – to increase the logging percentage for the entire Feed: the cost of such a step is incomparable to the task. Instead, we built the work with the Discovery platform such that the logging percentage is set separately for a specific type of candidate. For product posts, we increased it to 100%.

It looks like this: there wasn't enough data – we didn't log the entire Feed – we implemented selective 100% logging only for the new domain. There are relatively few product candidates, so the additional load is incomparable to full Feed logging. And the dataset for the new domain is complete. In numbers, the transition from two percent to one hundred increased the training set for the product domain by 15 times.

This is probably the most underestimated technique when launching a new recommendation domain. Usually, a team first chooses a model architecture and then discovers that there is nothing to train it on. It's more useful the other way around: first, answer the question of where the data will come from, and make sure that the sampling configured for old tasks does not kill the new one.

What we used instead of orders

Even with full logging, the target event remains rare. An order is the bottom of the funnel, and at the start, its frequency is close to zero. We used four techniques simultaneously.

1. Similar historical signals

We looked at how users previously interacted with commercial content: with product showcases of communities in the VKontakte Market and with product publications by authors. Such content existed in VKontakte even before the launch of the new scenario, which means there is a history for it. It provides the first signals of product interest even before purchase history appears.

2. Simple tops by segments

If nothing is known about a person in the product domain, personalization is impossible. In such a situation, we rely on tops based on more general characteristics: gender, age, and product popularity by click-out. This is crude, but it allows us to start showing at least basically suitable products and collect the first real signals. Then, the person automatically exits this mode: as soon as they have their own product history, personal selectors start working.

3. Training on more frequent targets

Initially, the model learned on available signals – primarily positive reactions. As more data became available, the targets gradually shifted closer to the target action: to transitions and orders. This is not a one-time replacement, but a planned migration: as events accumulate, the lower stages of the funnel become statistically reliable, and the weight in training shifts to them.

4. Expand as an early proxy for purchase

The most illustrative example of target evolution. We noticed that a significant portion of transitions to the partner goes through the link in the post text. And to click this link, a person first needs to expand the text – perform an expand.

The logic is simple: expanded the text – became interested – the probability of transitioning to the partner is higher. Expand correlated significantly with subsequent transitions and, at the same time, occurred many times more often than the transition itself. In the early stages, it became one of the key proxy targets.

The technique can be applied to any cold domain. If the target event is too rare, find an event one step higher in the funnel, check its correlation with the target, and train the model on it. The main thing is not to forget to switch back to the actual target later: a proxy is always slightly different.

What signals does the system collect

Signals are divided into two groups. Content signals – the same as for a regular post:

  • view;

  • view time;

  • like;

  • comment;

  • photo opening;

  • carousel swipe;

  • post text expansion (expand);

  • dislike;

  • hide and report.

And product signals – those that a regular feed does not have:

  • transition to product card in VKontakte Market;

  • transition to partner (click-out);

  • order.

A transition to a partner has two different paths, and this is important for attribution. The first is directly via a link in the post text. The second is through the product card in the VKontakte Market; and the purchase button on it. The paths provide signals of different strengths, so we distinguish them.

How we find candidates

Source 1. Product behavior on the partner's side

We receive user behavior signals from the partner in encrypted form and build collaborative representations based on them. This refers to events of various types: purchase, card opening, adding to cart, adding to favorites, as well as their combination.

In VK, feature modeling, counters, and recommendation mechanics work on the Discovery platform. Based on these representations, selectors work to find product posts that are close to the user's behavior with the partner.

Source 2. Content proximity

The second source is more interesting because it works even for a person without a product history. The starting point is ordinary content interest.

The scheme is simple: a person likes a post about books – the system finds product publications that are content-wise close to this post, for example, posts with books – they are included in the candidate set – then the ranker decides whether to show them to this person or not. That is, a product recommendation can start with an ordinary content signal.

End-to-end example

Let's break down the entire process step by step. Essentially, these are item2item recommendations: the anchor is a regular VKontakte post, the candidate is a post with a product.

  1. A person views a post with a recipe. An ordinary content post, it has nothing to do with products.

  2. They like or save it. This is a clear positive signal.

  3. The signal enters the recommendation system. The event goes into the general stream and becomes available for building the next output.

  4. When forming the next batch of recommendations, the system sees a fresh positive signal. Not just any event from history is considered fresh: the system looks at the three most recent posts for each positive action, with priority given to the most recent one.

  5. A separate selector searches among product posts for content-wise similar options. In the case of a recipe, this would be cookware, knives, recipe books, and other related products.

  6. These posts are included in the general candidate set. They have no privileges at this stage.

  7. The model evaluates the relevance of each candidate for a specific person. This is where the fate of the product post is decided.

  8. If the score is high, the probability of display increases. The person sees the product post and potentially proceeds to purchase.

Multi-target ranking: order is more important than a like

Ranking is done using gradient boosting, specifically CatBoost. The model has multiple objectives simultaneously, meaning it's multi-target ranking. The priority of events is structured as follows:

  1. Order. The main goal of the contour, maximum weight.

  2. Transition to partner, click-out. The next strongest step in the funnel.

  3. Lighter positive interactions with content. Likes, comments, view time – they indicate interest, but not purchase intent.

  4. At the bottom are irrelevant displays, quick scrolls, and negative feedback. The model penalizes such outcomes.

For commercial content, weights are set so that publications that are more likely to lead to a purchase are promoted higher during ranking, followed by transitions to a partner, and only then are more general engagement signals considered.

The product logic behind this is: not to maximize CTR at any cost, but to build a chain from interest in a product to an order. If you only optimize the top of the funnel, you can easily get a nice click metric and zero sales.

How the product contour lives within the Feed

Product recommendations are not a separate independent feed. They exist within the general recommendation mechanism but have their own quota. This scheme gives us three things:

  1. Control over the share of product content. We explicitly set a maximum, rather than leaving this question to the general ranker.

  2. Own relevance filters. Product candidates are subject to requirements that regular content does not have.

  3. Separate optimization of transitions and orders. The contour can be improved independently of the rest of the feed.

In terms of magnitude, product content is mixed into the output with a limited probability. Basically, it's 1.5%. But if a post with a high-converting product is found for a specific user – according to the model's assessment – the quota momentarily increases to 5%. The majority of the Feed remains regular content, and this is a fundamental position: the Feed should not turn into a storefront.

On top of the quota, there is another protective layer – the relevance threshold. After ranking, posts with a score below the threshold are not shown at all. This means the system is not obligated to fill the product quota at any cost: if there are no good candidates for this person at the moment, they simply won't see product posts. The quota is a ceiling, not a plan.

The threshold itself is selected based on offline validation: it was chosen so that the majority of click-outs and orders remain to its right. The second limitation comes from runtime – the threshold should not drive up dislikes.

A product post can also be removed from the output for another reason – due to stockouts on the partner's side. When a product runs out, the post with it is removed from recommendations and returns as soon as stocks reappear.

How not to flood the Feed with identical products

As the number of candidates grew, a new problem emerged – categorical diversity. When the catalog is small, there's no question: just show something suitable. When there are many candidates, ranking develops an unpleasant property: if a person shows interest in one category, the top by score can easily be filled with products from that category.

We are currently testing a mechanism that ensures that in a certain output window, identical categories are not repeated – provided that good candidates from other categories are available. We are trying windows of 2, 5, and 10 positions. The category is taken from the topic tree, not from a separate classifier. An important caveat here: diversity should not come at the expense of relevance. If there are no alternatives of decent quality, the mechanism will not substitute a weak candidate for the sake of formal diversity – the threshold will work, and the position will remain for regular content.

What the Discovery platform gave us

This whole story relies heavily on the Discovery platform – our common infrastructure layer for recommendations, search, and advertising. The platform provided ready-made mechanisms and components, so some tasks that would previously have required writing separate services and a lot of code are now solved at the configuration level.

The most striking example was mentioned earlier: a separate logging percentage for a candidate type. Outside the platform, this would have been a task to modify the event collection pipeline with a separate release cycle. Within the platform, it's a setting.

The effect on the project was speed:

  1. Experiments were conducted faster. Less infrastructure work for each hypothesis.

  2. More A/B tests were run in the same period. The team's hypothesis throughput increased.

  3. Working solutions were reached faster. More tested hypotheses mean finding a successful configuration sooner.

  4. Exceeded initially expected growth in product metrics. The conversion plan was overfulfilled by half, and display by threefold. Conversion and displays grew simultaneously, meaning transitions to the partner increased multiplicatively.

What we got in numbers

Let's compare two identical windows: March 1-30 and August 1-30. Five months of contour work lie between them.

  1. End-to-end conversion "display → created order" increased by 53%. This is the main project metric: it shows that a separate contour for purchase intent works better than regular ranking.

  2. Created orders – a 43.2-fold increase. The final action of the entire funnel.

  3. Transitions to partner – a 55.3-fold increase. The step in the funnel just before the order.

  4. Views of product posts – a 28.3-fold increase. This is due to both the quota and the fact that there are now significantly more candidates in the system.

  5. CTR of transition to partner from post increased by 95.6%. Almost doubled.

These numbers should be read separately because they refer to different things. The 28-fold increase in views is volume: there are more product posts in the system, and the quota has started to let them through. And conversion and CTR are quality: with the same display, a person has become significantly more likely to reach an order. The increase in orders is a combination of both effects and breaks down almost exactly: 28.3 × 1.53 ≈ 43.2.

The separation is also important because volume hits a ceiling. The quota is limited, the catalog does not grow infinitely, and further gains come mainly from the quality of ranking. That is why conversion is the main metric for us, not the number of displays.

Side effect: authors brought more product posts

There is a product effect that we did not directly aim for. The number of product publications in author shops has increased to about 5,000 per day – this includes posts and clips together, according to data from early September. The reason is not content procurement, but the fact that authors saw that recommendations actually reach an audience, which means they can earn money from product posts.

This resulted in a positive cycle: better recommendations – larger audience for product posts – greater motivation for authors to create such content – more candidates in the system – even better opportunities for recommendations.

For the recommendation team, this is an important point. The quality of recommendations in a marketplace scenario is not just about user convenience. It directly affects the supply: the candidate catalog grows on its own if it's profitable for authors to fill it. Conversely, weak recommendations in a new domain stifle supply and drive the project back into a cold start.

What to take away from this

The main idea of the project in one sentence: we didn't teach a regular recommender to sell. We built a separate contour within it, which gradually learned to distinguish interest in content from purchase intent.

If you are launching recommendations in a new domain on top of an existing system, here's what we would advise checking first:

  1. Start with data, not the model. Sampling configured for old tasks can completely nullify the training set of a new domain. Check this before choosing an architecture.

  2. Look for a targeted solution instead of a global one. Increasing logging for the entire feed is expensive and unnecessary. Increasing it for one type of candidate is cheap and sufficient.

  3. Build a ladder of targets. Start with frequent proxy events like expand, and as data accumulates, move closer to the actual goal. And decide in advance on what criterion you will move.

  4. Separate quota from plan. A relevance threshold on top of the quota eliminates the temptation to fill a slot with any candidate. An empty position is often better than a bad recommendation.

  5. Keep content metrics in acceptance criteria. Otherwise, the commercial contour will sooner or later pay for its growth with the quality of the main product.
