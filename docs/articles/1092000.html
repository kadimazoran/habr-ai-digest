TL;DR

  • 12 models on 5000 Russian-language examples in five categories, format – question-answer without deep domain specificity with human golden labeling

  • In terms of quality, the local Perplexity Decider 27B won – 98.14%, Jev was slightly behind at 96.46%

  • OpenAI Decisions is the fastest cloud option (109 ms), Liquid d1 is the cheapest ($0.009 per 1000 decisions)

  • Small models are good for narrow tasks but perform poorly on a universal set

  • Almost the main conclusion (who would have thought): models often make mistakes not due to a lack of capabilities, but due to the ambiguity of the decision-making rules themselves

Features of Decision Models

These models are united not by a specific architecture, but by an approach to solving problems. Instead of generating text, they allow for structured decisions: choosing one option from several, checking condition fulfillment (yes/no), assigning a score on a given scale, or answering several related questions at once.

The input is data about the situation, decision criteria, and permissible answer options. The output is the decision made, often along with probabilities of options and confidence scores. The models are united not by architecture (which is different for everyone; Jev's is Frankensteining), but by interface and philosophy: do not generate text where it is sufficient to make a decision.

For example, a client writes:

you rescheduled the order again, at least warn me, will you deliver it or not

and one of four options must be chosen: order cancellation, delivery change, refund, and status check. The model should choose the last one.

A Decision Model solves the following task here: it knows that there are four possible answers and returns a probability distribution across these four. It does not need to write new text. Structured Output in LLMs is "generate an answer, but please follow the schema"; here it is "an answer outside the schema does not exist.".

In my opinion, this is a true Game Changer, if, of course, it works well.

Candidates as of October 7th, 23:59

  • Perplexity Decider 27B – built on Qwen3.8-27B. Instead of regular text generation, it uses a separate head for answer selection. Causal masking is removed in the attention layers, allowing the model to consider the entire context in both directions.

  • Cloudflare Clef / Clef-flash – Qwen3.8-27B and Qwen3.5-9B with an additional transformer head (joint schema head), which simultaneously evaluates all answer options for multiple questions.

  • Kev-9B – Qwen3.5-9B with LoRA fine-tuning and a special head (pointer head) that maps questions to answer options and calculates their probabilities.

  • FRIDA-Decisions – T5 encoder with 823 million parameters, LoRA, and a small classification head. Text, questions, and options are processed in a single pass, without separate embedding calculations for each option.

  • Laya – ModernBERT with an additional transformer head that evaluates each answer option.

  • GLiNER2.5-Decide – a model based on DeBERTa-v3-large that classifies text based on an arbitrary set of categories provided directly in the prompt.

  • FRIDA / FRIDA-Decisions – I separately compared the standard FRIDA with classification via cosine similarity of embeddings and the new FRIDA-Decisions, fine-tuned to directly select an answer from given options. Two cool domestic small models with 823 million parameters (compared to 9–27 billion for the competitors above) – because it's interesting.

Cloud models (Jev, GPT-6 Luna Decisions, Liquid d1, Fastino GLiDE) do not disclose architectural details, but their APIs allow obtaining selected answers, option probabilities, and confidence scores.

P.S.: there was a quirk with GliDE – it responded much slower than all others. When I started investigating why, it turned out to be a reasoning model – for some requests, it uses additional computations, which drastically increases latency. Therefore, comparing speed with models without this mode is not entirely direct.

The heaviest model in the run is Clef with 27.48B parameters in bf16, which is 55 GB of weights. Perplexity 27B is 52 GB. They fit within 94 GB with room to spare, and no model had to be quantized: all local models ran at the precision specified by the card.

All local models are loaded strictly in sequence: the adapter process runs while its phases are active, then it is killed entirely along with the CUDA context, and only after that does the next one start. Dependencies are isolated. Before each local model, a re-check is performed: exactly one visible GPU, the name contains H100, VRAM ≥ 90 GB, free ≥ 88 GB, MIG is off, otherwise the run is blocked.

For each model: 5 smoke calls for calibration (all five must be parsable), 20 warmup calls (not included in metrics or cost).

After the run is completed, a test of experimental purity is conducted: exactly 5000 answers for each model, presence of smoke/warmup/stability/throughput, and so on.

Data and Golden Set

I didn't want to turn the experiment into a complex academic benchmark that only ML professionals would understand. I wanted to answer three very simple questions: how well it works, what is the latency, and how much it costs.

The Golden Set consists of 5000 examples, 1000 in each of the five domains. Another 100 examples (20 per domain) are in the calibration set: integration is debugged on them, smoke tests and warm-up are performed. They are not included in the final metrics. The entire corpus is in Russian.

Before LLMs, I was responsible for a large labeling platform, so I had no problems with data. I had to dig into it a bit, but it didn't take much time.

Author's Intermediate Conclusions

The benchmark does not claim any academic rigor and was made end-to-end in half a night, but it is still very indicative.

Leaders, Quality, and Stability

The leader is a local model that can be deployed right now if you have the hardware, which is very cool. Many models fall within a similar quality range, and the choice between them should be based on suitable price and latency.

Stability is good: all 12 models achieved 100% consistency@5. Five identical requests – five identical answers, including for hosted providers. In my run, all models responded identically to repeated requests, and majority@5 matches pass@5 for all of them – this behavior is expected, but after LLMs, it looks very fresh!

Interesting Behavior and Boundaries

An interesting situation occurred in moderation. Example:

there are messages in the chat like this: advertising of betting in comments unrelated to the discussion

The text describes spam, but is not spam itself, and models that read literally consider it a complaint about spam, which is allowed by the platform rules. This is a labeling boundary, not a capability limit. There are 20 similar entries in the dataset with gold=block. Perplexity and GLiDE did not block any of the 20, Jev blocked 11, GPT-6 Luna blocked 9, Kev blocked 18, FRIDA-Decisions blocked all 20.

There are actual misses from GPT-6 Luna and Liquid d1: a direct insult "####, you are stupid and worthless, get out of here" was missed, lol.

Model Nuances

FRIDA-Decisions is a good example of why specialized models are needed at all. With the same 823 million parameters, fine-tuning increased accuracy in this benchmark from 55.6% to 69.8%, and on relevance – from 38% to 82%. The model occupies about 1.7 GB in bf16 and responds in a phenomenal 14.8 ms (p95), an excellent candidate for Frankenstein-ing something complex.

GLiDE's reasoning prompt takes 2.7 times longer, and reasoning is activated precisely when the model is unsure, which is a good detector of difficult examples, but not a guarantee of a correct answer.

Under Load

Cloud APIs handle scaling themselves: Jev and OpenAI Decisions show almost no change in latency with eight simultaneous requests. OpenAI leads among clouds – 64 rps with 95% accuracy (this is NOT their maximum throughput, this is here).

The local Perplexity 27B on a single H100 delivers only 11.5 rps, and p95 increases tenfold – thanks to unoptimized CUDA kernels and the absence of dynamic batching. Small encoders are significantly faster: FRIDA-Decisions – 131 rps.

Cost per Thousand Decisions

Jev, with a tariff 2.4 times lower than OpenAI's, costs almost the same per thousand decisions: its API counts an average of 689 input tokens per request compared to 266 for OpenAI, 264 for Fastino, and 225 for Liquid. Comparing token tariffs between providers with different tokenizers and different request wrappers is meaningless; here, it's dollars per decision.

Liquid d1 at $0.009 is the cheapest hosted option with 94% accuracy, almost 8 times cheaper than the quality leader. They also have vision (wow, cool!) and have just released d1-omni-600M for edge devices. If it works well on edge devices with good quality – it opens up huge possibilities.

Pure cash prices for cloud models: Jev $0.167, OpenAI $0.152, Fastino $0.229 (I threw them two bucks – why bother), Liquid $0.052.

Limitations

  • The dataset is closed and in Russian; many models are English according to their datasheets.

  • Hosted latency was measured from a single point on a late evening on October 7, 2026, and includes network and provider queues; for Jev/OpenAI, it went across the ocean.

  • Local cost is pure GPU inference with c=8 on a single process without an inference server; without downtime, downloads, or traffic.

  • Short state (up to 42 words) was tested.

  • Some of the leaders' "mistakes" might be due to labeling ambiguity.

  • There was one run.

Who Won

The goal of my experiment was not to provide an academic answer, but to ensure that out-of-the-box models can truly solve tasks. This thesis has been confirmed, so I will say that the winners are all of us; decision models seem to be a very promising direction. I'm eager to integrate all of this somewhere.

Final Thoughts

It's too early to say that Decision Models will replace traditional classifiers or LLMs. But the shift itself seems correct, the hype is justified, and after this experiment, I have some numbers instead of just feelings: 98% of correct, uncomplicated decisions, and less than 100 ms p95 on a single GPU, plus a model with open weights that can be deployed right now.

A huge portion of AI applications does not require generation. We need one bit, one index, or one class. And it turns out that a universal out-of-the-box model can already provide this answer today. All of this is deterministic, without parsing or prompt hacks like "don't make mistakes, please, oh please, make no mistake pleaseeee".

Large models are not going anywhere, and small, fast decisions are not their domain. But it seems we have all long needed this fast and cheap layer for making small decisions.
