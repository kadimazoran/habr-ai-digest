You've implemented an AI agent into your work. It displays information about invoices, generates summaries, processes incoming messages, and does much more. But what level of automation are you at? And what's next?

In this article, we'll explore what "AI-ization" is, how to integrate an AI agent into a workflow, and why the next step is not necessarily a smarter agent, but a move towards an AI-Native approach to process management.

I identify five levels of AI-ization: from AI as a personal assistant to a system where processes are initially designed around AI capabilities and software automation.

This is my practical model, which I developed for myself and my clients to determine the necessary level of automation and choose the next step. It does not claim to be a universal maturity standard, but it helps to understand the current position and possible next steps.

We will consider all examples using one simple process – processing incoming requests.

Classic Process Without AI

This is our starting point.

Requests come in via phone, email, through CRM, or in a chat on the website. Channels can be combined into a single interface, or they can operate independently.

The operator receives the message, determines its essence, assigns a task to a specialist, or responds independently. After the task is completed, they receive the result and pass it on to the client.

What we have: the process works, but it depends on employees. Requests need to be read, classified, distributed, monitored, and closed manually.

Level 1. AI as a Personal Assistant

Many who start using AI agents consider this level the pinnacle of automation. In reality, it's just the beginning.

The operator uses AI to process messages or connects the agent to their communication channels. The agent can work with files, use tools, and even control a computer through Computer Use.

Now it is capable of classifying messages, preparing responses, searching for information, and creating tasks for specialists.

It would seem, here's happiness! But with the growth of the request flow, limitations begin to emerge: limits, request costs, delays, and the need to constantly monitor the result. The connector might disconnect, the agent might perform the wrong action, or it might respond to the client with something other than what was intended.

What has changed: the employee has started working faster, but the process itself has practically not changed. The human still remains its main operator.

Level 2. AI Agent Integrated into the Process

The next step is to move the agent from the workstation to the server and connect it directly to the process. For example, using OpenClaw, n8n, and similar tools, or a custom integration.

Now the agent monitors incoming requests around the clock, classifies them, searches for information, answers standard questions, and forwards complex cases to managers.

There are two approaches.

The first is a ready-made agent with broad authority. This is a relatively simple way to start, but with intensive use, the costs for models and tools can be substantial. Each request, conversation history, and additional actions consume resources. The cost depends on the model, context size, architecture, and tariffs.

The second is custom integration of LLM into the process. A specialist connects the model via API, and all predictable actions are performed by specialized software modules. The model determines what needs to be done, and the code handles the execution of specific operations.

This way, you can reduce processing costs, improve manageability, and eliminate unnecessary model calls.

What has changed: the agent has become part of the workflow and independently performs certain operations.

But for now, we've just delegated the work to AI. We haven't optimized the architecture of the process itself yet.

In my observation, most AI integrators are currently working at this level. You can reach this level independently without having the necessary knowledge, but the solution will likely be unstable.

Level 3. Specialized Models and Software Automation

This is the next stage – optimizing the previous level. At first glance, the changes are insignificant, but with a large flow of requests, they can yield significant results. Depending on the complexity, the restructuring process can take weeks or months.

Suppose a client sent an invoice, an act, a technical specification, or a quote. At the previous level, a large language model could receive the entire document, analyze it, and decide what to do next.

With a large volume of documents, this is not always justified. Moreover, the generation result can be unpredictable.

Therefore, we divide responsibilities:

  • a small specialized model classifies the request;

  • OCR or a document processing model extracts text and structure;

  • a specialized model extracts details and generates a structured result;

  • software code checks mandatory fields and performs calculations;

  • LLM is connected when interpretation of a complex or ambiguous request is required;

  • a large model is used only when its capabilities are truly necessary.

For example, the question "What is the status of order #1254?" does not require complex reasoning. The system extracts the number, accesses the CRM, and generates a response based on a template.

However, a client's claim describing several problems may require context analysis and the involvement of a more powerful model.

Specialized models can be run on your own servers if it is economically justified. Some tasks will require retraining, while others will be sufficient with a ready-made model.

What has changed: we stopped using a universal AI where a specialized tool can solve the task more cheaply and reliably.

Costs can decrease, processing speed can increase, and quality can become more predictable. But the result must be confirmed by measurements, not assumptions.

This is the level where a good IT specialist, ideally more than one, is already needed. Without them, such optimization cannot be built.

Level 4. End-to-End Process Automation

At this level, we automate not individual operations, but the entire path from an incoming request to a result.

The client sends a message. The system classifies it, extracts the necessary data, creates a task, assigns responsibility, monitors deadlines, and reminds about execution. After completion, it generates a response and sends it to the client.

If the request is standard, a human may not be involved in its processing at all.

If a non-standard calculation, agreement on terms, or a decision beyond the system's authority is required, an employee is involved.

It is here that we begin to systematically replace manual operations with automated ones. It is important not just to remove the human from the chain, but to ensure result control, exception handling, and the possibility of intervention.

What has changed: the system manages the entire process, not just executes commands. The human ceases to be a mandatory participant at every stage and primarily works with complex cases.

At this stage, ordinary IT competencies are no longer sufficient. One needs to understand not only technologies but also the business itself: how processes are structured, where losses occur, how to measure the effect of changes, and how to implement them so that the team truly starts working in a new way.

This is already a different engineering task, where not only development skills are important, but also experience in change management, systems thinking, and organizing people's work.

It is at the intersection of IT, business, and process management that we work: helping to identify bottlenecks, assess automation potential, and design solutions that can be tested in practice. It is best to start not with choosing a model or agent, but with a specific process, its initial indicators, and the desired outcome.

Level 5. AI-Native Approach to Process Management

AI-Native is not just another agent, nor is it necessarily a system where humans are completely excluded from work.

It is an approach where the process is designed from the outset considering the capabilities of AI, software automation, specialized models, and human involvement.

We no longer take an existing process and try to systematically replace employees with agents. We rethink the work architecture itself.

For example, a request processing system consists of several specialized components. One is responsible for classification, another for documents, a third for calculations, and a fourth for interaction with CRM. An orchestrator manages the sequence of operations, monitors the process state, and directs non-standard situations to the appropriate specialists or models.

The system collects statistics: processing cost, execution time, number of errors, proportion of requests handled without human intervention. Based on this data, bottlenecks can be identified and the process improved.

If a simple model handles a task well enough, there's no need to call a more expensive one. If an automatic scenario produces errors, it can be limited or rolled back to a previous version. If a task requires a human decision, it should be passed to a human.

At the same time, autonomy does not mean lack of control. On the contrary, the more authority the system receives, the more important monitoring, access rights, action limitations, and rollback capabilities become.

Frankly, I have not yet encountered fully AI-Native processes where the architecture, management, metrics, and feedback are initially built around AI capabilities and are also effective. Individual elements of this approach are already quite feasible.

What has changed: we have moved from implementing AI into existing work to designing the process itself around automation, data, and measurable results.

These levels describe not mandatory stages of maturity for every company, but different scales and principles of automation organization. Not every process needs all levels: sometimes a simple integration yields a better economic result than a complex system.

How to Implement AI Correctly?

Do not try to build an AI-Native enterprise or a fully autonomous process right away. Start with a specific task, measurable indicators, and an understanding of limitations.

As you move to higher levels, collect statistics, evaluate costs, speed, and quality, and verify results with real data. Provide for the possibility of disabling automation or reverting to a previous version if quality deteriorates.

AI can speed up work, but poorly designed automation can accelerate the spread of errors. Customer loyalty is earned slowly and lost quickly. Staff reductions can lead to a loss of expertise that will then need to be restored.

AI-ization is not a race for the number of agents. It is engineering work to ensure that business processes are performed faster, cheaper, and more reliably.
