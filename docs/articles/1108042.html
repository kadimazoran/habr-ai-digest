AI on the Team: What We Expect from Each Other

AI tools can speed up tasks, deepen understanding, and uncover missed solutions. However, they can also lead to unfinished work being passed on, endless refinements, and decision fatigue. This article explores how AI impacts team efficiency, why some tasks take longer, and how to evaluate human contribution when AI is involved. Drawing on project experience and research, it examines the expectations between team members and AI.

Repost of an article from my personal blog.

It has become very convenient to report on work done with neural networks. There is a document, it has considerable volume, and it is written convincingly. As for what to do with it next, let the next lucky person figure it out.

Suppose a colleague sends you twenty pages of requirements for a new feature. Scenarios, constraints, exceptions – all are present. You don't have time to read it now, so you ask your neural network to extract the main points. It, of course, handles it. And here it can easily turn out that two crucial decisions, without which development cannot begin, have not been made by anyone. But now we have both the full document and its summary.

Moreover, you don't even need to slack off to achieve such a result. The author tried to describe the task in detail, the recipient – to understand it faster. The model might not have lied anywhere. We simply managed to write much more than we managed to solve.

This part of working with AI is what worries me. What happens after the generation result is sent to colleagues? Who will sort it out, how much time will it take, and at what point can we say that the team has truly started working better?

Who Gets Your Acceleration

Unfinished work doesn't disappear just because it's nicely formatted and sent. The developer still needs to understand what to do. The manager – to check the conclusions. The adjacent team – to figure out which of the promised features actually exist.

Previously, at least time had to be spent on producing such a document. This means that new ideas could arise in the process, and unexpected problems could surface. Now, you can get it done much faster. This is convenient if you have truly mastered the subject and only need to present the solution. It's slightly worse if, due to the speed of generation, you managed to skip the actual understanding process.

A name has even been coined for this – workslop. In a survey by BetterUp Labs and Stanford Social Media Lab conducted in September 2025, employees described generated materials that looked complete but required the recipient to sort them out and refine them. Researchers are directly considering this as offloading work onto colleagues.

Suppose a document used to take a day, and now it takes an hour. Great. If the team quickly agreed on it and started building a working solution, the savings are obvious. But if the next person in the chain spent a whole day trying to extract meaning from the text and gather missing answers, I would hold off on the efficiency report for now.

Therefore, it's not enough for me to know how quickly a person got a result from the model. I'm interested in what happened to that result afterward. How many times was it returned, what had to be re-asked, and who ultimately brought it to a workable state.

The Neural Network Has Already Decided Everything

This story is particularly evident with project documentation. Let's take a simple design task: the system needs to transfer a request from one employee to another. A button, selecting a recipient, a notification. It seems like you can hand it over to the agent and move on to the next task.

Of course not. We don't know when the responsible person changes specifically. When the first employee clicked the button, or when the second agreed to accept the request? If the second hasn't agreed yet, who is responsible for its completion?

The answer determines what needs to be done at all. In one case, the request immediately goes to another person. In another, confirmation will be needed, refusal, and rules for working during this waiting period. It can be implemented either way, but not every option suits the team.

A neural network can certainly help: outline both scenarios, identify awkward cases, compare consequences. But it can also simply choose one of the options to make the description look complete. Especially if we ourselves haven't noticed that there's something to discuss here.

And so the developer sees the requirement and considers it agreed upon. The manager looks at the result and asks why the request is now being transferred in exactly this way. The developer replies that here is the document, Claude made it word for word. The author of the document remembers discussing it with Claude, but he will never admit that he simply left the decision to the neural network. As a result, everyone is busy, the process is moving, but in the wrong direction.

Everything is going according to plan, only the plan is sh*t.

At such a moment, it's much more useful for me to know who chose this behavior and why, rather than getting ten more pages of useless details. Where is our agreed-upon decision, where is the assumption, and where is the option that the model simply liked?

In Architectural Decision Records (ADRs), statuses are provided for this, such as "proposed," "accepted," "superseded by new decision." In our task, a simple phrase would suffice:

We propose transferring responsibility after confirmation by the recipient. Not yet agreed with the support manager.

Now, both neural networks and humans will quickly understand that there is no decision yet and no need to invent anything.

In fact, AI can now help organize the agreements themselves: study documentation, collect decisions from discussions, find contradictions, compare descriptions with implementations. To do this, it will have to pass the current context, but in the current reality, it's strange if you haven't automated this at least partially. Here, of course, you need to be careful. If there are three versions of a solution in the chat, and it's not specified anywhere which one is final, the model can meticulously and very convincingly describe the one we have already abandoned. Set statuses for documents and point your RAG only at current sources.

Because in a month, someone else will be sorting this out. Or another agent. And it would be good if a seemingly strange limitation isn't removed during the next "improvement" simply because no one recorded why it was needed.

Why I Sometimes Work Longer with a Neural Network

Now let's go back to the same request and change the conditions slightly. What if the neural network itself asked how to organize the transfer of the request? We missed this case (for some reason), and it helped notice that the request might get stuck between two employees. And that both would think that the other should handle it.

We'll have to sort it out, talk to people, change the initial description. The task will take more time. But there's a chance not to release a feature that will cause requests to get lost. It would be strange to record this time as a loss from using AI.

Some of my tasks with neural networks have also started taking two to three days instead of one. And I'm quite happy with that. During these days, I manage to see things I would have missed before, or find a better solution than the initial one. Of course, a long chat with AI in itself proves nothing. But the comparison "I used to do it in a day, now in three" doesn't yet mean it's gotten worse.

In the METR 2026 survey, participants were specifically asked to assess the impact of AI on the speed and value of completed work. With new tools, the tasks a person even undertakes can change. There are also attempts to check quality without the performer's "self-assessment." In an experiment by David Autor and co-authors with patent lawyers, documents were evaluated by independent experts who were not informed about AI usage. The group with access to a specialized assistant received a higher average quality rating.

With the transfer of our request, there's also something to check: can it still remain without an assignee, is it clear what to do in case of refusal? If this was sorted out before developers, managers, or, God forbid, users discovered the problems, then the extra time was well spent.

However, all this is just one side of the coin. The other side is a very convenient excuse for endless improvements. Since the neural network found one useful question, maybe it will find another? At the same time, you can add assignee selection based on workload, substitution during vacation, automatic distribution. Each idea is quite reasonable. And now we are designing a whole system instead of transferring one request.

It's easy to get lost at this point. You still feel like there's a little bit left to polish, but the work has long gone beyond the initial scope. You planned one feature, but you're already discussing five. And no one, except you and the AI, knows about this expansion yet. At this point, I would ask: are we fixing the identified problem, or have we already started a new task? A new task can also have excellent justifications. Then its scope and deadline will have to be discussed separately.

And if, after another improvement, you can no longer clearly explain how it all works, it's time to sort out what has resulted. The next generation might well add another thing that we accepted but didn't understand.

No Break for Understandable Work

Okay, let's say we've figured out the task and decided what to do. Previously, after that, you could work for some time according to a clear plan: write specifications or code, draw scenarios or mockups. Now, this part is done by the agent, and you deal with the next task. Execution also requires attention and skills, but at least you already have a plan. You don't need to choose a direction anew each time, compare options, and decide what constitutes a good result. When AI takes over the execution phase, the freed-up time is easily filled with a new task where nothing is yet clear.

If there are several such processes, they also end in a mixed order. You've barely managed to get into one, and it's already time to accept another. Between your instruction and the result, the model managed to choose details, connect them, and abandon some things. To evaluate the result, you first need to understand what it has done. Perhaps everything is done well. But you have to reach that conclusion first, and the adjacent chat is already waiting for your decision.

The cost of such acceleration is also visible in the DORA analysis based on responses from Google engineers: time saved in writing code can be spent on reviewing it.

This way, you can spend a normal workday almost without leaving a state of constant crisis. A state where you constantly have to figure things out and make decisions. From the outside, everything looks great: tasks are moving, agents are busy, results are available. But by the evening, you don't want to open another "ready" because you'll have to dive into something again.

A similar contradiction was observed by Berkeley researchers who studied a tech company for eight months. Employees described individual episodes of working with AI as engaging and empowering, but their overall workday as more stressful. You can be happy about what you can now do and simultaneously be more tired from how the day unfolds.

Therefore, I wouldn't rush to load a person with a new task immediately after the agent sends the result for the previous one. It still needs to be understood. And after several such immersions, sometimes you just want to work according to a clear plan, without making new decisions at every step. Three more chats can wait, even if there are plenty of tokens available.

The Boss Can Also Type

It's perfectly normal to expect benefits from AI. If the team completes understandable tasks faster, you can shorten deadlines or tackle things you couldn't get to before. The question is whether the work has truly accelerated and what was paid for it.

Did we eliminate repetitive actions? Find a simpler solution? Or did we just start sending the result for review earlier, and let them figure it out? In a report, all three options can look like increased efficiency. For the team, the consequences will be different.

If, after all checks and revisions, the team has truly gained time, it can be spent on subsequent tasks or on more thorough elaboration of current ones. But this needs to be agreed upon with the employee. Otherwise, it might turn out that the manager is already counting on new results, while the employee with the neural network is still perfecting the previous one. Everyone seems to be increasing efficiency, but there's no agreement on what exactly needs to be done.

With neural networks, we really want to skip this conversation. The tool is smart, the employee isn't stupid either, they'll figure it out somehow. Only later do we find out that they figured out something completely different from what we expected.

But the manager also has their own way of adding work to the team. Ask the model to create a strategy, a plan for changes, or a detailed product description, and then send it all to subordinates. It looks solid. Tasks are outlined. All that's left is to execute.

Figuring out where the priorities came from, why these specific constraints exist, and whether the promised outcome is even achievable will now be up to the executors. It's quite likely they will discover the same uncoordinated decisions we started the article with. Only asking questions to the boss is sometimes harder than to a colleague.

Handing over a draft for discussion or asking the team to test a hypothesis is normal work. If it's truly a draft and a hypothesis test. When deadlines are already being calculated based on such material, it becomes important what has been accepted in it and what the neural network added for persuasiveness.

So, the requirement to understand the AI's output also applies to ourselves. Before sending a task down, it's good to be able to explain why we decided to do it this way. A job title doesn't make the model's output more reliable.

And I would still evaluate team efficiency considering what happened after the work was handed over. Where clarifications arose, how many revisions were needed, who actually finished the task. Otherwise, you can very successfully accelerate reporting and not even notice how much hassle remained outside of it.

Did You Do It Yourself?

Assessing people becomes even more interesting. A large, neat document no longer provides much basis for assuming that the author has deeply understood the topic. A short remark to it, however, can save the team several days, even though it looks much less impressive.

We live in interesting times when a well-written text can itself raise suspicion. In a Bloomberg report, the authors talk about how they are changing their language to avoid looking like ChatGPT users.

However, literature may have its own authorship agreements. What concerns us here is a regular work team where using AI is allowed. If initially an employee is expected to speed up with neural networks, and then upon seeing the result, they grimace: "Clearly, GPT wrote this for you," they are being offered a rather silly game. You need to get the benefit from the tool, but its participation should somehow be hidden.

And the concerns about this attitude are not entirely unfounded. In a study published in PNAS in 2025, participants in one of the online experiments evaluated an employee based on a brief description. If AI assistance was mentioned, they were, on average, considered less competent and diligent than with similar assistance from other sources. In hiring scenarios, the attitude also depended on the task and the evaluator's own experience with neural networks.

You can also spend a lot of time trying to avoid these suspicions. Tidying up the text, removing formal phrases, expressing your thoughts more precisely – this is understandable work. But if you've been rearranging words for several rounds so that no one guesses GPT's involvement, the question arises: are you even doing the right thing? After which correction will your internal or actual reviewer finally calm down?

For me, it's much more useful to discuss the result itself with the person. Why did they choose this solution? What is the important conclusion based on? What will happen in a controversial case? From the answers, you can learn more about their contribution than from counting independently typed characters.

In the description of Canva's technical interviews published in 2025, this is clearly illustrated. The company included AI in interviews and made the tasks more complex: candidates need to deal with ambiguous conditions, make technical decisions, and fix generated code. Using the tool is allowed, but understanding the result is still necessary.

In your team, you will also have to maintain this consistency somehow. It's very easy to consider your own work with AI a deserved victory, and someone else's – a successful response from a neural network. Especially if you don't know how the person arrived at it. I think evaluating the suitability of the result and the author's ability to explain it is more honest.

Of course, we expect different things from a junior specialist and an experienced manager. If a person cannot yet check what they have been instructed to generate, they will need help or training. This skill will not appear on its own from another call to use AI more actively.

Instead of a Conclusion

I like that with neural networks, I can delve deeper into a task and see solutions that I would have missed before. And I want to see this benefit in my work: more thoroughly thought-out solutions, clear agreements, less refactoring and rework. Sometimes, for this, the author will have to spend more time on the task. But then the next person will be able to continue from where you have actually reached.

And if, after your cheerful "done," they have to re-figure out what needed to be done at all, then you and the neural network have not prepared anything yet.
