AI is like a scalpel that, for $20 a month, has ended up in everyone's hands. In the hands of a surgeon, it allows us to do what was previously impossible. In the hands of an untrained person, it also leaves a mark – but the result can be unpredictable.

The AI scalpel has a feature that a real one doesn't: it doesn't just follow the hand and obey it. It suggests where to cut and does so confidently and convincingly, even when it's wrong. Therefore, an erroneous suggestion is easily mistaken for an unquestionable professional judgment.

For a professional, AI provides significant acceleration in work: it allows for faster problem exploration, hypothesis testing, code writing, and data analysis. But in the hands of an untrained person, the same tool helps create convincing materials that no one has properly checked.

A report with erroneous conclusions becomes more beautiful and voluminous – it's immediately clear what "great work" has been done. A bug in the code gets into production faster, and a response to a client sounds convincing, even if the conditions are fabricated.

In 2023, a New York court fined lawyers in the Mata v. Avianca case $5,000: they submitted documents citing non-existent court decisions generated by one of many AI models.

In 2024, Air Canada lost a dispute with a client: the airline's chatbot provided him with incorrect information about discount conditions. The company tried to shift responsibility to the chatbot, but the court ruled that the owner of the website is responsible for the answers on their own site. It doesn't matter if the answer was generated by AI or written by a human – the company will have to answer for it.

Yes, people also make mistakes – and sometimes very confidently. But AI confidently provides both correct answers and its own hallucinations. You can't tell from the tone of the response where the error is, and with mass AI usage, such errors are especially hard to notice before they reach the stakeholder's desk or go into production.

We are also poor at assessing our own benefits from using AI. In the METR 2025 study, 16 experienced developers worked on familiar projects with available AI tools and spent 19% more time than without them. But according to their own feelings, they accelerated by about 20%. It's worth noting that this result applies to specific developers with specific skills, but it illustrates well how the feeling of productivity can diverge from reality.

Those who are older remember the appearance of Wikipedia. For some, it became a starting point for a school report or term paper: read, understand, find sources, and write your own text. Others simply pressed two magic key combinations – Ctrl+C and Ctrl+V, and along with the text, punctuation marks in complex words ended up in the report.

With AI, we are currently at the Ctrl+C and Ctrl+V stage. We're just not copying a finished school report, but an answer that looks like it was written specifically for our task. Therefore, we want to double-check it even less.

Mass corporate enthusiasm for AI (I can hardly call it implementation, at least in Russia) does not inherently provide a business advantage. It amplifies what already exists in companies: professionalism or incompetence, clear and transparent processes or chaos. If people know how to ask questions, verify facts, critically evaluate projects, and take responsibility for the outcome, AI provides a qualitative leap. If not, it helps to make bad decisions faster and scale them across the entire business. A qualitative leap requires professionals, not more advice on how to write a better prompt so that AI hallucinates less and uses fewer tokens.

The main question is not how many employees use AI and how often. The main question is: "Who can understand when AI is wrong, and who is responsible for the result?" It seems the answer is as simple as possible – "a good specialist." But this sounds too abstract, and we want more specifics.

For myself, I have formulated several criteria. In many ways, they are similar to what we learned while working with Wikipedia:

  1. Checks the answer against primary sources: data, documents, code, contracts, legal norms, not against another model's answer.

  2. Knows their business so well that they understand in advance where AI is most likely to err and will check exactly that first.

  3. Can explain over a glass of tea at the bar why the AI's results for a specific query can be trusted or not, and explain why.

  4. Is personally responsible for the result, not hiding behind "the AI said so.".

  5. Reads quickly and separates the essence from the fluff. No, seriously – to digest such a volume of content from AI, it's crucial to read and understand the meaning quickly (I wonder if the demand for speed-reading courses has increased – if you have data, please share).

These are the kinds of specialists companies should be looking for – their work will be accelerated by AI, and their experience will help teams not to accept a beautiful and voluminous answer as correct.

However, responsibility cannot lie solely with the employee using AI. When technology becomes part of a product or business process, the owner of that process is also responsible for the outcome. It is necessary to determine in advance who controls the quality of the model's work, who makes decisions in case of errors, and who is responsible for the consequences. Otherwise, we will reach a point where AI is already making decisions, and no one is responsible for it.

The safety of operations relies not only on the talent of an individual surgeon but on the system around them. Years of residency before you are trusted to operate independently. A checklist before each surgery – the WHO introduced it in 2008, and it significantly reduced errors, although it consists of banal questions like "is this the right patient?" Regular reviews of complications where errors are discussed so they don't repeat, not to find a scapegoat. All of this needs to be applied to working with AI: a path from simple tasks to complex ones, a short list of mandatory checks before delivering results, and open team discussions about instances where an AI error reached the client or stakeholder.

The manager's task is to teach the entire team to use the scalpel. Start with theory: where AI can be useful, how and where it can err, and what is important to check. Then, simple operations under the supervision of more experienced colleagues. And only when an employee learns to spot errors and take responsibility for the outcome can they be released into the wild.

Yes, this scheme has a bottleneck – the time of experts. If every result is checked by a senior, the savings go into reviews. But this should be treated as an investment in a junior and planned as part of the work, not considered an additional burden or "career growth." We are already at a stage where a Product Senior with vast experience and business knowledge can be an AI junior.

But if the check relies solely on senior time, it doesn't scale. Part of the control should be built into the process itself: tests before review, automatic comparison of numbers with source data, mandatory links to primary sources.

It's important to understand that the number of AI users in companies, generated texts, processes with AI, or closed tickets shows activity, not real benefit. Real results are indicated by other metrics:

  1. Errors: how many errors are found during the checking stage and how many are discovered after the result is delivered.

  2. Reworks: how many tickets had to be reopened and how much time was spent on corrections.

  3. Cost of a correct result: how much the company spent to complete the task, including the cost of AI, checking, rework, and expert time.

  4. Time to a finished result: not to the first draft, but to the completed work, including all corrections.

  5. Customer satisfaction: if we are talking about automating customer interactions.

The list is not exhaustive, and these metrics alone say little without comparison to pre-AI era work. If a task previously took 2 hours, and with AI it takes only an hour to complete plus another hour and a half for checking and corrections, then there has been no acceleration, even though the first result appeared in a couple of minutes. Ultimately, what matters is what the business will receive. Otherwise, it's just a hobby or a pastime.

If the frequency of AI use and the number of processes involving it are growing, while quality, speed, and economics are not improving, then the business is scaling the use of the technology, but not its benefits.

The Agile Manifesto appeared when followers of flexible approaches gathered and formulated common principles of work – they were looking for an alternative to heavy and cumbersome development processes. With AI, I think we are approaching a similar moment: the tool is already everywhere, enough experience, including negative, has been accumulated, but common professional principles and approaches are still lacking.

For example, we could start with these principles:

  1. Responsibility is more important than authorship. It doesn't matter who created the result: a person or a model. The person or team that owns the process is responsible for it, and responsibility is determined before the result is obtained.

  2. Verification is more important than speed. A result that no one has verified is not final, no matter how good and colorful it looks. The depth of verification is determined by the risk to the product: a product/AI senior reviews not everything, but where an error is too costly.

  3. Primary source is more important than plausibility. Any facts, figures, and conclusions from AI must be verified against original data, documents, and other reliable sources.

  4. Quality of decisions is more important than the quantity and frequency of AI use. We should measure what has improved, not how many times the model was run.

This list does not claim to be the ultimate truth – it is only an invitation to open discussion with professionals who implement and use models in real work.

The scalpel is already in everyone's hands. It's impossible to take it back, and generally, it shouldn't be. But mass availability doesn't make a person a professional, nor mass AI usage make a company more effective.

Competitive advantage will be gained not by those who implement AI faster or use it more often in all processes, but by those who learn to control errors, obtain quality results, and scale real economic value, not just optimize prompts and skills.

The rest will have to learn from their own mistakes at the expense of shareholder money and customer happiness.
