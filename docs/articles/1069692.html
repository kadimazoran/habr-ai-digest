Triage of Vulnerabilities in AI Platforms: Why Calendar SLA Broke and What to Use Instead

On June 10, 2026, CISA released directive BOD 26-04, replacing BOD 19-02 and BOD 22-01. The new directive shifts from fixed deadlines for vulnerabilities in the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) catalog to a risk-based approach for prioritizing security updates. CISA now considers asset public accessibility, KEV status, exploitability automation, and attack impact. The new model, based on SSVC, mandates a 3-day fix for the most urgent cases and allows for scheduled updates for the least urgent. However, AI platforms present a challenge due to the diverse nature of "fixing" vulnerabilities across different layers (web API, ML dependencies, GPU runtime, inference engine, model weights). A uniform CVSS score does not equate to uniform priority, timeline, or remediation strategy. This article analyzes eight public CVEs from a typical AI stack using a decision tree, examining asset accessibility, exploitation evidence, exploit automation, technical impact, and actual remediation paths. It also demonstrates how to derive decision thresholds from FIRST's published data. The content is intended for AppSec and DevSecOps engineers managing platform vulnerability backlogs, team leads whose releases depend on security tickets, and CISOs. A dedicated section addresses FSTEC requirements for Russian CISOs. The approach is also applicable to standard infrastructure without GPUs and models, with adjustments to the asset inventory but not the prioritization logic.

On June 10, 2026, CISA released directive BOD 26-04 Prioritizing Security Updates Based on Risk. The new document supersedes BOD 19-02 and BOD 22-01, a directive that since 2021 had set uniform calendar deadlines for federal agencies in the US to fix vulnerabilities from the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) catalog. Instead of a flat deadline, the new model proposes determining the deadline based on the risk to a specific asset. For prioritization, CISA considers four circumstances: is the asset publicly accessible, is the vulnerability in KEV, can its exploitation be automated, and what technical effect does the attack lead to. The deadline table, as CISA itself indicates, is based on SSVC. In the most urgent cases, a vulnerability must be fixed within three days, and initial forensic triage must be performed; in the least urgent cases, the fix can be implemented during the next scheduled system update.

The most inconvenient object for this approach is an AI platform. In its various layers, the word "fix" implies fundamentally different actions. In a web API, it might be a standard patch; in an ML dependency chain, a major framework update; in a GPU runtime, a driver or firmware update requiring downtime; in an inference engine, waiting for a decision from the maintainer or replacing the component. For model weights, a patch in the conventional sense might not exist at all. Therefore, the same CVSS score does not imply the same priority, deadline, or remediation method. In this article, eight public CVEs from a typical AI stack are put through a decision tree: for each entry, we will analyze asset accessibility, exploitation indicators, attack automation, technical impact, and the actual path to remediation. We will also show how to derive decision thresholds from published FIRST data. The material is intended for AppSec and DevSecOps engineers who manage the platform's vulnerability backlog, and for team leads whose releases depend on open security tickets. CISOs will find the section on FSTEC requirements useful. How a Flat Deadline Became an Industry Benchmark

BOD 22-01 was addressed to US federal civilian agencies, but its logic extended beyond the public sector. The due date field in the KEV catalog is displayed by vulnerability scanners and considered when setting internal SLAs. Therefore, the cancellation of the flat calendar approach is important not only for organizations subject to CISA directives: it changes one of the most prominent external benchmarks for vulnerability management.

BOD 26-04 does not propose slower remediation of known exploited vulnerabilities. The principle itself changes: instead of a general deadline for everyone, the team assesses the specific situation—where the vulnerable component operates, whether it is accessible to the attacker, how easy it is to automate the attack, and what its consequences will be. Based on this context, a realistic fix deadline is chosen.

In Russia, a risk-oriented approach to vulnerability remediation is also enshrined in FSTEC documents: the methodological document of June 30, 2025, and order No. 117, which sets remediation deadlines. In this article, these requirements are considered as a separate regulatory framework, not a direct copy of the CISA or SSVC model.

Where the Calendar Came From

A calendar SLA is built on a simple scheme: a vulnerability has a criticality rating, and based on that, a fix deadline can be assigned. For example, critical CVEs must be closed within a few days, high ones within a week, and others within the regular update cycle.

This scheme was convenient for a long time. It allowed for quickly sorting a large flow of vulnerabilities by deadline, formalizing requirements for contractors, and reporting to auditors. But today, it has two weak points: a CVSS assessment is not available for every new CVE, and by itself, it describes the severity of the vulnerability, not the risk to a specific system.

NVD Can No Longer Assess All CVEs in Time

In April 2026, NIST changed the operating procedure of the National Vulnerability Database (NVD). The reason is the growing volume of CVEs and the accumulated backlog of unparsed records. According to NIST, the number of incoming CVEs increased by 263% from 2020 to 2025, and the first quarter of 2026 was almost a third higher than the same period the previous year. NIST reports on changes in NVD operations.

Since April 15, 2026, NVD primarily enriches three groups of records:

  • vulnerabilities from the CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) catalog;

  • vulnerabilities in software used by US federal agencies;

  • vulnerabilities in critical software from the Executive Order 14028 list.

For other CVEs, NVD still publishes a record but may not add information typically needed for automatic prioritization: a list of affected products, mapping to CPE, its own CVSS assessment, and other analytical processing results. Such records receive the status Lowest Priority — not scheduled for immediate enrichment. The old backlog—records published before March 1, 2026—has been moved to the status Not Scheduled. NIST also indicates that it will generally no longer add its own severity assessment if a CNA has already published one. [nist]

For a platform team, this changes the practice of working with sources. A new CVE may appear in NVD without a NIST CVSS, and an old backlog record may remain un-enriched for a long time. Below, we will encounter two vulnerabilities in the AI stack for which there is no NVD assessment. One of them directly indicates the status Not Scheduled in its card: NVD does not plan to process it in the near future.

The status in NVD shows how a record is queued for processing, not how dangerous the vulnerability is for a specific infrastructure. Therefore, the team has to independently find out which versions are affected, whether the component is used in its systems, whether it is accessible from the outside, and whether there are compensating measures.

CVSS Measures Severity, Not Risk

The second problem is related to CVSS itself. The FIRST CVSS 4.0 User Guide explicitly states that the base CVSS score measures severity, i.e., the technical severity of a vulnerability, and cannot be used as the sole indicator of risk. The base score describes the internal properties of the defect; it does not consider whether a working exploit exists, whether the vulnerability is attacked in practice, or in what environment the vulnerable product operates. CVSS v4.0 User Guide.

Therefore, in CVSS 4.0, a Threat metric group has been introduced. It allows adjusting the base score with threat intelligence—information about vulnerability exploitation and the availability of technical means for attack. FIRST separately notes that this should reduce the problem of inflated base scores.

FIRST expresses a similar idea in the EPSS FAQ: CVSS assesses severity, but a high score only weakly helps predict actual exploitation. For prioritization tasks, it is not enough to know how severe a theoretical attack scenario appears. It is necessary to understand how likely exploitation is and what consequences it will cause in this particular environment. EPSS FAQ.

In the NVD interface, next to the scoring block, there is a hint: CVSS does not measure risk. The score should be considered for prioritization, but a fix deadline cannot be assigned based on it alone.

Scores Differ Even Among Sources

Even when a CVSS score is available, it is not always the same across different assessors. In the preprint by researchers Zhang, Massacci, and Zhang dated July 6, 2026, discrepancies between NVD and public CNA assessments are called widespread, though not uniform. Most often, the authors note discrepancies in the Attack Complexity, User Interaction, and impact metrics.

The authors tested how this affects prioritization models. A model trained on data from one source may lose up to 40% accuracy when transferred to data from another. They also introduced the term self-divergence: the same CNA may assess CVEs with the same description and the same CWE differently.

Different assessments do not necessarily mean an error: the assessor might have considered information not present in the public description or interpreted the exploitation conditions differently. The authors note that in some cases, the discrepancy is justified and that the situation has been gradually improving since 2025. However, the work is published as a preprint and has not yet undergone peer review.

Why the Calendar Still Lives On

Despite the limitations of CVSS, calendar update cycles remain part of normal engineering practice. An engineer from the Vulnerability Management team at Ozon described that the full cycle for installing Windows updates takes 30 calendar days, and for Linux—90 days. These deadlines are tied to the rhythm of update vendors, testing, and releasing changes into the production environment.

This model works well for scheduled updates. If a vulnerability is difficult to exploit, the team can avoid an emergency patch and include the fix in the next regular cycle. Ozon's commentary on the material states the same principle: for complex exploitation, a CVE does not require an emergency patch and can wait for a scheduled update.

However, in platforms with heterogeneous stacks, not every fix fits into the usual cycle. A web service patch can be rolled out quickly. Updating an ML framework sometimes requires checking compatibility and retraining or validating the model. Replacing a GPU driver version may require downtime and workload migration. For a vulnerability in an unsupported inference engine or a model artifact, a ready-made patch may not exist.

Meanwhile, mature teams no longer rely solely on CVSS. At Ozon, prioritization considers the presence of CVE in KEV, EPSS score, EOL status of software, asset accessibility, and attack surface size. Open risk tickets can block releases, and escalations, depending on the situation, reach the CTO.

Where the Calendar Becomes Mandatory

A calendar deadline is still needed where a uniform and verifiable rule is required: in contractual SLAs, audit reports, regulatory requirements, and vulnerability management programs that are still being formed.

In the Russian context, deadlines are legally fixed. FSTEC Order No. 117 of April 11, 2025 came into force on March 1, 2026. Clause 38 establishes the maximum remediation deadlines: for critical vulnerabilities—no more than 24 hours, for high ones—no more than seven calendar days. We will return to what exactly the regulator considers remediation and how these deadlines relate to the specifics of the AI stack in the section on FSTEC.

This leads to a practical contradiction. Vulnerability deadlines are still needed: SLAs, audits, and regulators require them. But automatic deadline calculation based on a single score is becoming increasingly unreliable: the score may be missing, it may differ between sources, and it may not reflect the exploitation conditions of a specific asset.

The next step is to replace the single-channel "CVSS → deadline" scheme with a decision tree that considers indicators of real threats and the method of vulnerability remediation.

One Score for Five Different Layers

An AI platform does not fit well into a calendar SLA. It consists of components for which "fixing a vulnerability" means different things: updating a library, switching to a new framework version, rebooting GPU nodes, replacing an unsupported component, or revoking a model file. The same CVSS score in these cases leads to different actions, deadlines, and limitations.

Web API and Wrapper

Everything looks familiar here: there is a component version, there is a fix, a scanner finds a vulnerability, and the update can be included in the release plan. But even in this layer, CVSS sometimes gives very different answers.

CVE-2024-8309 in LangChain is an SQL injection via GraphCypherQAChain. NVD provides two assessments for it: 9.8 Critical from NVD and 4.9 Medium from huntr and Protect AI as CNAs. The difference is 4.9 points.

Assessors disagree on all three metric groups. NVD believes the attack is network-based, requires no complex conditions, and leads to high impacts on confidentiality, integrity, and availability. CNA describes a local attack with high complexity and low impact. Even the CWEs are different: CWE-74 for NVD and CWE-89 for CNA. In one SLA scheme, such a CVE would fall into the "fix within 30 days" bucket, and in another, "close within 90 days.".

For CVE-2024-5184 in EmailGPT, there are three assessments: 9.1 Critical from NVD, 6.5 Medium from Synopsys using CVSS 3.1, and 8.5 High from Synopsys using CVSS 4.0. The vulnerability itself hasn't changed, but the transition to a new version of the scale moves it to a different SLA category. In three records with dual assessments within the same CVSS version, the spread ranged from 1.7 to 4.9 points.

ML Dependencies: A Patch as a Major Upgrade

CVE-2025-32434 in PyTorch received a 9.3 Critical score on CVSS 4.0 from GitHub as a CNA. The vulnerability allows remote code execution via torch.load even with weights_only=True—a parameter teams enabled specifically for protection.

Meanwhile, NVD has not yet added a CVSS 4.0 assessment for the record: the card states NVD assessment not yet provided. For CVSS 3.1, there is an NVD assessment—9.8 Critical. As a result, one CVE has 9.8 from NVD using the old scale version and 9.3 from CNA using the new one side-by-side.

The main problem here is not the score discrepancy but the remediation method. PyTorch versions 2.5.1 and below are vulnerable; the fix is in 2.6.0. Version 2.5.2 is not in the PyPI release feed. PyTorch's SECURITY.md explicitly states: security fixes are only applied to the current release and are not backported to older versions.

Therefore, closing CVE-2025-32434 means updating PyTorch to a new minor branch. For many ML systems, this is no longer a simple dependency update but a project: compatibility of packages, containers, CUDA stack, training, and inference pipelines must be checked, and sometimes, the reproducibility of model results must be verified.

The deadline, counted from the CVE publication, can also be misleading. PyTorch 2.5.1 was released on October 29, 2024, version 2.6.0 on January 29, 2025, and the CVE was published on April 18, 2025. The fix existed for 79 days before the record was published. A team that regularly updated PyTorch might have closed the issue even before the CVE appeared. A team with a rigidly fixed version received a requirement to perform a major upgrade on a tight schedule.

The scale of this dependency is well illustrated by Patrick Smyth's report from Chainguard at PyTorch Conference 2024. In the official PyTorch image, he counted one Critical, five High, 40 Medium, and over 50 Low vulnerabilities. According to his estimate, about 2% of CVEs in a typical ML deployment arise in the team's own code, while the rest come from upstream dependencies and base images.

The release pace makes fixed versions a separate risk factor. vLLM had 24 releases across 14 minor branches in 204 days: a new minor version appeared approximately every two weeks. The project has no LTS branches, and patches are only released for the current minor line. PyTorch had eight minor branches and 11 releases (including patch releases) over twenty and a half months. If you fix the inference engine version for a quarter, by the end of the period, you could be six or seven minor releases behind—where security fixes are usually located.

GPU Runtime and Firmware

CVE-2025-23266, also known as NVIDIAScape, has a 9.0 Critical score on CVSS 3.1 from NVIDIA as a CNA. The vulnerability in the NVIDIA Container Toolkit allows escaping the container to the host. An OCI hook launches nvidia-ctk with root privileges and inherits environment variables from the container image, including LD_PRELOAD. Exploitation requires only three lines in a Dockerfile. Description of the NVIDIAScape exploit.

The result is root access on the host and potential access to models, data, and workloads of neighboring tenants. For this vulnerability, public host accessibility is not the main criterion. Wiz specifically emphasizes: the attacked node does not need to be accessible from the internet. It is sufficient for the attacker to be able to run a container on shared GPU infrastructure. NVIDIAScape analysis by Wiz.

CVE-2025-23266 has no NVD assessment: the card is marked with the status Not Scheduled. Fixes are available—NVIDIA Container Toolkit 1.17.8 and newer, GPU Operator 25.3.1 and newer. However, updating the GPU Operator on a running cluster usually requires a separate operation: coordinating a window, migrating or stopping workloads, checking driver compatibility, and restarting nodes. As a temporary measure, NVIDIA suggests disabling the CUDA compatibility library hook by setting the parameter disable-cuda-compat-lib-hook = true in /etc/nvidia-container-toolkit/config.toml.

Further down the stack, the nature of the fix itself changes. CVE-2023-4969, LeftoverLocals, allows one GPU kernel to read data from local memory remaining after another kernel has executed, including data belonging to another user or application. In NVD, the vulnerability is rated 6.5 Medium.

In a calendar model, it might fall into the "close within 90 days" task category. For a multi-tenant AI platform, this is a breach of isolation between tenants. The fix requires updating the accelerator firmware and driver, which is an operation at the hardware level. A container image scanner will not see it and will not close it. Affected devices include AMD Instinct MI300X, MI250, MI210, Radeon Pro W7600, and other GPUs.

Inference Engine Without a Patch

CVE-2025-30165 in vLLM received an 8.0 score on CVSS 3.1 and is related to CWE-502—insecure deserialization. The NVD card states that fixing it would require too extensive code refactoring, so the maintainers do not plan to release a patch. Instead, they advise deploying vLLM only in an isolated, controlled network.

The team will not be able to close such a vulnerability with a standard update. If the internal SLA requires fixing a High vulnerability within 60 days, the requirement must be met with compensating measures: restrict network access to the service, isolate it from untrusted systems, prohibit processing of untrusted input data, or replace vLLM with another component.

The vLLM maintainers indicate that the old execution path has been replaced by V1, which became the default mode starting from version 0.8.0. However, the project's release notes state that this is only true for supported scenarios. For models and functions marked SupportsV0Only, the old path may still be used. Therefore, the operator needs to check which path is actually working in their configuration.

CVE-2023-48022 in Ray is known from the ShadowRay campaign. NVD assigned it 9.8 Critical and tagged the record as disputed. The vendor's position is that Ray is intended for a strictly controlled network and should not operate in an open environment. Token authentication became an option starting from version 2.52.0.

Here, the discrepancy is not in the scores but in the understanding of the acceptable exploitation model. In the registry, the vulnerability appears as a Critical CVE in a working component and falls under a strict SLA. The vendor believes that the risk is mitigated by proper network isolation: Ray is initially designed to work only in a controlled environment. Among the eight entries in this analysis, only for this CVE does CISA SSVC indicate automatable = yes—the attack can be fully automated.

In this layer, the boundary of the CVE itself is also important. Oligo described the ShadowMQ pattern: an insecure combination of ZeroMQ recv_pyobj() and pickle made its way into several projects through reuse of the reference implementation. The pattern was found in inference servers from Meta, NVIDIA, Microsoft, vLLM, SGLang, and Modular. In SGLang, the vulnerable file begins with the comment Adapted from vLLM. No fix or CVE in public sources

If a component has no CVE, a scanner will usually not notice it, and there will be no entry for it in the SLA table. This is how a process that relies solely on vulnerability registries works.

A similar situation arises with a chain of three NVIDIA Triton vulnerabilities: CVE-2025-23319, CVE-2025-23320, and CVE-2025-23334. Together, they allow an unauthenticated remote attacker to take over the server. Description of the chain in NVIDIA Triton.

The first step of this chain, by itself, appears as a relatively weak information leak: it is needed to learn the unique name of an internal resource and proceed to the next stage of the attack. Dark Reading describes this dependency between steps.

For CVE-2025-23319, NVIDIA PSIRT assigned 8.1 High and indicated CWE-805, while NVD assigned 9.8 Critical and CWE-787. The vectors differ only in Attack Complexity. 81 days passed between the internal report and the patch publication; details of the coordinated disclosure are documented.

Model Weights Outside of CVE

A model file may not have a version number, vendor, or CVE. However, loading it can lead to code execution. PyTorch's SECURITY.md states this directly: a PyTorch model should be considered a program, and running an untrusted model is equivalent to running untrusted code.

It also includes a section Issues That Are Not Security Vulnerabilities. This includes:

  • crashes and buffer overflows with incorrect arguments;

  • denial of service through resource consumption;

  • local cache poisoning.

For the project, these are regular bugs, not CVEs. Consequently, a vulnerability management process that only receives input from CVE feeds will not see this class of risks. PyTorch documentation explicitly states that recommendations from the Using PyTorch Securely section do not apply to PyTorch's own vulnerabilities.

The problem is not theoretical. In February 2024, JFrog discovered about a hundred models uploaded by users to Hugging Face containing malicious payloads. Most often, it was a pickle with the __reduce__ method, which executed code upon loading; one of the models opened a reverse shell to a hardcoded address. Analysis of malicious models by JFrog.

In February 2025, ReversingLabs described the nullifAI technique. In it, a deliberately corrupted pickle format and a 7z archive instead of ZIP bypassed Picklescan checks, although a more tolerant Python deserializer still processed the file. Hugging Face removed the detected malicious models in less than a day, but this is a reaction after publication and artifact upload, not a guarantee that a dangerous file will not enter the environment. Analysis of nullifAI by ReversingLabs.

Positive Technologies also analyzed ML model scanners and methods for bypassing their checks. Detection tools help filter out some dangerous files, but the decision to trust a specific model remains with the team. Scanning ML models and bypassing checks.

Above this layer is another class of risks without version numbers or CVEs: prompt injection. OWASP in LLM01:2025 states that due to the stochastic nature of models, there is currently no guaranteed way to completely prevent such attacks. RAG and model fine-tuning only partially reduce the risk.

As of August 11, 2026, MITRE ATLAS lists 16 tactics, 178 techniques, and 37 countermeasures. These countermeasures are organizational and architectural controls: tool isolation, privilege restrictions, input validation, output filtering, logging, and monitoring.

Decision Tree Questions and Threshold Table

How SSVC Works

In SSVC, a vulnerability does not receive a final priority as a single number. Instead, the team sequentially answers several questions, and based on their combination, chooses an action.

The CISA tree was published in 2020 and is built around five indicators: is there exploitation data, what is the technical impact of the attack, can it be automated, how widely is the component used in critical processes, and how will the incident affect people or socially important functions.

The tree does not assign a deadline at the output. It indicates the level of attention and reaction required for a vulnerability within the organization.

A calendar SLA immediately assigns a deadline. SSVC first breaks down the situation by several indicators and then determines the required level of response. The deadline becomes a consequence of this decision.

Over the past year, collecting data for such a tree has become easier. Since June 17, 2026, NVD has been publishing SSVC metadata in API version 2.0.3, using CISA-ADP data. The update covers about 95% of records. NIST calls this the Computed SSVC Score: the record shows the indicators used to determine its priority. NVD.

This means that a separate enrichment pipeline is not needed for the transition to a tree. In most cases, it is sufficient to read the SSVC fields from the NVD API response along with CVSS and information about affected versions.

The definitions of decision points and the source files for the trees are available in the CERT/CC repository. The machine-readable version of the CISA tree is in the file CISA-Coordinator.json.

Which Tree Does an Operator Need

SSVC has several trees because the supplier, deployer, and incident coordinator answer different questions.

Deployer

What to do with a vulnerability in a specific operating environment

A platform team acts as a deployer: it decides what to do with a vulnerability in its own infrastructure. To do this, it needs to know where the component runs, who can access it, whether the service is isolated, whether downtime is acceptable, and how long the update will take.

The CISA tree is primarily designed for a coordinator. It lacks some operational context, but the data for it is published in open sources through Vulnrichment. It can be used as a basis and supplemented with indicators relevant to a specific platform. SSVC: Roles and Decision Trees.

Five Questions for the Platform

Before the tree, it is necessary to check if the finding has a CVE. If there is no record, it will not appear in NVD, KEV, or the automatic SLA backlog. This is often the case with model files, insecure templates, borrowed code, and other AI risks.

The first four questions are taken from BOD 26-04: is the CVE in KEV, is the asset publicly accessible, can exploitation be automated, and what is the technical impact of the attack. The values for automatable and technicalImpact are already available in the NVD API data alongside CVSS and CVE descriptions.

SSVC considers an attack automatable if all its stages—reconnaissance, preparation, delivery, and exploitation—can be reliably automated. Technical impact can be partial or complete. In the first case, the attacker gains limited access or discloses some data; in the second, they gain full control of the component or full access to data. SSVC Definitions for the CISA Model

The fifth question is added for AI platforms: is there an applicable fix. The original CISA tree does not have this axis.

In the table above, a patch is missing for some cases. In others, it requires a major framework update, a GPU driver update, or accelerator firmware. For model weights, there is no patch at all. If this is not considered, the tree might require fixing a CVE for which the team has no ready remediation method.

This principle is also familiar in classic AppSec. The VK Security Gate description states that the criticality calculator considers not only CVSS but also the probability of exploitation in the next 30 days, the possibility of automating the attack, and the invocation of dependency methods in the application code.

The possibility of automating an attack corresponds to the automatable axis. Checking method calls helps establish reachability: a library may be present in the container image, but the application does not call the vulnerable code. This indicator will be needed later when the CVE assessment moves to evaluating a specific deployment.

This tree has six outcomes instead of CISA's four. Two are added for situations that do not fit the standard scheme:

  • mitigation under signature—when the tree requires fixing the vulnerability, but there is no patch, and the team must choose compensating measures, documenting the decision made;

  • "CVE out of queue"—for findings without a number, including poisoned model weights, insecure serialization formats, and vulnerable code patterns for which there is no database entry.

The question about an applicable fix arises before each outcome that requires remediation. Without it, the tree would repeat the problem of the calendar SLA: assigning a deadline for an action that cannot be performed. To work with the tree, several more concepts need clarification.

What constitutes a trusted perimeter. This is a network to which neither the platform user nor a neighboring tenant has access. For multi-tenant hardware, the answer to the fourth question is always "yes": the neighbor on the accelerator is the external party. Otherwise, the axis will reproduce the situation that Wiz warns about in the NVIDIAScape analysis. This rule applies to vulnerabilities exploited through adjacency on the accelerator, not to everything running on it.

What constitutes an applicable patch. The fix exists and can be installed by your team within the regular release cycle. Accelerator firmware in a third-party cloud and a major upgrade with breaking changes differ by this definition: the former is unavailable to the team, the latter is available, although it requires significant effort. This definition also separates areas of responsibility. The provider updates the firmware, hypervisor, and host runtime; the team updates the image and dependencies. The fifth axis is assessed differently on different sides of this boundary, so it must be explicitly fixed in the contract with the provider—along with responsibility for compensating measures until a patch is available.

Whose assessment to use in case of discrepancy. The values for automatable and technicalImpact are taken from Vulnrichment—the same CISA program on whose fields the directive is based. This eliminates the situation where NVD and CNA assess the technical impact of the same record differently.

The temptation to reduce two metrics to a single composite score arises constantly. FIRST highlights such an approach in its list of incorrect ways to use EPSS: "Applying incorrect statistics, also known as 'score laundering.' Do not multiply the EPSS score by an ordinal score, such as CVSS, and do not assume the result provides a combined risk assessment."

In the EPSS FAQ, the same idea is formulated even more concisely: "Multiplying EPSS by a CVSS score does not calculate the product of probability and severity and never yields a meaningful result: you get a number that is <...> meaningless." The metrics have different types of scales: CVSS is an ordinal score obtained by expert vector comparison, while EPSS is a probability.

Where Thresholds Come From

EPSS fundamentally does not provide ready-made levels. In the FAQ, the authors explicitly state that EPSS "does not define score ranges with labels like 'critical,' 'high,' or 'medium' <...> and deliberately leaves this decision to the specialist." The popular 10% threshold in the same document is accompanied by a caveat: it "has no special status from the EPSS perspective" and is approximately at the 95th percentile of the distribution.

The team sets the threshold itself, based on its monthly capacity.

FIRST's operational guide offers ready-made correspondences between familiar categories and probabilistic thresholds. Over the past 12 months, about 61,000 CVE records have been published, with slightly more than 10% receiving the Critical category. If the current action threshold is CVSS Critical, the equivalent workload would correspond to approximately the 90th percentile of EPSS, i.e., a probability of 4% or more. If the threshold is High and above, which covers the upper approximately 48% of all published records, the equivalent would be about 0.8%. A threshold of 50% excludes 98.8% of the entire published flow, and a threshold of 90% leaves the top 0.2%.

It is also useful to keep baseline rates handy. The KEV catalog includes about 0.5% of published records. Exploitation activity within a 30-day window is observed for approximately 2–3% of records. FIRST provides different values for this indicator on various pages; the formulation from the operational guide is used here. EPSS partner telemetry is predominantly Western; the share of Russian sensors is not disclosed.

The second considers reachability: this is why AppSec platforms usually lower the criticality of findings on code in tests and mocks.

Where the Tree Lies

The weakest point of the tree is the model weights layer and everything adjacent to it.

The first two axes rely on exploitation signals. For niche ML software, such signals are usually absent. EPSS is trained on telemetry from partner sensors, but for records in vLLM, Triton, Ray, or model serialization formats, such telemetry is almost non-existent. FIRST states this directly: "An estimated probability close to zero is a prediction, not a data gap." For a platform team, the difference between "zero predicted" and "no data" does not change the operational decision.

In the analyzed selection of eight NVD records, the value automatable=yes is present for only one—CVE-2023-48022 in Ray, which is disputed by the vendor. However, it does not reach this node: the automation branch only opens upon confirmed exploitation. Confirmed exploitation is absent for any of the eight entries, including a vulnerability with a WONTFIX status and a record for which internet accessibility is irrelevant as per the researchers' direct statement.

In December 2025, CyberOK published a check of EPSS's predictive power on its own data and released a repository of calculations: "Let's combine both rituals and see how much better CyberOK experts could control the attack surface if they blindly believed in the magic of EPSS. Spoiler: they wouldn't control it very well." In 2025, 245 entries were added to KEV. According to CyberOK's calculations, as of the December 29, 2025 catalog snapshot, EPSS values were found for 203 of them—approximately 83%. The author's conclusion has two parts: for him, EPSS is an "excellent 'sorting accelerator,' but a poor 'entry turnstile.'" He states even more concisely about the case in question: "They have no CVE → no EPSS → no magic."

The fifth axis pulls out records that fail both the calendar SLA and the probability assessment: WONTFIX in a production component and accelerator firmware that is updated by a team other than yours in the cloud. Findings without a CVE number are filtered out by the zero question—they do not reach the fifth axis. Without the fifth axis, the tree reproduces the same blind spot as a score-based SLA, only faster.

Russian Context and FSTEC Calculation

The Russian regulator followed a path similar to CISA, and did so earlier.

On June 30, 2025, FSTEC approved a new methodology for assessing the criticality level of vulnerabilities. The formula became four-factor:

V = Icvss × Iinfr × (Iat + Iimp)

The multiplier Iinfr is composed of the component type, its impact on system functioning, and internet accessibility. Two new multipliers describe the possibility of exploitation and the consequences of its successful execution. The previous methodology from October 28, 2022, used the product of only two indicators and did not include such multipliers.

In the same methodology, Iat corresponds to the exploitation status in the tree, Iimp to the technical impact, and the perimeter component within Iinfr to accessibility outside the trusted perimeter. The regulator independently arrived at the same three axes almost a year before the US directive: the methodology was approved on June 30, 2025, and BOD 26-04 was published on June 10, 2026. A critical level according to the methodology starts at V > 8.0.

The Icvss value is taken from the FSTEC Information Security Threat Database (BDU) as the vendor's assessment based on basic CVSS 3.1 metrics. If it is unavailable, clause 13 prescribes: "the specialist independently determines the CVSS version for the assessment." For foreign ML dependencies, this means the specialist chooses the scale version for calculation. As of August 10, 2026, the BDU contains 92,434 vulnerabilities and 227 threats, while the global database has accumulated over 308,000 records. BDU covers approximately one-third of the total CVE count.

Deadlines are set by another document. Order No. 117 was issued on April 11, 2025, and came into force on March 1, 2026. It replaced Order No. 17 of 2013, which established requirements for information protection in state information systems. Clause 38 requires: "Remediation of vulnerabilities <...> or exclusion of their use by applying compensating measures must be carried out." For the critical level, the deadline is set at no more than 24 hours; for high, no more than 7 calendar days. For medium and low levels, deadlines are determined by internal regulations.

Clause 38 places compensating measures and remediation on equal footing: both options are subject to the same deadline. The regulator distinguishes two requirements: meeting the deadline is mandatory, but fixing the vulnerability with a patch is not. A compensating measure in the terms of the order corresponds to mitigation from the second row of the release gate.

Clause 39 prohibits uncontrolled installation of updates and requires deadlines to be set considering the risks associated with the update itself. The regulator explicitly acknowledges that upgrades also create risks. For layers where vulnerability remediation requires a major framework update, this is significant support.

If information about a vulnerability is not in the BDU, the order allows no more than five business days to submit it to FSTEC.

Two similar contexts are deliberately excluded from the analysis. Critical Information Infrastructure (CII) is regulated by Federal Law 187-FZ with separate categorization and FSTEC orders. In certified systems using certified security tools, a major upgrade additionally depends on the status of the certificate and attestation. Both contexts add their own branches to the tree and require separate analysis.

The Russian regulator assesses risk based on axes similar to the US model. The deadline is mandatory, and the team chooses the method to meet it. Compensating measures are permissible alongside patches.

Calculation Boundaries

The calculation is limited by the properties of available sources in three areas.

Inventory Composition. The proportion of AI components in the backlog varies for each platform, and there is no public statistics on this distribution. The buckets above are calculated from the global flow of published CVEs, so they need to be recalculated for your platform based on your own backlog.

Records Without NVD Assessment. They are not included in the calculation of the Critical proportion at all. All five buckets are underestimated by an unknown amount, and the more recent AI components in the inventory, the greater this underestimation.

Telemetry Geography. The population proportions of 48%, 10.5%, 1.2%, and 0.2% are calculated based on the global population of records and EPSS partner telemetry. FIRST warns about this directly: assessments reflect "the global observed population, not your specific environment."

How Calculations Were Made

The numbers in the threshold table are derivative. Input data: approximately 61,000 CVEs over 12 months and a Critical category proportion slightly above 10%. Both values, as well as population proportions of 48%, 1.2%, and 0.2%, are taken from the FIRST operational guide. The baseline KEV rate of about 0.5% is provided in the EPSS FAQ. The capacity "one in ten per month" is taken from the third volume of Prioritization to Prediction by Cyentia Institute and Kenna Security. Annual volumes are divided evenly by 12.

The calculation ceases to work under two assumptions.

The flow of CVEs within a year is uneven. According to NIST data, the number of submitted records in the first quarter of 2026 exceeded the figure for the same quarter of 2025 by almost a third. Therefore, uniform division by 12 underestimates the volume for the second half of the year.

The capacity "one in ten per month" was measured on classic IT infrastructure. For layers where remediation requires a major upgrade or accelerator firmware update, it is definitely lower.

What Remains of the Deadline

The calendar deadline does not disappear from the process and cannot disappear. In the Russian context, it is mandatory by order, and for audits, it is the only metric that can be verified without discussing engineering details. CISA has retained deadlines but made them a function of four variables. The directive requires reviewing the deadlines themselves once per fiscal year.

The method of fulfilling the requirement has changed. Order No. 117 allows meeting the 24-hour deadline with a compensating measure: the regulator explicitly separates vulnerability remediation from deadline compliance. According to survival analysis from Verizon DBIR 2026, 60–70% of KEV vulnerabilities remain open on the seventh day. The sample is international and covers over 13,000 organizations. This proportion does not depend on the year, size, or maturity of the organization.

A calendar deadline is assigned to those who physically cannot meet it, and this is measured. The tree is placed before the deadline so that the deadline is only applied to cases where it is feasible.

Conclusion

An AI platform is not reducible to a single application and a single list of dependencies. It simultaneously hosts web interfaces, APIs, inference services, cluster components, GPU runtime, drivers, firmware, models, and artifacts for which a CVE may not exist at all. A single CVSS score and a calendar SLA do not differentiate these layers, their external accessibility, real exploitation, reachable vulnerable paths, and the possibility of installing a fix.

Therefore, prioritization should begin not with the deadline, but with an actionable decision. SSVC provides a basis for this: first, it separates confirmed exploitation from poc and lack of signal, then it considers technical impact, trusted perimeter, and automation possibility. The fifth axis—the availability of an applicable fix—covers cases where there is no patch, it is outside the team's responsibility, or it requires a disproportionate update.

This approach does not abolish CVSS, EPSS, KEV, and regulatory deadlines. CVSS remains an assessment of vulnerability properties and is used in FSTEC calculations. EPSS helps manage the queue volume within a selected class but does not replace the decision on what to include in the queue. KEV and ssvcV203 provide input signals for the tree. Order No. 117 preserves mandatory deadlines while allowing compliance through compensating measures.

The tree has blind spots. For niche ML components and model weights, there may be no CVE, EPSS, or exploitation telemetry. Therefore, decisions on such findings should be made at the artifact level: checking the serialization format, the origin of weights, upload conditions, isolation, and co-location possibility. A vulnerability without a number should not be considered safe simply because it did not appear in NVD, KEV, or BDU.

A practical next step is simple: tag your own inventory by layers, export ssvcV203 for the current backlog, check the distribution by outcomes, and set EPSS thresholds based on the team's actual monthly capacity. After that, deadlines can be applied to decisions that are actually feasible: install a patch, prepare an update, implement a permanent compensating measure, or block the release.
