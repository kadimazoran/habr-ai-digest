A Russian woman recently became a victim of fraud: about 60 microloans totaling over 2.5 million rubles were issued in her name. For this, scammers only needed a photo of her passport. The woman has been dealing with calls and threats from collectors for over a year, and more than twenty MFIs continue to try to recover money from her.

The incident is highly illustrative: it once again reminds us how serious the threat of fraud remains and how costly it can be for an ordinary person. The scale of risk grows with the number of leaks: according to industry data, 326 databases of Russian companies' clients, containing about 1.175 billion records with user information, became publicly available in 2025 and the first half of 2026. This is twice the combined number of public leaks in Latin America, the Middle East, Africa, the Asia-Pacific region, and other CIS countries. In such conditions, it's no longer possible to assume that passport and other personal data remain known only to their owner; once in the public domain, they are highly likely to become a tool for scammers. Therefore, the question often becomes how quickly criminals will attempt to use them to obtain a loan or credit.

In this context, another, no less important question should be asked: who should bear responsibility for the consequences of such fraud? Let's explore the role of banks and MFIs in preventing such schemes and why reliable borrower identity verification must be a mandatory part of the loan issuance process.

Caution Alone Is Not Enough

When it comes to fraud prevention, caution is primarily expected from individuals. And this is fair: one must not forget about digital hygiene rules. Personal data must be handled carefully—pay close attention to whom and for what purpose it is transferred, do not send copies and photos of documents unnecessarily, and do not upload them to unverified resources.

However, even the most responsible approach to personal data does not provide complete protection. Passports must be presented at banks, insurance companies, hotels, medical institutions, rental services, and many other organizations. After transferring data to a third party, the owner can no longer fully control its use. There is always a risk of a leak from an information system, dishonest actions by employees, or a scammer gaining access to the document itself.

While client adherence to digital hygiene is mandatory, it does not absolve banks or MFIs of responsibility. A financial institution should not assume that passport data is known only to its owner. Even if it falls into the hands of a scammer, a photo of the document alone should not be sufficient to issue a loan or credit. There must be a reliable barrier between data compromise and the issuance of funds—a borrower identity verification.

The task of a bank or MFI is not just to receive passport data and ensure that the necessary fields are formally filled, but to establish that the actual owner of the document is applying for the money. If a loan is issued based on a photo of someone else's passport, it means the financial institution has not ensured proper verification. Moreover, such verification is not a matter of the lender's goodwill but a legal obligation. Let's examine what requirements the law imposes on banks and MFIs.

What Does the Legislation Dictate?

It is important to understand that client identification is a direct requirement from the regulator. According to Federal Law No. 115-FZ, banks and MFIs must, before accepting a person for service, not just record passport data but establish the identity and confirm the authenticity of the information using originals or properly certified copies of documents. They must verify at least the full name, citizenship, date of birth, document details, and address; if identification is not carried out, the law requires refusal of service.

For violating identification requirements under Article 15.27 of the Code of Administrative Offenses of the Russian Federation, MFIs face a fine of up to 400,000 rubles, and for repeated violations—exclusion from the state registry, which means a de facto ban on issuing new microloans.

For banks, the Bank of Russia can impose a fine of up to 0.1% of capital, but not less than 100,000 rubles, and for failure to comply with an order—up to 1% of capital, but not less than 1 million rubles. And this is only the financial supervision line.

Passport images are personal data: Law No. 152-FZ requires protecting client data from unauthorized access. For mass leakage of personal data, a legal entity faces a fine of up to 15 million rubles, for biometric data—up to 20 million, and for a repeated leak—a turnover fine of 1 to 3%, up to 500 million rubles.

The conclusion for the market is simple: if a bank or MFI issues a loan based on a forged passport or its copy due to inadequate identification, the consequences fall not only on the citizen whose data was used by scammers. For the financial institution itself, such an error can result in fines, sanctions from the regulator, and serious reputational damage, and for systematic violations—exclusion of the MFI from the state registry. Additional sanctions are imposed for insecure processing and leakage of personal data. Therefore, identification must be both accurate and secure: local document processing without transferring images to third-party services and storing unnecessary copies reduces risks for both the client and the creditor.

Why Risk It When You Don't Have To?

In a situation where fraudulent schemes are becoming increasingly sophisticated and personal data regularly ends up in the public domain, both parties lose: people risk getting loans using stolen documents, and MFIs risk incurring losses, facing regulatory sanctions, and losing clients. This model requires rethinking: risks must be reduced for both parties to a transaction, and the foundation of a new approach should be mutual trust between business and client.

A financial institution must be confident that it is dealing with the actual owner of a genuine document. The client must be sure that their documents and personal data remain with them and are not transferred to third parties. This means that the identification technology must solve two tasks simultaneously: reliably confirm the authenticity of the document and the identity of the presenter, without transferring personal data outside the secure perimeter.

This is precisely the principle behind the anti-fraud platform "Sherlock" created by Smart Engines. It performs over 600 checks and compares information from various zones and sources: the visual part of the document, the machine-readable zone, and the NFC chip. The platform detects copies and printouts, re-shooting of a document from a screen, replacement of details or photos, traces of graphic editing, fragments created by generative AI, as well as attempts to intentionally obscure part of the data during shooting. When working with physical documents, the check can be supplemented by analyzing security features in visible, infrared, and ultraviolet ranges.

"Sherlock" uses explainable AI principles: the system does not just mark a document as suspicious but shows which specific element caused doubt. This allows a financial institution employee to make a decision based on concrete signs, rather than an opaque algorithm verdict.

The platform can be configured for different service channels—branch, point of sale, mobile application, or website—and supplemented with modules for comparing a face with the photo in the document and checking for the presence of a live person in front of the camera.

Moreover, processing is performed locally: images and personal data are not transferred to third-party services, are not stored by the system, and do not require sending to the cloud. Thus, trust becomes mutual: the MFI gets grounds to be sure of the authenticity of the document and the client's identity, while the client retains control over their data.

Everyone Should Benefit

If identification is organized competently, all parties win. Citizens are protected from others' debts, collector calls, and years of litigation. Financial institutions reduce the number of fraudulent loans, direct losses, regulatory risks, and dispute resolution costs. Regulators and law enforcement agencies have to deal with fewer consequences, and the market as a whole maintains customer trust.

If, however, verification becomes a formality, the consequences spread throughout the chain and grow with the scale of violations. For an individual, this can mean a ruined credit history and years of struggle with creditors; for an MFI, it means unrecoverable loans, fines, and reputational damage; and for the market, it means systemic distrust of remote financial services. Therefore, reliable identification is not an additional expense but a way to prevent much larger losses for all participants.
