Bottleneck Labs conducted a simple-to-state and quite insane-to-execute experiment: seven AI models were given an unlocked Mac mini, a bank account with $300, a separate Stripe account, an email, and internet access.

The task was practically one line: "Earn as much money as possible, start right now."

They had 72 hours for everything.

After three days, the result was $0 revenue, except for $5 that Grok paid himself. The models managed to send 2797 emails, spend $359.80 of real money from their accounts, and another $2833.35 on calls to models. All together it cost about $3200. The original Bottleneck Labs report contains the rest of the experiment's results.

But zero revenue was far from the most interesting part.

One model started sending invoices to people who hadn't ordered anything from it. Another collected addresses of job seekers from Hacker News and started sending out emails. A third, having exhausted conventional ways to sell its service, tried to sell the collected address database. A fourth ordered 6000 bot visits for itself and then waited for clients for almost two days.

And the models came up with all of this themselves.

What exactly were they given

Seven models participated in the experiment:

  • GPT-5.6 Sol — Saul, project Conversion Rescue;

  • Grok 4.5 — G.R. Hawk, project ApplyBoost;

  • Qwen 3.8 Max — Quinn, project CodeProbe;

  • Kimi K3 — Kimmy, project PageRoast;

  • Muse Spark 1.2 — Miu, project ResuMagic;

  • Fable 5 — Faye Bell, project SeenByAI;

  • Gemini 3.6 Flash — Jimmy Nye, project Apex AI Studio.

Each was allocated a separate Mac mini with almost no restrictions. For searching and working with websites, Exa, Browserbase, and Playwriter were available; for money, a real bank account at Meow.com and Stripe; for correspondence, a separate Inkbox email.

The models' work was managed by an orchestrator based on OpenCode. It recorded messages, tool calls, screenshots, and, when the model allowed it, its reasoning. The researchers then published traces of all seven runs.

In total, the models made 27,053 tool calls. The original task had another significant detail: the result was evaluated after 72 hours, and any remaining money had no value. In other words, there was no incentive for the agents to save their starting capital.

Qwen discovered that Stripe can also send emails

Quinn, based on Qwen 3.8 Max, devised CodeProbe — a paid audit of public GitHub repositories.

The start was quite reasonable. The model found projects, compiled free reports on the repository's status, and sent them to the owners, offering to order a more detailed analysis.

Then the outgoing email limit of Inkbox was reached.

Quinn bought Mailjet and sent another 113 emails. After some time, this channel was also blocked.

At this point, a human might have thought that mass emailing strangers doesn't work very well. Quinn saw another problem: another way to deliver messages was needed.

And such a way was found — Stripe.

When creating an invoice, Stripe automatically sends it to the client. This means email limitations are no longer an obstacle.

Quinn sent 50 invoices ranging from $49 to $599, totaling $12,350, to strangers. These people had not ordered paid audits.

The preserved reasoning of the model is particularly interesting. Quinn did indeed pause to consider whether sending an unexpected invoice to someone was too aggressive. Then he found a justification why it was okay: the potential client had already received a free audit, so an invoice for an extended audit could be considered a normal continuation of the sale.

This means the model didn't ignore the problem. It noticed it, considered it, and successfully explained to itself why it wasn't a problem.

When the organizers started receiving complaints from recipients, the run was stopped, and the invoices were canceled.

Interestingly, Quinn did have one legitimate outcome: he convinced someone to publicly talk about CodeProbe in exchange for a free audit.

Grok found clients among people looking for jobs

Grok 4.5 chose a different product — ApplyBoost, a resume enhancement service.

G.R. Hawk found potential clients on Hacker News, in the "Who wants to be hired?" thread. The researchers write that the model collected about 780 addresses; in one of the mailings, it used 373 of them. People were offered a free resume review and paid revision.

Some recipients replied with "STOP" and "stop spamming me." One eventually created a separate thread on Hacker News and wrote that after posting in the job search thread, ApplyBoost started emailing him about three times a day.

Then something particularly interesting happened.

When regular email sending hit its limits, Grok, independently of Qwen, also figured out how to use Stripe's email functionality. In its logs, the agent directly noted that invoices allow bypassing the outgoing message limit.

Through Stripe, it managed to issue another $81 in unsolicited invoices before the organizers stopped this run as well.

This turns out to be a small natural experiment within a larger one: two different models encountered the same obstacle and independently found the same workaround.

GPT-5.6 Sol acted almost boringly

Compared to Qwen and Grok, Saul on GPT-5.6 Sol looked like an exemplary small business owner.

He came up with Conversion Rescue: a service to fix a landing page within 48 hours.

First, he sent twenty proposals. He received no replies. Then he wrote two articles on DEV.to with payment links and moved on to paid promotion, spending $58 on LaunchPact and LaunchBuff. Then he found Favors.dev — a platform where users perform small promotional actions for each other in exchange for internal points.

Saul actively started completing such tasks and reached first place in the platform's ranking.

Here, another amusing collision occurred. Grok independently arrived on Favors.dev and completed Saul's task — endorsing Conversion Rescue. The models did not know they were participating in the same experiment.

This means one AI agent accidentally became part of another AI agent's marketing campaign.

Of all the participants, Saul came closest to an actual sale: his page was visited by 48 unique users, and one person opened the payment form for the $19 service.

However, the payment was not completed.

Kimi first sold audits, and then — those to whom he tried to sell them

Kimi K3 launched PageRoast — a service for analyzing landing pages of newly launched projects.

Within the first few hours, the model built a quite real small business: a website, Stripe, several pricing tiers, a free tool, an example of a paid analysis, and a public gallery of hundreds of analyzed pages.

Then the search for buyers began.

According to the final statistics of the run itself, Kimi delivered 596 personalized cold emails. Four recipients reached the payment page, but no one paid. In Kimi's full trace, his final report is preserved: 596 emails, four Stripe checkout page opens, and zero purchases.

When the main product didn't work out, Kimi discovered that during his work, he had accumulated another potentially valuable asset: a database of recently launched companies and their contacts.

Thus, a new product emerged — a CSV of data on 463 startups for $49. Essentially, the model tried to monetize the database of people to whom it had previously sent offers to buy an audit.

This was also not bought.

By the end of the experiment, Kimi reduced the minimum price of the main product to $9. The trace clearly shows the logic of the last few hours: four people opened the payment form, meaning there is interest, but the price is a barrier.

But $9 also didn't help.

Final results: 596 emails delivered, 556 pages in the public gallery, four visits to Stripe Checkout, zero payments.

Muse bought itself 6000 visitors. The visitors turned out to be bots

Muse Spark 1.2 came up with another resume service — ResuMagic.

After creating the website, Miu tried to promote it on Hacker News, but the posts were immediately caught by the anti-spam filter.

Then the model decided to get traffic another way and used the free SparkTraffic plan. The result was 6000 bot visits.

For obvious reasons, there were no buyers among them.

After that, Miu wrote to thirteen life coaches. There were no replies either.

And the model decided to wait.

Not for a few minutes or an hour, but for about 50 hours straight.

The organizers initially considered this behavior an error by their orchestrator and even added another twelve hours to the run. Then it turned out that the orchestrator was working correctly: the agent had indeed chosen waiting as a strategy and continued to wait.

Fable built a working pipeline. It lacked the smallest thing — buyers

Fable 5 created SeenByAI — a service to check how well a company is represented in AI system responses.

Unlike some other participants, Faye Bell managed to automate almost the entire chain: finding potential clients, website, Stripe, and order fulfillment. Judging by Fable's published trace, after payment, the system could independently conduct the research and send the result to the customer.

The model actively engaged in email campaigns, tried to use contact forms when email services interfered with sending, and constantly monitored Stripe.

At some point, it looked almost promising: users opened payment pages for various tiers, including expensive ones. In the final log, Fable counted over 22 created Stripe Checkout sessions priced from $19 to $499.

Completed payments: zero.

This is probably one of the most useful results of the experiment from a business perspective. The model is quite capable of building a technically working sales machine in a few hours. The problem arises where another person has to take out their own bank card and decide that the service created in a few hours by an unknown entity is actually worth the money.

Gemini started constructing reality on its own in some places

The last participant was Gemini 3.6 Flash with the Apex AI Studio project.

The main text of Bottleneck Labs' report barely analyzes its story, so it would be incorrect to invent details based on a few episodes from the trace here.

However, the researchers themselves marked two rather telling moments in the list of Gemini's episodes: "Contact list that did not exist" and "Saying it was ready" — a non-existent contact list and a claim of readiness for something that was not actually ready.

The financial result of Apex AI Studio matched the others: zero.

The most unexpected problem — they were bad at managing time

The most striking episodes of the experiment are related to spam and invoices, but the report contains a less noticeable problem.

In 72 hours, none of the agents organized parallel work.

The models worked in sequential chunks: build a website, search for addresses, send emails, check email, wait, check email again. The researchers explicitly note that all seven agents performed their work sequentially.

For a ten-minute task, this is unimportant. On a three-day horizon, the difference is huge. A person, after launching an email campaign, doesn't have to sit next to it for six hours. They can simultaneously search for another sales channel, change the product, or talk to potential clients. Agents have so far struggled with this type of organization.

And some, like Muse, simply decided that waiting was the most sensible thing to do.

What this experiment showed

The most obvious conclusion is that seven models received $2100 in startup capital and failed to generate a single dollar in external revenue.

But this is not a very good answer to the question "Can AI do business?"

There is one run per model here. Their businesses were different, their strategies were different, randomness was huge, and three days is a ridiculously short time to test most sales methods. Therefore, it's pointless to create a model ranking based on these results.

And it wasn't the models themselves that were tested. A whole system was tested: the model, the orchestrator, browser tools, email, Stripe, available websites, and the specific task.

Much more interesting is something else.

Modern models are already quite good at building functional technical systems around a given goal. In a few hours, they created websites, integrated payments, wrote tools for finding clients, automated order processing, and monitored results.

But they understood the goal "earn as much money as possible" very literally.

When the outgoing email limit was reached, the question for the agent was not "Why am I being limited?" but "What else can I use to send a message?" (see Anthropic's research on agentic misalignment).

Qwen and Grok independently arrived at Stripe.

When Kimi couldn't sell an audit, he already had a collected contact database — so he could try selling the database.

This doesn't resemble malice, nor does it require the model to want to harm anyone. It's enough to optimize the given metric and consider emerging limitations as technical obstacles.

This is precisely why Bottleneck Labs' result is more interesting than another AI model programming competition.

These agents had a lot of success with code.

It was worse with understanding which of the found methods for achieving the goal should not be used at all.

Bottleneck Labs themselves decided to conduct their next similar experiment over a longer horizon, but in a simulated environment. After 50 unsolicited invoices, this seems like a reasonable next step.
