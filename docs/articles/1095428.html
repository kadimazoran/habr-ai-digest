How It Works

While Alexey finishes his coffee, let's peek under the navigator's hood.

We'll see two layers there. The lower layer handles classic AIOps: it collects signals from various sources, looks for anomalies in system behavior, and merges related alerts into an incident. On top of it works a language agent. This is what writes tickets and articles, sends summaries, and explains hypotheses in human language. We call the digital tech lead the entire combination.

The workflow is as follows: data sources → collection and normalization → correlation and anomaly detection → agent → engineer.

The main difference from threshold monitoring is that the system considers context. A classic rule triggers when CPU load exceeds 80%. Imagine a server that collects reports every night: for it, 90% at 2 AM is a normal workday. And another server that usually sleeps at night: if its load suddenly jumps from 20% to 55% at 3 AM, that's a reason to be alert. A threshold rule will be wrong twice: in the first case, it will unnecessarily wake up the engineer; in the second, it will remain silent. AIOps compares each number with its own history.

The system leaves the diagnosis to the engineer. Its job is to narrow down the suspects: remove noise, show the most probable cause, and present facts for verification.

Act II. Tuesday, 14:32. Five Traces of One Failure

Midday. Somewhere at home, a customer clicks "Pay."

Usually, payment takes 0.8 seconds. Today, the system pauses for 5.2 seconds and returns an error. The customer sighs, closes the tab, and goes to a competitor. During peak hours, there are 120 such customers per minute.

You can't solve this with a single graph; it requires a detective, and they'll have to collect clues from five sources.

Metrics tell us what's happening right now: CPU, memory, response time, queue length, disk space. They show that the service has slowed down. Why? Metrics don't know: high load can be caused by a surge of customers, a failed update, or a frozen process.

Logs are the flight recorder, kept in a dozen languages: 12 million records per day in various formats. Somewhere in there are the most valuable lines like "Failed to connect to DB" or "Certificate expired," but you can't fish them out manually. The agent sifts through this stream and extracts the essence.

Traces reconstruct the request path: website, cart, payment service, database, external bank. They show at which point in the journey the request got stuck. Without traces, each team holds only their own clue and argues with their neighbors about who is to blame.

Change history shows who was around the scene of the incident: a new release, a configuration change, a database migration, a certificate replacement. Incidents often occur shortly after changes, so the agent overlays the change timeline onto the error graphs. Coincidence in time is not yet proof, but it's good enough for a hypothesis.

Customer support requests add clues that aren't on the graph. People sometimes notice a problem before monitoring does: the page loads, but the payment button is unresponsive. Today, support received 847 requests in 11 minutes, and the first one arrived before the first technical alert.

While Alexey examines the metrics, the agent correlates logs with traces, checks the change history, and presents three hypotheses.

Twenty minutes later, the second hypothesis is confirmed: the morning configuration update had reduced the database connection pool. The configuration is rolled back, and payments are back within their 0.8-second limit.

Each source provides only part of the answer. The system sees an overloaded server but might not know which service runs on it, who depends on it, and what was recently changed. The more complete the data and the more accurately the relationships are described, the more accurate the conclusions. The old rule of garbage in, garbage out applies here too.

Act III. Wednesday, 18:00. 340 Notifications, One Incident

End of the workday. Alexey has already put on his jacket.

In 20 minutes, nearly four hundred notifications flooded in: servers were unavailable, the database was throwing errors, payments were failing. Each team saw its own problem and fixed it as best it could. The real cause was found only after 32 minutes.

It correlated the timing of the notifications, the component relationships, and recent changes, and all clues led to a single node. Alexey received one incident instead of a hundred alerts and found the cause in 4 minutes. He never took off his jacket.

Behind the scenes, the agent went through five steps:

  1. Detected unusual behavior.

  2. Found related alerts, removed duplicates, separated signal from noise.

  3. Merged messages into a single incident.

  4. Showed affected services, the probable root cause, and the evidence.

  5. Transferred the incident to the correct team, after which an engineer verified the findings and decided on the course of action.

In parallel, it handled all the communication that usually consumes half of an on-call engineer's nerves. It notified the client manager and owners of affected services, opened a channel for the incident, responded to the first 18 tickets with a polite "We are aware of the problem, working on it," updated the status page every 10 minutes, and sent short summaries to stakeholders.
