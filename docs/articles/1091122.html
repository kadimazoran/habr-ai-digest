How OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and xAI are Competing for Models, Clouds, and Developers

OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and xAI are no longer just competing on model quality. The real battle is for computing power, custom chips, cloud presence, API pricing, and developer ecosystems. This article examines their strengths in each area, why competitors are also partners and suppliers, and why in 2026, infrastructure is becoming more critical than model releases.

In May 2026, Anthropic announced it is receiving Colossus 1 – a cluster with over 300 MW of power, housing more than 220,000 NVIDIA GPUs. It was built for Grok, and is owned by SpaceX, which acquired xAI in February. One model developer rented a supercomputer from another, and the market accepted this calmly.

This is the norm in 2026. Model releases happen every couple of months, while the real battle is fought more quietly: in contracts for gigawatts, in custom chips, in cloud catalogs, in pricing per million tokens, and in the tools developers become accustomed to.

The First Weapon: Gigawatts

AI power is now measured in gigawatts, with the number of accelerators being secondary. Chips can be purchased in months, but connecting a new data center to the grid and cooling it takes years. Hence the announcements of deals where power capacity is listed first.

OpenAI is the most detailed about its current power capacity. The company's CFO cited figures in January: 0.2 GW in 2023, 0.6 GW in 2024, and around 1.9 GW in 2025. In April 2026, OpenAI announced that it has secured over 10 GW through contracts, surpassing its 2029 Stargate goal. Hardware contracts are also in place: with NVIDIA for 5 GW of Vera Rubin systems, with AMD for 6 GW, with Broadcom for 10 GW of custom accelerators, and with AWS for approximately 2 GW of Trainium. These figures should not be simply added up, as much of this hardware is for the same data centers.

The contracted power is not yet operational. According to Epoch AI, which monitors construction via satellite imagery and contractor statements, only one of the seven Stargate sites was operational in mid-April, in Abilene, at approximately 0.3 GW. Epoch itself expected 0.6 GW there by the end of May and 1.2 GW by the end of the year. The expansion of Abilene beyond 1.2 GW was halted in March due to delays in grid connection.

AWS promises Anthropic up to 5 GW, including about 1 GW on Trainium by the end of 2026. Google agreed back in 2025 to supply up to a million TPUs, and Broadcom mentioned on its September investor call that it would deliver 1 GW of Ironwood for Anthropic in 2026 and another 5 GW of TPU 8i in 2027. Further capacity includes up to 1 GW in Azure, up to 2 GW on AMD, Colossus 1, and its own data centers in Texas and New York. Anthropic does not disclose how much of this is already operational.

xAI is building rapidly: the first Colossus, according to the company, was assembled in 122 days. SpaceX's IPO prospectus states that Colossus and Colossus II together provide about 1 GW. Google is less dependent on external hardware than anyone else: it has its own cloud, its own data centers, and TPUs that it develops with Broadcom. Alphabet is budgeting between $195 and $205 billion in capital expenditures for 2026.

The Second Weapon: Custom Chips

While everyone else is signing contracts, NVIDIA is raking in cash. For the quarter ending July 2026, its revenue was $96.2 billion, with data centers contributing $89 billion, a 117% increase year-over-year. Everyone who can is trying to reduce this dependency, at least partially.

Google has made the most progress. In April, it unveiled its eighth-generation TPU, featuring two specialized chips this time: 8t for training and 8i for running trained models. Training primarily requires computational power, while inference demands ample fast memory for long contexts.

Google claims up to 2.7 times better performance per dollar for training with the 8t compared to Ironwood. These are Google's own figures; independent benchmarks are not yet available, and general availability is promised by the end of the year. In April, Google also announced it would begin supplying TPUs to select customers for their own data centers. The first revenue from this is expected in late 2026, with the main revenue in 2027.

Amazon has its own line, Trainium. The third generation has been available since December 2025, featuring 144 GB of HBM3e per chip. Amazon lists both Anthropic and OpenAI among its customers. In January, Microsoft launched Maia 200 and uses it to serve models like GPT-5.2.

OpenAI has commissioned its own chip from Broadcom. According to OpenAI's own benchmarks, the Jalapeño accelerator for inference achieves 1.5–1.9 times more work per watt at peak throughput than GB200 and GB300 systems. In September, Broadcom reported that it had started shipments, with deployment of 1.3 GW planned for 2027.

xAI continues to rely entirely on NVIDIA. While construction waits for its own silicon to mature, the company has to pay NVIDIA's full markup.

The Third Weapon: Cloud Without Exclusivity

Just a couple of years ago, OpenAI operated primarily on Azure, Anthropic on AWS, and Gemini on Google Cloud. In 2026, exclusivity has largely disappeared. In April, OpenAI and Microsoft revised their agreement: OpenAI can sell its products through any cloud, although new releases still appear on Azure first. Since June, OpenAI models and Codex have been generally available on Amazon Bedrock. Claude has been available on all three major clouds since late 2025, and Grok is sold through Microsoft Foundry and Oracle OCI.

This shift is driven by how large clients make purchases. A bank or retailer already has a cloud contract, approved security requirements, and a budget to spend. A model not available on that cloud is practically non-existent for them. Thus, model providers go where the client's money already resides.

Meanwhile, clouds are selling competitor models, leasing them hardware, and investing in them. If we map the connections between the two largest labs and their suppliers, we get the following:

The market has divided. The most powerful models are sold as premium: GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.1 both cost $10 per million input tokens and $50 per million output tokens. For comparison, the flagship GPT-5 in 2025 cost $1.25 and $10. Gemini 4 Argon, not yet publicly available via API, was announced by Google at a promotional price of $2 and $10, with prices increasing to $4 and $20 after the promotion.

In the mid-range segment, there's a price war. GPT-6.1 Sol, Gemini 3.1 Pro, and Grok 4.7 all cost $2 for input. Opus cost $15 and $75 a year ago, while Opus 5.5 is now $4 and $20.

There are also reverse trends. The Gemini 3.8 Flash pricing page explicitly states that from January 1, 2027, input costs will increase from $0.75 to $1.5, and output from $3.75 to $7.5. Google has not provided reasons, but it appears the promotional period is ending.

In the long run, prices are still falling. Epoch AI calculated that the cost of the same result on mathematical and scientific benchmarks has decreased by approximately 13 times per year since 2023, with the fastest drops occurring in the months following the release of a record-breaking model.

However, the price per token is not the price of a task. Artificial Analysis runs models through a standardized set of tests and shows the average cost per task. Grok 4.7 in xhigh mode costs $3.74 per task, while GPT-6.1 Sol in max mode costs $0.72, despite similar tariffs. Sol also achieves higher scores: 52 points versus 46. A model that engages in extensive reasoning consumes the tariff difference through its token usage.

The Fifth Weapon: Developer Ecosystem

The final weapon is developer tools. Each of the four companies has its own code agent: Codex for OpenAI, Claude Code for Anthropic, Gemini CLI for Google, and Grok Build for xAI. How they are structured and differ could be a topic for a separate article; what's more important here is something else.

Part of the ecosystem has become shared. The MCP protocol for connecting tools to models was developed by Anthropic and later supported by OpenAI and Google. A tool written once works with models from different providers, reducing the cost of switching.

The situation is different with open weights. OpenAI, Google, and xAI have released models with open weights: gpt-oss, Gemma, and Grok 2. These can be run on one's own server, eliminating dependency on external APIs. Claude is only available via API and clouds, so a team needing an on-premises model would not turn to Anthropic for this.

What About the Models Themselves?

The gap between the best models is surprisingly small. In the Artificial Analysis index as of early October, Claude Opus 5.5 holds the top spot with 58 points in max mode, where a task costs around $6. GPT-6 Astra scores 53 points for $3.26, Gemini 4 Argon scores 53 for $1.99, and Grok 4.7 scores 46.

In the arena where users compare models live, Gemini 4 Argon is in first place, with about 20 points separating the first and fourth positions. However, GPT-6 Astra is in 29th place, and Grok 4.7 is in 92nd, indicating that different platforms show different leaders. The classic SWE-bench Verified programming test has lost credibility: OpenAI stopped publishing results for it after discovering errors in the tests and traces of tasks in the training data.

The lead of the top models is maintained for months, and the next competitor's release erodes it. Therefore, a single strong model is not enough, and the competition has shifted to areas where advantages last longer.

Our Thoughts

If we had to choose one weapon, we would bet on operational gigawatts – power that is already connected to the grid and cooled. Models catch up to each other in months, prices are revised quarterly, but a data center is built over years and cannot be replicated after a competitor's release.

By this criterion, each company has its strengths and vulnerabilities. Google is the least dependent on external chips and clouds, but its new flagship Gemini 4 Argon is currently available only to a select group of clients. OpenAI has secured the most contracts and publishes operational gigawatts by year, making the gap between contracted and operational capacity most visible. Abilene showed that a grid connection cannot be accelerated by contract. Anthropic has spread its orders across four platforms, but almost all its hardware belongs to others, and it does not disclose its operational power capacity. xAI is building its clusters faster than others but relies on a single chip supplier, and SpaceX leased Colossus 1 to a competitor.

When capacity is insufficient for everyone, prices don't only go down, as the Gemini 3.8 Flash example has already shown. The advice for developers is simple: keep model calls behind a thin abstraction layer, compare the cost of a completed task rather than the token price, and do not tie your product to a feature exclusive to one provider if it can be done without.
