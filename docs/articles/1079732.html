About the Author and the Book's Target Audience

The author, Suhas Pai, is the co-founder and CTO of Hudson Labs, a Y Combinator-backed fintech startup in Toronto. Previously, he co-chaired the privacy working group for the BigScience project during the creation of the BLOOM model. He has over a decade of experience in machine learning, including contributions to the development of open large language models. This experience is evident in the text's depth and the thoroughness of its practical details – the material is written by someone who has personally designed LLM systems.

The target audience is broadly defined in the preface: software developers transitioning to AI application development, machine learning specialists (both practitioners and researchers), and product managers. The formal requirement for the reader is a solid understanding of Python and a basic grasp of machine learning and deep learning concepts.

In practice, the material is intended for readers who have already moved beyond the working prototype stage.

  • A beginner who is just starting to explore LLMs and wants to learn how to send requests to pre-trained models via API might find the book excessively detailed. The author covers the simplest level of model usage in a few pages in the first chapter and quickly moves on to the internal workings of the transformer.

  • However, for a developer who has already built a minimally functional chatbot and is facing the challenge of turning it into a product application with predictable behavior and reasonable request costs, the book is almost ideal.

  • Technical leads and product managers may also find the book useful. It provides an understanding of LLM system architecture for making decisions about fine-tuning, RAG, or agents, without turning the reader into a machine learning researcher.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is the development of an artificial intelligence system that combines a large language model (LLM) with an external company database or documents. When a user asks a question, the program first searches this repository for relevant facts and then provides them to the neural network so it can formulate an accurate answer based on real corporate data.

Let's Start with Skepticism: Is the Book's Tech Stack Still Relevant?

The English original, "Designing Large Language Model Applications," was released in March 2025 by O'Reilly. This means the author's main work on the text was completed no later than the end of 2024. In the AI field, where update cycles are measured in months, this represents a significant lag by mid-2026, and the book shows characteristic signs of this delay.

Among models with native tool-calling support, the author uses Llama 3.1 Instruct (8B/70B/405B) and details its system prompt with tokens like <|python_tag|> and <|eom_id|>. The frameworks (sets of libraries and development methods) for agent orchestration listed include LangChain, LlamaIndex, CrewAI, AutoGen, MetaGPT, and XAgent.

The author himself acknowledges that agent frameworks are likely to undergo significant changes in the coming months and years. The section on guardrails relies on separate libraries like Guardrails, NeMo-Guardrails, and the Llama Guard model. Anthropic's demonstration of computer desktop control is described as an early version with limited results on comparative tests (benchmarks).

The section on inference-time computation stands out for its accurate trend prediction. The author identifies this as one of the most relevant topics for 2025 and beyond, even before such models became widely available. At the time the book was written, the technology existed more as research prototypes than ready-to-use AI products, but the author's prediction has been confirmed.

It's best to approach the rapid pace of model version and framework name changes during the preparation, printing of the original, and subsequent translation of the book into Russian with a balanced perspective, accepting it as an inevitability. More importantly, the fundamental chapters on transformer architecture, tokenization, alignment training, and the RAG pipeline are based on principles that are independent of the specific version of a particular library.

Now, let's look inside the book, and then in a separate section, we will examine in detail what exactly has changed in LLM application development practices since the original book was released eighteen months ago.

How the Book is Structured and Its Strengths

The book consists of thirteen chapters, divided into three parts, totaling around 400 pages. Part One, "LLM Components" (Chapters 1-4), delves into the internal workings of a language model:

• Definition and brief history of LLMs, prompting, accessing models via API;

• Pre-training data, its filtering, deduplication, and personal information removal;

• Vocabulary and tokenizer, including BPE and WordPiece algorithms;

• Transformer architecture, attention mechanism, training objectives.

The author explicitly warns in the introduction that creating a language model from scratch is not the book's goal, but understanding its internal structure remains key to effectively working with the model in practice. The initial chapters are dense due to the material's density, but they provide insight into why a model behaves in a certain way.

Part Two, "LLM Applications" (Chapters 5-9), focuses on practical model adaptation for specific tasks:

• Model selection, evaluation using HELM, Open LLM Leaderboard, and the Elo rating system in Chatbot Arena;

• Fine-tuning – from training algorithm parameters to efficient parameter tuning;

• Advanced fine-tuning approaches, including continuous pre-training and model merging;

• Alignment training, hallucinations, and methods to combat them, developing reasoning skills;

• Inference optimization – caching, quantization, speculative decoding.

The chapter on benchmarking is particularly strong. The author methodically explains why leaderboards like MMLU can be interpreted in multiple ways with vastly different results, providing a concrete example using a sample of data from Llama 2.

The section on mitigating AI hallucinations consolidates methods that are typically scattered across dozens of individual articles – self-consistency, citation, contrastive decoding.

Part Three, "LLM Application Paradigms" (Chapters 10-13), is dedicated to the system level:

• LLM interaction with external tools and agent definition;

• Representation learning and vector embeddings, chunking, vector databases;

• Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) as a complete pipeline;

• Design patterns and system architecture for multi-model systems.

An important detail is that the RAG chapter doesn't reduce the topic to a simple "put documents in a vector database and retrieve similar chunks via prompt." The pipeline is broken down into six distinct stages – query rewriting, retrieval, re-ranking, refinement, insertion, and generation – with specific techniques and their limitations discussed for each stage.

The chapter on agents provides a strict definition of an agent, separately discusses passive, explicit, and autonomous interaction of LLMs with their environment, and details the components of an industrial agent system: models, tools, data stores, guardrails, and verifiers.

The book contains over 80 practical exercises and references to numerous research papers, making it resemble a researcher's notes more than recommended reading for IT courses.

How LLM Application Development Has Changed by Summer 2026 – About Claude Code and Modern Models

In eighteen months, several technological generations have passed in the world of large language models. It's difficult to cover everything in one section, but let's try to highlight the main shifts that are important to keep in mind while reading the book.

  1. Tool Integration. Chapter 10 demonstrates calling tools by manually defining a JSON schema in the system prompt and connecting wrappers for web search, Wikipedia API, and databases via LangChain. In November 2024, almost concurrently with the release of the English original, Anthropic published the Model Context Protocol (MCP) – an open standard for connecting models to external tools and data sources.

By 2026, MCP is supported by OpenAI, Google DeepMind, and Microsoft. The protocol has been transferred to the management of the Agentic AI Foundation under the Linux Foundation, and the number of public MCP servers has exceeded ten thousand. The task that the book solves by writing custom wrappers for each service is now accomplished with a few clicks and connecting a ready-made MCP server via a standard protocol.

  1. Agent Development Environments. Web agents are described in the book as a promising but immature technology with limited benchmark results. Claude Code and similar tools have since evolved into a full-fledged development environment for agent development – with isolated sub-agents, each having its own context window and toolset, and a system of skills for reusable expertise on specific tasks.

So-called "hooks" have appeared for AI agents – these are control points in their operation where the process is temporarily interrupted to run pre-written code or check rules. They are needed for humans to manage AI actions, set strict boundaries, and protect the system from errors.

  1. Reasoning Models. The book describes inference-time computation as an emerging research trend, citing simple techniques like re-sampling and chain-of-thought prompting as examples. In the intervening time, chain-of-thought reasoning has evolved from a separate prompt engineering technique into a built-in model mode that can be enabled or disabled.

Modern reasoning models solve this task correctly without any workaround prompts because the process of step-by-step reasoning before answering is built into their operation by default.

  1. Verification and Protection. The separate libraries for output verification and security described in the book have not disappeared but are no longer the sole solution. The approach of using an LLM as an arbiter, which the author mentions as one option, has become standard practice for quality assessment, and some guardrail functions have moved to the model and API provider level.

The conclusions regarding the material's relevance for the reader are as follows: Chapters 1-9, focusing on the model's internal workings, fine-tuning, and inference optimization, should be read as written – these principles are not outdated and will not become so in the near future.

Chapter 10 on agents and Chapter 13 on multi-model architectures should be read with consideration for the current state of LLM ecosystems. The core logic of building an agent system – models, tools, data stores, verifiers, orchestration – remains valid, but the specific tools for its implementation need to be checked against current documentation.

How This Book Differs from Other AI Development Publications

The book's cover features a quote from Jay Alammar, co-author of the well-known book "Hands-On Large Language Models" – this serves as a benchmark for comparison. The book has a Russian edition; see the review on Habr. Alammar's and Maarten Grutenhorst's book is closer to a practical introduction built around ready-made libraries and short code examples for specific tasks. The reviewed book, "Designing Large Language Model Applications," moves in the opposite direction – from fundamentals to practice, with an emphasis on engineering trade-offs and system architecture.

Another book with a similar spirit is "AI Engineering" by Chip Huyen (original O'Reilly, 2025, with a Russian edition), which examines the construction of AI products from the perspective of infrastructure and operational processes. Our author's book is less focused on MLOps and pays more attention to the internal workings of the model itself – tokenization, transformer architecture, training objectives – material that is only briefly touched upon in Huyen's book.

There are virtually no Russian-language equivalents with the same depth as Suhas Pai's "Designing Large Language Model Applications." The topic is usually covered in fragments in blogs or IT courses focused on quick practical results. The translated edition fills this niche, arguably without competition.

Conclusion

The main value of the book "Designing Large Language Model Applications" lies in its conceptual framework, which is more valuable than any specific code examples. When a developer builds an agent system through trial and error, they often lack the vocabulary to describe system components, an understanding of why a separate verification layer is needed, or a grasp of how a model cascade differs from a router. This book provides precisely that vocabulary and understanding, and further practice builds upon it.

Chapter 8 on hallucinations and reasoning is particularly recommended – this material is worth reading even for those who do not plan to launch their own RAG pipeline (an AI model with access to a corporate database). Understanding why a model hallucinates and what methods reduce the likelihood of this phenomenon is useful for anyone building a working product on top of LLMs, regardless of the specific stack.
