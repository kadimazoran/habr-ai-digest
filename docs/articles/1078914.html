1. Excellent Source Materials

During the first year of unsuccessful attempts, I polished "good source materials," changed upscalers, and searched for the best ones – and found them. But one day, I delved deeper into the issue – and it turned out that they were only "good" in part.

As you know, the original cartoon from the 40s-60s was recorded on film, and nowadays it is found and restored: physically cleaned, treated with solutions to remove scratches, deeply scanned, and expensive software is used on the digitized material.

And here's the catch: the resulting restorations are most often released in two formats – DVD (480p) and Blu-ray (720p–1080p).

Naturally, I didn't know about Blu-ray for a long time, and when the news reached me – I tried to upscale it the old-fashioned way and ended up in a mess.

Watch the video only in 4K – otherwise, YouTube will degrade the main subject of discussion with its low bitrate.

"Ants" crawl all over the video – what to do about it? "Ah, a mystery," I thought, after running the video through a dozen more upscalers, still getting no good results, and almost gave up on the idea. But there's an antidote for every poison ↓↓↓

1.1. The Cause

The "ants" are not entirely an upscaler artifact. Any film print has grainy noise, and it so happened that in the restoration industry, it is considered as important as the video beneath it – an aesthetic charm and preservation of the original's microscopic details. Except for the very latest webcams on streaming services, you won't find film without noise.

But, contrary to my expectations, upscalers don't work by magic, and when trying to "increase" the resolution on grainy video, most of them don't know how to handle the "extra baggage." This is how "crawling ants" appear. The resolution is increased – the sharpness is raised, but with a ton of local mini-artifacts.

Obviously, for the upscaling to work correctly, the noise must be removed – otherwise, any action is meaningless.

2. Unified Russian Audio Track

A small but very important digression, after which we will return to the video.

I consider the audio accompaniment to be just as important as the video – and… There is no complete dubbing of all episodes in any form – only about a couple of dozen episodes out of a hundred and fifty have been dubbed. Yes – one of the most popular cartoons on the planet has never been fully dubbed in Russia. In the 90s and 00s, there were many different translations of "Tom and Jerry" into Russian; the cartoon was extremely popular – enthusiasts in the late 10s collected over 20 different voiceovers. I created one "all-Russian" one from them – it uses the best voices and translates every title card – 100% coverage of the cartoon series after 2 months of work – removing seams and mixing music. Although there was one flaw that made my life difficult back then.

Each voiceover was of varying quality, but all had a common problem: compression quality during re-recording and sometimes the durability of the medium itself.

From the histogram alone, it is clear that the re-recording, in addition to voiceovers, killed most of the frequencies, even of the musical accompaniment.

So, the goal was to take the voice from the dubbing and the crystal-clear musical accompaniment from the original film – the answer is "UVR". After a bit of fiddling, you can separate the voice and sounds, but it doesn't always work stably, and then you need to adjust it in Audacity. The main thing is that I succeeded, having paid a couple of months of this life:

3. Noise Removal

There are two quality options for solving the problem, which I managed to develop after extensive trial and error:

  1. Quickly use a denoiser neural network.

    There was a snag with the first one – such neural networks are very slow, and they can't handle all types of noise. It took about 2 hours of waiting for one 7-minute episode.

    Using one such (SCUNet), I processed all 163 episodes over two weeks of aggressive GPU work… and rejected about 100 of them.

4. Posters

After all the stages – searching for source materials, mixing audio, and months of noise removal – I was simply tired of the routine and turned my attention to a less noticeable, but appealing part of any official release – the accompanying content, posters. For the theatrical releases of "Tom and Jerry" up to episode 71 (1952), full posters with the comedic premise of the episode were always drawn. And after half a century, they are often preserved in very poor condition: the paint has dried, and there are creases on the paper.

5. History: Clip Shows and Repeated Parasitism on Old Content from Old Episodes

AND DO YOU KNOW how to reduce costs when you've accumulated a lot of old material? – That's right, reuse it!

6 episodes consist almost entirely of compilations of old episodes. Yes, yes, what you see on the Warner Bros. YouTube channel – lots of sloppy compilations of "Tom and Jerry | MEGA-ULTRA COMPILATION | Collection No. X" with tens of millions of views and not a single normal full episode – is not new speak at all.

"So what's your problem with these 'clip shows'?" – the answer lies in their original technical implementation back then. You see, film doesn't need 30 years to age – two years is enough, and it's even worse if the film itself is a release print and not the original. And compilations were also made from 4-8 episodes – so the range of noise quality can be simply cosmic:

And instead of the simple solution "leave it as is," I chose the most "correct" one, classically. Clip shows were assembled by editing different videos – so I can also make a 1-to-1 compilation. But instead of "mismatched" quality – use the highest quality available.

I got lost in this endeavor for about 10 hours. No, not because it's difficult to find 6 episodes out of 163 by a short segment of 10 seconds – I can recall any episode number by a single frame with a piece of cloud background – but because the original editor suffered from some kind of senility. It turns out that in the middle of most of these cuts, a couple of frames were cut out almost every second.

And in a couple of moments, I was completely blown away – in episode 149, they twice took original CEL layers from somewhere deep within the studio, slightly redrew the character's animation, and replaced the background or even extended the frame. I spent about 2-3 hours searching for the source material for this one episode because of this "black swan."

7. Encoding (Compression)

I won't go into much detail about the preset – if you're interested, look at the spoiler with the code – but there are two interesting points worth mentioning.

FFmpeg Preset

-c:v hevc_nvenc -preset p7 -tune uhq -multipass fullres -rc vbr -cq 28 -qmin 18 -qmax 51 -profile:v main10 -pix_fmt p010le -b_ref_mode each -bf 5 -g 240 -keyint_min 1 -rc-lookahead 53 -lookahead_level auto -spatial-aq 1 -temporal-aq 1 -aq-strength 8 -nonref_p 1 -qp_cb_offset 0 -qp_cr_offset 0 -tf_level 0

I used hardware encoding (GPU) instead of software encoding (CPU) – it works much faster on my specs: Ryzen 5600 and RTX 4060 Ti. However, this choice usually has a catch – the GPU encoder is primarily focused on fast streaming, not on maximizing picture quality, and the corresponding conclusions are obvious. But for me, this compromise works out easier – let's recall the meat grinder the video went through:
Noisy film → noise removal (+ blur one way or another) → halo removal (also + a little blur) → upscaling.

10-bit, or the invisible carrier wave

Simply put: most videos you watch are in an 8-bit color space, and adding just 2 bits allows for a significant optimization of video weight without loss of quality, with a slight increase in player resource consumption – especially in 2D animation.

Among other things, this is one of the main drivers of radical weight reduction: in the previous remaster, the 2K video weighed ~60 GB after compression, and in the new one, at 4K – only 56 GB. Resolution – +125%, weight – -6.67% – not +50%, but minus.
