Some of these decisions can be offloaded from the LLM to a fast classifier model. The most discussed one now is Jev from TypeSafe, and it already has open-source alternatives. Below, I analyze how Jev-like models are used in voice bots by other teams, and compare their cases with our experience at Voximplant.

Why an LLM alone is not enough

In 2022–2024, when large language models were not yet widely available, and a bot in a phone conversation already needed to understand intent, we built our own classifier, Voximplant Avatar. It quickly and cheaply extracted intent from phrases based on a configurable catalog and marked whether entities, i.e., structured data, system or custom, were included in the phrase. It worked well, and still does in some projects.

Then large language models appeared, and for a moment it seemed that a classifier was no longer needed: the model itself would understand the meaning and compose an answer. In practice, the euphoria, as usual, ended quite quickly. In live projects, medium-sized models burst from the size of the initial prompt or after several topic changes and started hallucinating. Large and expensive ones handled the load, but either broke the unit economics of the solution or responded so slowly that it was unacceptable in a phone conversation. And some models were not available in certain regions at all.

When you build a voice bot professionally, for a large enterprise client, you need to optimize everything: response quality, metric adherence, budget. Bot builders have circumvented these limitations through engineering. They created multi-stage bots, placed several agents on one number with a router model, and where the dialogue strictly followed a script, they left scripted parts. We handle several hundred large commercial projects a year and have accumulated enough experience to say: a single model misses the moment of tool invocation, goes beyond the context window, and burns an unacceptable number of tokens. In real-world cases, you cannot rely on a single model; it almost certainly won't handle the entire conversation.

By this time, Avatar was already outdated, primarily architecturally. We started replacing it with a similar scheme using a small, cheap, and fast language model, and then Jev came out. Everything new, as it turned out, was well-forgotten old.

What is it anyway

Jev is the System One model from TypeSafe, a decision model released in early access in September 2026. It takes a state (a piece of transcript, the last bot utterance, a scenario step) and a set of questions as input. It returns a typed answer: a choice from predefined options, a rating on a short scale, or a probability for a "yes/no" answer to a single direct question.

Jev does not write text; it has no output tokens as such, it simply returns a typed structure. What to do with this structure next is decided by the bot builder. The official description and question types are on the TypeSafe website.

Jev is quite fast. Latency measurements vary, but generally fall within "up to 500 ms": some found a batch of thirty-five questions took about 170 ms on a warmed-up connection, while others took 332–892 ms. TypeSafe's materials mention a range of 70–500 ms.

For completeness, I'll add that the topic of classifier models has recently gained a second wind, and Jev is not the only one. Here's what has come out recently (reviews are available in Latent Space and in the Reddit discussion):

  • Laya from a researcher at Convai Innovations, the main and most popular free open-source alternative (Apache 2.0 license).

  • Clef from Cloudflare, an open model for ultra-fast decision-making within software systems.

  • CMF from Cortiq, a lightweight open model weighing only 304.5 MB. It operates in milliseconds without generating tokens and shows high accuracy in classification tasks.

  • Bespoke Nimble from Bespoke Labs, an "open Jev" recipe based on LoRA fine-tuning of the Qwen3.5-9B model using limited decoding.

  • Kev from @jaredpalmer. It started as an ultra-compact Jev-like model Kev-0.5B based on Qwen2.5-0.5B, designed for efficient local execution on consumer devices like MacBook Pro, and is now a family of models.

  • CLM-8B, a joint development by Stanford and Nvidia. The model caches reusable agent actions and, in the authors' tests, shows higher speed than Jev.

In short, there are already many Jev-like models. I want to consider the topic as a whole: how a classifier model can improve a voice bot.

Classifier at the input, specialized agent for each topic

A typical situation in a voice menu: one number, but several topics within it. Booking, order status, complaint, "connect me to a human." If all this lives in one prompt, a medium-sized model swells up and after the second or third topic change starts talking nonsense.

The solution is as follows: place a fast classifier at the input. It selects a small agent, tailored to one topic, and connects it to the conversation. Topic changes are also its decision, as a separate step, before control is handed over to the large model and it starts composing an answer.

Piotr Zaczek demonstrated this on a voice circuit with a short video. The video is about the time it takes for the system to decide to change the dialogue direction: 0.5 s versus almost 5 s, plus savings on the large model's tokens. Piotr took away its ability to make inferences and reason beyond its topic; now the classifier does it.

Why do this: a specialized agent has a short prompt and one set of tools. The classifier is cheap and fast; it's not a pity to ask it at every topic change.

In the same post, Zaczek separately, without the video, lists three applications he likes besides routing:

  • post-call analysis;

  • always-on background constraints, bypassing voice response;

  • deciding when to hand over the call to another agent or human.

Let's remember these classifier applications.

Model suggests, Jev evaluates

The most expensive bot errors occur in actions when it changes something: booking a client under the wrong name or not hanging up after "goodbye."

Giorgi Lomidze discusses in the post JEV in Production from September 24, 2026, how they moved such decisions from the speech model to a Jev-based classifier in two weeks. The product is called Pocos, a phone assistant for salons and studios.

How it works. A call comes into Voximplant via SIP. The VoxEngine scenario connects to the LLM (Gemini Live in their production) via RealtimeAPIClient and maintains the voice dialogue: greeting, interruption control, timers, hang-up. The speech model hears the caller and suggests calling a tool. All model inferences, before they can change anything in the system or dialogue, are moderated by the classifier: a short packet of facts is assembled, Jev receives several "yes/no" questions, and probabilities are returned.

The questions are literal. "Did the caller confirm this action?" "Does the last utterance change, confirm, or cancel what we just said?" "Did the caller say goodbye?" Further actions depend on the "yes/no" probability returned by Jev. Specific thresholds need to be adjusted to the situation. Giorgi's strong agreement starts at 0.9, weak agreement drops to 0.1, and goodbye is considered strong from 0.75 and only if the caller hasn't brought up a new topic.

The essence of the approach can be stated as: LLM only suggests, Jev evaluates, the business makes the decision.

How to semantically determine when the caller has finished speaking

The caller suddenly falls silent mid-sentence. The silence timer is ready to hand over to the bot. "Yes" after the question "reschedule to Tuesday?" is considered a completed utterance. "I wanted to say that..." is not, even though the pause length is the same.

We have our own tools for determining the end of a turn in a dialogue, Agentic VoiceDSP. One module of VoxEngine includes Silero VAD, the Pipecat Smart Turn end-of-turn detector, and an optional Hush noise suppressor. The developer has control over turn events (TurnStarted, TurnInferenceTriggered, TurnStopped), and can react to each. Smart Turn specifically determines based on the pause whether the turn has ended or not, and if not, it holds the silence for stopSecs. That is, it's control at the audio level, based on an acoustic turn model.

On top of this, we want to determine the end of an utterance based on the meaning of what was said: is the thought finished, or is the person looking for the next word. Technically, this should work. Typical ASR latency is about 50 ms, Jev classification is about 200 ms, together about 250 ms. Not instantaneous, but for most industries, when applied to a full-cascade architecture of phone bots, this should be sufficient.

What we want to experiment with:

  1. First, the turn is tracked by VoiceDSP and VAD.

  2. On partial or final text, we ask Jev one question: "Has the speaker finished what they wanted to say?" We put the last bot utterance into the state, because "yes" can be a complete answer only when it was preceded by a question.

  3. Suppose turn detection says "end," but Jev sees an unfinished thought in the text. The conclusions diverge, and in this case, we consider that the caller wants to continue the thought and do not pass the turn to the bot. If both decisions say "end," we can pass the utterance and start the response.

Supreet Tare from Taritas implemented the same mechanism in a production voice coaching project and described it in the article Semantic turn detection with Jev. In his case, the audio captures silence, and after 350 ms of silence, Jev receives one question. The answer comes in about 0.4 s. Then the code chooses how much longer to wait: 1.7 s if the thought is finished (probability from 0.75), 2.8 s if the answer is uncertain, and 4.5 s if the phrase breaks off at "and," "but," or a comma. It seems very slow, but in Supreet's trainer, it's more important not to miss anything important than to answer quickly.

Interruption, "hold on a sec," backchannel, and side remarks

Jay Albo analyzed an interesting case at Agora Conversational AI in the post Jev VAD, the code is in the repository AgoraIO-Community/convoai-jev-vad. We'll skip audio control and speech start detection by signal energy here. The caller starts speaking, and the text goes to Jev. One call returns four probabilities:

  • turn_complete: the caller has finished their thought, the turn can be passed. We've covered this already.

  • holding_floor: the caller is explicitly asking for a pause. "Wait, let me check..." More interesting.

  • addressed_to_agent: is this definitely said to the bot? "Hey, you there! Keep it down, I'm on the phone." This is the most curious detail, and we encountered it for the first time.

  • taking_floor: while the bot is speaking, is this a real interruption or a backchannel like "mm" and "uh-huh"? Some models can determine such incomplete phrases themselves, but generally, this behavior is inconsistent, and changing or updating the model can break the bot in this area.

In his sessions, the Jev call returned in about 300 ms.

How Jev parses data for a form

Booking an appointment, rescheduling, canceling a visit, inquiring about a bill. The domain is small, variability is limited, phrases are natural, and the caller brings their data, the specialist for booking, and the date and time of the visit all in one utterance. A large language model that also speaks is overkill here. What's needed is that Avatar: the classifier selects significant data from what the person said and passes it to the bot's code.

Jason Stiles set this up as a fictional clinic's phone line and described it in the review Jev IVR, updated on September 24, 2026. Stack: Node, Twilio ConversationRelay, Deepgram for recognition, ElevenLabs for voice, Jev for understanding.

The bot code maintains the form state, and each turn to Jev involves one request with about 30–35 questions.

Intent is determined by selection based on description. "Reschedule," "let's do another day," and "I need to change my appointment" fall into reschedule because the criteria describe what the person wants.

Structured data is trickier than entity extraction. The code itself finds candidates for data in the transcript: all parts resembling a number for the birth year, all parts of one to four words for the name. Jev chooses which part is the year and which is someone's name. The main advantage of a classifier here is that hallucinations are non-existent. Jev cannot invent a date; data errors always come from the caller's words. Confirmation always comes as a separate request: did the caller agree, refuse, or name the form field to be changed. It sounds old-fashioned, of course, but it works quickly and clearly.

Expensive answer or cheap one

The topic router from the first section chooses what to talk about. There's another choice: at what cost to answer this turn. For "yes," a postal code, or a short status, a cheap answer is possible. Confusion, instructions, a lengthy explanation, or a refusal are unlikely to be cheap.

Deepesh Jayal from Evalgent discussed this choice in the article Jev for Model Routing in Voice Agents. In his case, models are divided into three classes:

  1. A template with answers. Here, the model is not called; the answer is given by simple synthesis or a script.

  2. A fast model for a short factual answer.

  3. A heavy and expensive model for ambiguity and high error cost.

On each turn, Jev chooses how to answer, effectively answering the question "is an expensive answer needed?" Deepesh relies on ModelRouterMiddleware from the langchain-typesafe package: model selection criteria are written in natural language, probabilities remain in the agent's state. This post-call analysis shows why a turn went to an expensive model.

I would fix three rules that are worth adopting from this article:

  • Low confidence raises the choice to the next class, rather than leaving it on the cheap one.

  • A question with a high error cost does not fall into the cheap class, even if the classifier is confident that "the question is simple."
