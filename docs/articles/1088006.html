I've heard the opinion that education is slow to adopt AI use solely out of conservatism and fear of change. I would say the situation is actually more complex, and language models are a double-edged sword. But first, what I take as an axiom.

The goal of education is to ensure a given level of an agreed-upon set of skills in the learner. The skills can be basic (school) or not, relate to different areas of physical and/or mental labor (vocational school/university), but the goal doesn't change. Discussions about "learning to learn" or "forming thinking" upon closer examination boil down to the same thing – these are skills of mental labor.

And then this wonderful goal clashes with reality. Firstly, training usually needs to be conducted incrementally, from simple to complex. This is especially true when the level of education provided by the previous stage turns out to be very uneven and/or insufficient. Secondly, it is currently impossible to get inside a person's head and objectively assess their skill level (and this is not such a bad thing, considering the possible applications of such technology). This makes it necessary to use indirect assessments – for example, the ability to cope with learning tasks. Thirdly, education takes place under conditions of fierce competition for the time and attention of both the learner and the teacher. And the situation in recent years has been complicated by the introduction of both larger class/group sizes and an increasing number of ideologically charged disciplines.

This makes a gap in complexity between educational and real-world tasks inevitable – there is simply not enough time to work through real-world tasks, and one needs to reach this level gradually. And it is precisely into this gap that popular language models fall. On the one hand, they are not capable enough to independently solve real-world problems without careful supervision by a specialist. Worse, they are still not capable enough to assess the limits of their abilities, which manifests in hallucinations (though humans are not always better). But on the other hand, they are capable enough to crack educational tasks with ease, despite minor variations in conditions used to counter classic cheating.

From this, we can derive four possible scenarios for the learner's independent use of language models:

  • did not use, delved into – the ideal of classical education;

  • did not use, did not delve into – classic cheating, the methods of countering which are more or less worked out;

  • used, delved into – the ideal that proponents of AI in education talk about;

  • used, did not delve into – the crisis that critics of AI in education talk about.

This leads to the following factor: motivation. In other words, humans are lazy creatures, an evolutionary mechanism that is not so easy to suppress. Therefore, "delved/cheated" is a spectrum, the position on which will be individual for each person-discipline pair. It is determined by the balance of risks of each approach and the learner's motivation. And there is an opinion that the visible "omnipotence" of AI will negatively affect both risks (the chances that AI will solve the educational task better are higher) and motivation (why bother making an effort if there is a magic button?). Yes, the "used, delved into" scenario is quite possible – but in itself, it may not be widespread enough to rely on. And this is without touching upon the problem of the same hallucinations. From personal experience, LLMs start to spout nonsense with greater probability if the question is imprecise and incomplete – but to ask the right question, you need to know half the answer. Thus, LLMs begin to act as a kind of separator: motivated and smart students can effectively use AI tools, but those who neglect their studies will slide even lower. And modern education is a conveyor belt by necessity; the average result there is more important than the few best.

Thus, the intermediate conclusion is simple: if everything is left to chance, the risks of uncontrolled use of LLMs in education outweigh the benefits. But how to control it...

It is clear that teachers, to one degree or another, feel this pressure. In particular, let's consider the problem of using LLMs for post-processing texts. Firstly, consistently and competently formulating one's thoughts is also a skill, and it should not be neglected. Calligraphy has gone into the past, as the typewriter (and later, the printer) did not distort the meaning of the text. With LLMs, this is not the case; their output can differ in meaning from the original text. And if so, the skill needs to be developed – and this means independently completing learning tasks.

Secondly, text processed by LLM makes two scenarios – "used, delved into" and "used, did not delve into" – indistinguishable for the teacher when assessing work remotely. Well, except for cases where the student submits blatant garbage – but that's not the scenario one wants to strive for. Is it surprising that teachers automatically assume the worst of the two? Of course, one can ask: why not assess the work in person (defense of laboratory work)? And this would be the best option, but... see above about competition for time and attention. Often, a teacher may simply not have enough time to seriously examine each student for each piece of work. And no improvements are expected in this regard at all.

So, the requirement to write in one's own words (I consider the requirement to write by hand an extreme option, and not very useful) is, in essence, a local optimum that a teacher falls into when trying to quickly increase the engagement level of the least motivated students ("let them at least read what they are submitting"), even if it's at the expense of the most motivated ones.
