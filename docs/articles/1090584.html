How Avito's recommendation feed works

Recommendations on the homepage are the user's first point of contact with the platform. They account for about half of all ad views and about 30% of buyer-seller contacts.

Everything is set up as usual. When a user arrives:

1️⃣ We contact the candidate generation engines — each focused on a specific user signal — and from hundreds of millions of ads, we get a few thousand of the most relevant ones.

2️⃣ We enrich them with ad features and user features, then rank them.

3️⃣ We send the ranked results to the diversification layer, which provides the final variety of the feed. I wrote about this in the article "Neural network model of user interests".

Despite this, we hit three limitations:

🔴 "Saturation" effect. We tried to take orders of magnitude more data, but the quality did not increase.

🔴 Embeddings were not fully utilized. Many powerful embeddings for items and users appeared. But they cannot be fed directly into the model; they had to be compressed into dot-products and counters — this is suboptimal.

🔴 Manual features became a bottleneck. To feed a new signal into CatBoost, you need to generate many manual features. This is inconvenient, and here we lose part of the information again.

💡 Neural networks allow us to overcome some of these limitations, so we looked in their direction. The assumption is simple: there is a lot of data and good embeddings — we will train a model, and due to scaling, it will beat CatBoost. Spoiler alert: it worked, but not on the first try.

How we prepared data for the neural network

We took the CatBoost dataset as a basis — literally ad impressions on the homepage and the target, which is collected from actions after the impression. We immediately scaled it by 10 times: up to 10 million impressions. We believed that the potential of the neural network would only be revealed on large data.

Then comes what we didn't have to do with CatBoost: all preprocessing is under the hood for it, but for a neural network, features need to be prepared manually. This is a significant drawback.

Here's how we live with it:

Categorical features. For each feature, we find all possible unique values and map them to integer indices.

In the model, each feature looks into its own trainable embedding table using this index. We don't need hashing tricks: there are few categorical features, and each has low cardinality.

Numerical features. If we feed them in raw form, we will encounter different scales, tails, and outliers — hoping that the model will train a normalization layer under the hood and be robust to outliers is risky.

Therefore, we use quantile encoding: we pre-calculate 128 bins by quantiles for each feature, and when a new value arrives, we see which bins it falls between and calculate the weight — how close it is to the left or right bin.

In the dataset, we store a simple triplet: the index of the left bin, the index of the right bin, and the weight. Inside the model, we interpolate the bin number and normalize it by the number of bins for that feature. This results in a number from 0 to 1, while maintaining a monotonic relationship with the original feature: as the feature increases, the encoded value increases.

Embeddings. Storing raw vectors in the dataset is costly. Here, the observation that the same item repeats between outputs — it can appear in tens and hundreds of thousands — comes in handy.

Therefore, we move the vectors of unique items to a separate memmap array, and in the dataset, we only store indices. In the model, for each version of the embedding, we use its own projection: there are many versions, and a projection is needed to bring everything to a single dimension and a single space.

Five iterations from MVP to production

Model #1. Quick launch. We decided that in 2026, there was no point in implementing everything ourselves, and asked an AI agent to draft the first version. It immediately implemented complex feature encoding and brought in DCNv2 with a mixture of experts and low-rank approximations. We trained it on binary cross-entropy — the results were terrible.

The model significantly underperformed CatBoost, and due to the initial complexity, we couldn't figure out what went wrong: was the problem in preprocessing or in the model? There was no way to assess the contribution of each component.

However, at the first stage, not the model quality was more important for us — it would have been difficult to immediately outperform CatBoost, which had been developed for years — but the ability to apply it online.

For the first experiments, we used ONNX Runtime directly within our Go service and realized that we couldn't score all 5,000 candidates with all features and embeddings using a neural network.

Thus, the scheme that we still use today appeared: CatBoost works on L2 and provides the top-300 best items, and the neural ranker on L3 reorders them within this top.

We use early binding of candidates and features — in our experience, it is significantly more powerful than late binding, which we use in other models. But early binding has a price: it's impossible to calculate it for a very large pool of candidates, so the neural ranker only works with the top-300, which CatBoost pre-selected.

This also affected latency: model inference added about 30 ms — not much. Later, we moved the model to Triton on GPU: inference is faster there, although there are overheads for transferring tensors over the network. In total, across the entire pipeline, this added about 6% to latency.

🏁 The first A/B test showed expected results:

  • −3% NDCG offline

  • −6% users with contacts from recommendations online

However, we tested the production environment, fixed integration bugs, got the first baseline, and started improving it.

Model #2. Removed unnecessary complexity. We started with the simplest possible architecture: numerical and categorical features, and on top of them — a standard MLP. On our data, such a model performed better than the more complex architecture from the previous iteration.

At the same time, we changed the loss. Binary cross-entropy optimizes the score of each candidate separately, but in recommendations, the score of a specific candidate is not as important as the final order of the entire output. Therefore, we switched to a listwise loss. In articles, it is found under the name ListMLE.

The idea is simple: sort candidates by target value in descending order. In the first step, the model should choose the best item, in the second — the best among the remaining, and so on. This can be represented as sequential cross-entropy: at each step, softmax defines the probability distribution over the not-yet-selected candidates. At the same time, the loss itself is calculated without iterations — we pre-build a triangular mask of the remaining items and calculate all softmax steps in parallel.

We still use this loss in production today. If you are working on similar tasks, use it at least as a baseline.

🏁 Results of the second A/B test:

  • −1.5% NDCG

  • −3% users with contacts

We were still significantly losing, but offline improved — and online followed suit. An important step for us: this means that hypotheses can be tested offline, and they need to be tested sequentially.

Model #3. Integrated embeddings. We supported projection for each embedding version and designed a pairwise interaction layer, within which we calculated all possible dot-products of the vectors that entered it.

The calculation was based on the assumption that the model would learn projections such that these products — for example, between different versions of user and item embeddings — would become meaningful and carry a relevance signal.

🏁 And this worked quite well:

  • Offline, the model outperformed CatBoost for the first time: +0.7% NDCG

  • The target metric — the number of users with contacts — fell again, by 1.8%

  • Positive signal: the number of users with target actions, which include, in addition to contacts, for example, adding to favorites, increased by 0.7%

At this point, we could have gotten discouraged and decided that offline doesn't match online — but it turned out to be simpler.

🏁 We look at offline metrics in business slices:

  • −1.9% NDCG for contacts

  • +0.8% for target actions

We are still ranking contacts worse than CatBoost, and the total metric is growing due to better ranking of additions to favorites — there are many more of them in the dataset.

Conclusion: look at metrics in slices, otherwise the total will mask the problem with the main business metric.

Model #4. Balanced contacts. It turned out that if we remove the clever pairwise interaction layer and feed the embeddings directly into the head, the total quality does not improve, but the slice for contacts increases.

Additionally, we introduced weights into the loss that penalize incorrect ranking of contacts more strongly. It didn't become a game-changer, but it slightly improved offline balance metrics.

🏁 In the fourth A/B test, the model finally matched CatBoost:

  • −0.6% users with contacts

  • +0.6% with target actions

Contacts entered the gray zone. We built a neural network, but there was no significant quality jump.

In the same A/B test, we checked another approach: the same model, but in the top-300, we mixed the ranks of CatBoost and the neural ranker to get the final ranking. This is a well-known technique from competitions: models of different natures compensate for each other's errors well, and when blending, the overall quality usually increases.

🏁 Metrics improved:

  • +0.7% users with contacts

  • +0.9% users with target actions from recommendations

  • +0.4% users with target actions across all of Avito

The result is modest, but we decided to roll out such a change. It also had an important engineering aspect. We consciously did not adjust the coefficients — we wanted to maintain the contribution of each model equally, otherwise, there would be a strong dependency on specific versions. But this way, CatBoost and the neural ranker can be developed independently: you improve either of them — you immediately see a product effect, and successful ideas for features and datasets are transferred from one model to another. And all this time, we have a stable production option.

Model #5. Added user history to the transformer. User history is probably the key signal for recommendations. The same item can be both good and bad depending on what the person has recently looked at and what they are interested in. And now we have a neural network — we can feed it a sequence of events and get a representation of each candidate that depends on the history.

First, we filtered the history down to relevant target actions and, as a baseline, limited its capacity to 50 events. We didn't add clicks: they could have displaced more significant user intent signals from the history.

Each event in the history is associated with a specific item. For example, a user might have contacted about a specific dog ad. For such an event, we have item embeddings, information about its position in the history, and additional features. For example, the type of event and the source of transition: recommendations, search, or external traffic.

We bring all these features to a common size and sum them up: projections of item embeddings, positional embedding, and trainable embeddings of event fields. As a result, each event is represented by a single vector.

For candidates, we only use item embeddings: they do not have an event type, source, or position in the user's history.

💡 Next is the trick that makes the whole scheme work. We concatenate the history and candidates into one common input for the transformer and, after processing it, get new hidden states for the candidates, already conditioned on the user's history. This allows us not to run the transformer for each candidate separately — one pass for the entire output is enough.

A separate interesting point is attention. To get a candidate representation that depends on history, we allowed candidates to look at the history. But we noticed that if we allow candidates to look at each other as well, the model's quality also increases — by about 0.6%.

Now the prediction for each candidate depends not only on itself but also on the competitive context: what other ads are in the output, among which alternatives the model chooses. There is no magic here — it's standard self-attention with a mask.

The new representation of candidates, which are aware of the history, goes into a parallel version of DCN, and the model is trained on the same loss — ListMLE.

The model's scale is currently modest — about 2.5 million parameters. We are not planning to scale up to hundreds of millions in the next six months: first, we want to get the most out of the architecture.

What we achieved online

In offline metrics, we saw strong growth:

  • +7.2% NDCG for target actions

  • +4.9% for contacts

This translated into online results:

  • +4.3% users with contacts from recommendations

  • +3.3% users with target actions

  • +0.8% users who made a transaction

The latter is the most important. The effect has translated into Avito's overall metric. This is one of the most effective changes in recommendations in recent years.

Three takeaways you can use

💡 Neural networks provide a noticeable effect if prepared correctly. You will have to scale the dataset, configure features, select the loss, and adapt the architecture to your data — there is more manual work here than with CatBoost.

💡 Offline metrics should lead to online results. This is especially important for neural networks: they are flexible, so there are many degrees of freedom and hypotheses that you want to test. It is impossible to test all hypotheses online. Therefore, in the early stages, it is important to understand whether there is a match between offline and online — and if not, to delve into the datasets and metric specifics.

💡 It's better to start with simple ideas. This leads to a better understanding of how the technology is developing. And, most likely, an overly complex solution will not reach production where the same task can be solved more simply.

The entire article in brief

CatBoost hit a ceiling: more data stopped providing growth, and embeddings and new signals required more and more manual work.

The transition to neural networks had to be gradual: from a simple MLP and ListMLE to embeddings, blending with CatBoost, and a full-fledged neural ranker.

The main leap was provided by user history: the last 50 target events along with candidates are fed into the transformer, and attention considers both history and the context of the output.

Online, the model resulted in +4.3% users with contacts from recommendations and +0.8% users who made a transaction.
