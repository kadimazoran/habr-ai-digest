A couple of months later, it turns out it's barely used: employees still go to external chatbots and pay for personal subscriptions to solve work tasks. It would seem that this is intentional – work gets done with AI – but it's not certain that sensitive information isn't leaking to external systems.

We have been working with AI within the company for a long time and help our clients implement it. We have seen various approaches to deploying corporate AI services in action and have tried them ourselves. In this article, we will share what from our experience works and what doesn't when implementing internal tools.

Why Companies Need Corporate AI

Typically, when implementing AI, businesses pursue three goals: increase employee productivity (because it can no longer be increased without AI), ensure the security of sensitive data (a concern for every large organization), and maintain the image of a technologically advanced company (for reputation and recruitment purposes).

In practice, the result often falls short of expectations: money is invested in the tool, infrastructure is created, but only a handful of people use it, and they are dissatisfied. Productivity does not increase, and security personnel are forced to watch as corporate data and code go to external services.

How to do it "right"? You need to provide employees with a tool they will want to use themselves. In essence, this is not a technological but a product task, where the product is an internal AI service. A controlled tool reduces concerns about data leaks, and if it helps with work, there will be no motivation to take tasks to external AI.

Why Bans Don't Work

Most often, the main motive for banning external AI is data security. If security personnel are tasked with "reducing the risk of leaks," the easiest way is to simply block access to external AI services.

But all the tasks that an employee wanted to solve with AI will not disappear. Therefore, employees work on their personal laptops or share internet from their mobile phones, and the company loses control over the data being sent.

What usually happens after a strict ban on AI is introduced:

  • there is no productivity increase from AI implementation or it is uneven, the company begins to lag behind competitors;

  • leakage risks still remain: people go to external services and sometimes send personal data, confidential information, documents marked "for internal use only," and information under NDA;

  • the effect of AI in work cannot be measured: the company does not see what the employee did, which service they used, and what task they solved;

  • and if there are penalties for circumventing the ban, employees see the company as a disloyal employer and start looking at the job market more actively.

There are industries where a strict ban is justified: the cost of error is high, and legal requirements or job descriptions apply. For example, medicine and industrial safety. In IT development, such stakes are less common, so the following works better:

  • explain the risks and concerns to employees;

  • clearly state in the rules what is allowed and what is not;

  • provide a convenient, permitted tool for work.

In short, the well-known principle literally works here: "If you can't ban it, lead it."

What goal does a ban usually pursue? Work data should not leave the company without control. This goal can be achieved without banning external services.

Not every request to an "external" AI automatically violates security policy. The fact of use, violation of rules, and leakage of confidential data are three different events of varying significance.

Much depends on the rules. Some companies do not officially prohibit personal requests to external services if they do not contain any corporate information. If the company specifies in its rules what is permitted, an employee has no reason to look for workarounds for a harmless request.

However, not all companies formalize these rules in official documents. In the summer of 2026, we conducted a survey with Habr: 31% of participants reported that their company has no rules for working with data in AI.

The shift to external services directly depends on how developed the corporate tool is. We call this the "maturity of internal AI." According to our internal estimates, if there is no mature corporate tool, almost all developers and 70-80% of other employees use external services for work.

But! When a company provides teams with a mature tool for solving tasks, this percentage drops sharply and can even reach zero.

What Happens if the Internal Tool is Immature

Most often, an employee turns to an external service for one of two reasons: the corporate model is weaker than the external one, or the corporate tool lacks a necessary function.

Here are three cases from our practice. In each, the corporate tool at the time could not do what employees needed.

  • Spreadsheets and Agents. A colleague from content marketing needed to analyze a table of article views by topic list and identify trends. It was most convenient to do this in Perplexity with agents: agents were not yet available in the internal environment. There is no violation here because article views on platforms are publicly available. Later, the company added agents to the corporate tool.

  • Video. Video editors used AI to create an assistant that cuts a video into meaningful segments, ensures the face stays in frame, and prepares subtitles. They built it outside the corporate environment: the corporate model only worked with text at the time. When the assistant became known, the module that prepares visuals for presentations was moved to the corporate tool. Editing functions remained in specialized video programs.

  • Photos. Speakers need photos in the corporate style for event announcements, and it's difficult to gather employees from different cities for a photoshoot. Employees uploaded their photos to an external service and received portraits in corporate hoodies on a white background. Consent for processing personal data – full name and photograph – is requested from speakers in advance.

How to Give Employees Powerful Models

What will work better than a ban? Convenience. If a legal, permitted tool solves tasks no worse than external services, an employee doesn't need to circumvent prohibitions and will switch to the permitted tool themselves.

  • The model handles work tasks. If the model underlying the corporate service requires more control and doesn't save time, employees will continue to use external services even to bypass prohibitions.

  • Built-in integrations with corporate systems. The tool is initially connected to corporate systems, and the employee doesn't need to configure them themselves.

For example, we built a corporate MCP server for developers with ready-made integrations with the task tracker, knowledge base, and documentation of our services. The tool itself finds the necessary information based on requests. We also maintain a unified database of verified skills.

  • Easy access. An employee installs the tool with one click from the catalog of permitted software, and all connections and models are already configured.

Companies take two paths in this regard.

The first path is a controlled AI service. The company deploys open models on its own infrastructure or connects them through a provider within the country. This approach provides more control over the location of request processing and data access. The model's placement in Russia or on its own infrastructure does not guarantee complete protection: it depends on access settings, logging, and connected integrations.

Another advantage of self-deployment is less dependence on an external provider. If the provider suspends the organization's account service, the team may lose access to the service and the data and settings stored within it. Migrating workflows to another infrastructure will take time; its complexity depends, among other things, on the ability to export data and settings.

The second path is to establish corporate rules for accessing external AI models and services. To reduce leakage risks, a gateway with masking is used: the employee works in an application connected to the gateway, which, before sending the request, replaces sensitive data found with placeholders and restores the original values instead of these placeholders in the model's response. Masking itself does not negate legal requirements, provider terms, or internal company rules.

What is in the employee's request:
Call the client back at +7 900 000-00-00 and duplicate the email to ivanov@example.com.

What goes to the model:
Call the client back at <PHONE_1> and duplicate the email to <EMAIL_1>.

Agreed access can be supported by full or partial payment for selected AI tools. However, a subscription to a user service and access to a model via API are different methods of connection and payment. Guardrails Filter works with requests directed through it: paying for a personal subscription does not automatically connect the provider's regular website to the gateway. To work through the filter, a compatible client or a separate integration is needed. In our practice, such a carrot works better than a stick.

Guardrails Filter masks data detected according to enabled rules: for example, phone numbers, email addresses, passwords, API keys, and tokens. Rules are based on templates; they do not guarantee the detection of all sensitive information.

In the open version of Guardrails Filter, you can add your own rules – for example, for internal contract numbers, client identifiers, or project codenames. Masking individual values does not automatically hide program logic. Even renaming variables does not protect algorithms, structure, or other essential code details.

For additional protection, companies need to define what information is prohibited from being transmitted to external AI services, train employees on these rules, and reinforce them with technical measures: access control, control of transmission channels, and limitation of available services.

What to Formalize in Documents

Control and responsibility work only if they are formalized in documents and the employee is aware of them. Most often, these are documents that employees sign upon hiring:

  • a list of information constituting commercial secrets. According to the "On Commercial Secrets" law, the employee must be familiarized with it in writing; without this, it is difficult to hold them accountable for disclosure;

  • a policy on working with AI services: which services are permitted, what data cannot be sent to them, and how the company monitors traffic on corporate devices;

  • a personal data processing policy. If personal data is sent to a foreign service, it is cross-border transfer, which has its own requirements under the "On Personal Data" law.

For disclosing commercial secrets or another employee's personal data, the Labor Code allows for the termination of employment. The procedure for monitoring and the wording of documents should be coordinated with legal counsel, the IT security department, and HR.

How to Organize Training

In large companies, compliance risk courses are regularly held for employees, covering topics like not calling friends abroad from a corporate number or not printing "War and Peace" on a corporate printer.

It's similar with AI: colleagues need to be regularly reminded that requests should not contain personal or confidential data, and sending customer information to neural networks is strictly prohibited. This needs to be communicated broadly, frequently, and through various channels: in courses, newsletters, messengers, and so on.

A warning in the AI tool's interface also helps, prominently displayed in red letters: "Before sending, make sure the request does not contain personal data or trade secrets!"

Not Everything Needs to Be Kept Internal

Internal AI use requires a significant budget. Departments consume many tokens, and the company pays for them to the provider or with its own computing resources. Therefore, not every task must be solved within internal services; some are more reasonably outsourced to external services if confidential data is not sent to them.

An example from our practice: a sandbox. We allocated resource quotas to departments in a sandbox where employees could legally experiment and develop their projects. About a year later, internal consumption grew so much that the sandbox had to be limited: resources were needed for more important internal products.

By then, some employees were already using their own projects in their work. Now, such a project can be registered and company resources allocated to it. Registration is intentionally not simple: it is for projects that affect company performance indicators.

How to Understand if AI is Bringing Value

The first thing that comes to mind when trying to quantify the impact of AI implementation is counting consumed tokens. However, this metric alone does not show whether these consumed tokens helped solve the company's business tasks or if employees were simply "learning" to work with the new tool.

Therefore, tokens can be counted if the company decides in advance what value they represent: investment "in employee training" or financial "more gained than spent."

Measuring AI effectiveness by story points is also unreliable: if a manager knows a task will be done with AI, they start underestimating its complexity, leading to "story point inflation." What would have been a complex rework without AI is now assessed as medium or even simple with AI.

The industry has not yet come up with a single metric, so measurement varies depending on the department:

Production Departments: Development, Testing, Product Design

In production departments, quantitative metrics can be used – the number of merge requests and autotests before and after implementation. In our pilot, the number of merge requests approximately doubled, but this doesn't yet indicate accelerated development.

A qualitative method is in-depth interviews: employees explain where exactly AI helps and what hinders them. This is how we analyzed a pilot with 27 developers; details are in the article "AI-adoption: Where the Development Boost is Hidden".

Moreover, agents did not necessarily ensure increased efficiency. Yes, agents generate more code, but this code needs to be reviewed, and developers become the bottleneck in the review process. It's physically impossible to keep up with agents, so some developers even returned from agents to the chat they used for discussing tasks and finding information.

Back Office: Accounting, Legal Department

Here, AI most often takes over routine operations. To measure efficiency, you need to look at which operations employees use AI for and then calculate the result: the number of approved contracts, the speed of payments, saved man-hours.

For example, if a process requires about a thousand manual operations per month and an agent handles some of them, the difference will be obvious in the numbers.

Front Office: Sales, Marketing, Customer Service

Here, you can compare the volume of prepared materials before and after AI implementation, as well as the time spent on typical operations.

In content marketing, we did this: one person takes two tasks of comparable complexity, does one with AI and the other without, and records the time spent on each stage. The goal of such measurement is to find stages where AI provides the greatest benefit with the least loss of quality.

In our case, such a measurement showed that source finding accelerated twofold. At the same time, verifying the result took 75% more time. On average, the time from idea to text publication decreased by 13-35%.

As a result, we outsourced brainstorming, outline planning, and source finding to AI, but kept drafting, fact-checking, and final editing for humans.

Where to Start

If your company is just beginning to regulate AI work, here is the recommended order based on our experience:

  1. Decide how you will measure the impact and in which areas of work.

  2. Write the rules: what can be done in external services and what data cannot be sent there.

  3. Formalize the rules in employment contracts and company internal regulations and have employees sign them.

  4. Ask employees what tasks they outsource to external services.

  5. Analyze the risks with the IT security department and choose a path: models under your control or a gateway with data masking.

  6. Allocate a product team to develop the corporate tool and bring integrations to fruition.

  7. Start with a pilot for a limited audience and scale up after it.

  8. Explain to employees which tool to use for which task and update training materials and guides as the internal tool's functions are updated.
