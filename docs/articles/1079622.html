Introduction

In this not-so-serious post, I'll try to tackle a very serious question: what makes the ideal laptop for an ML developer.

Here are a few basic requirements:

  • sufficiently powerful hardware to experiment with neural networks and other ML tasks locally;

  • a compact chassis and low weight, so you can carry the laptop between home and office several times a week without much suffering, take it on trips and business travel;

  • decent battery life — not necessarily a full workday, but at least enough to work for a few hours in a cafe, on transport, or in a coworking space without constantly looking for an outlet;

  • the ability to work with NVIDIA CUDA, as it remains the standard for most research ML projects and is necessary for my work;

  • good build quality and generally pleasant hardware that you want to use for several years, not replace at the first opportunity.

For several years, a MacBook Pro with M1 served as such a universal computer for me. But gradually its limitations began to outweigh its advantages, and in the end, I decided to try switching to an x86 laptop with Linux.

Below, I'll explain what exactly I stopped being satisfied with in the MacBook, how scary Linux is in 2026, what's wrong with the Thinkpad P1, and why I ultimately settled on the rather unusual ASUS ROG Flow Z13.

MacBook — a Great Laptop, But Not the Best Choice

I used a MacBook Pro with M1 for about three years, and overall, it's a great laptop. macOS is beautiful and very user-friendly. The system doesn't have as many hidden and hacky menus as Windows. It's very similar to Linux in many ways, making it convenient for command-line work. Excellent integration with other Apple devices, which are likely to appear if you already have a Mac :). The computer sleeps stably — perhaps with a couple of exceptions per year — and always wakes up quickly. It has some of the best trackpads and keyboards, and excellent build quality.

First and foremost, the MacBook is a versatile device that suits many people. It's especially good for those who don't want to waste time configuring the system and expect their computer to just work. The MacBook truly excels at this. But precisely because of this, technically inclined individuals might eventually find it a bit boring and limiting. The system and hardware are designed so that the user rarely thinks about what's happening inside, but along with convenience, this also brings a number of limitations. Below are a few examples from my own experience. The list is very selective, but these are the points that gradually pushed me to eventually sell my MacBook.

macOS

Despite all the advantages of macOS, it has its drawbacks. I could never get used to the fact that closing the last window doesn't quit the application: it continues to run, and a dot remains under the icon in the Dock. To fully close a program, you need to explicitly select the quit command or press Command-Q. Window management doesn't always seem intuitive or convenient. Stability and predictability are usually cited as major advantages of macOS, but, for example, in Tahoe, the familiar app grid in Launchpad was simply removed, leaving users no choice. In an operating system tailored for a closed ecosystem, it's generally easier for the manufacturer to dictate their vision of how things should work to users.

Ecosystem

Apple strongly ties users to its ecosystem — this is a well-known fact. With current payment issues, it's more of a bug than a feature. Of course, there's nothing stopping you from using Yandex Disk alongside iCloud or instead of it. But in that case, you're simply overpaying for services you don't use.

Pre-installed applications are a separate story. Without sufficient iCloud storage, many of them lose their appeal sharply: apps for books, documents, and photos stop syncing properly between Apple devices. Moreover, even with cloud access, many of them turn out to be not particularly practical. The initial pleasant impression gradually gives way to disappointment. For example, I haven't met a single acquaintance who regularly and fully uses Numbers instead of Excel or Google Sheets — although I myself honestly tried. The same applies to Pages instead of Word, FaceTime instead of Zoom, Google Meet, and other conferencing apps, Safari instead of Chrome, Apple Maps instead of Google Maps or Yandex Maps, Siri instead of ChatGPT (this might change soon). I've listed only the most noticeable examples, and there's a whole bunch of equally beautiful but ultimately useless apps that can't be deleted. Thank Apple at least for not installing a mountain of third-party software with the system, but in the end, there were only a few genuinely useful native apps for me personally. This is largely a consequence of the desire to draw the user deeper into their own ecosystem.

In the modern context, it seems to me, it's becoming increasingly useful to be able to independently build a reliable environment of tools that are minimally dependent on external factors, and to control at least the most important part of your data: personal photos, favorite books, music, and notes. This skill also needs to be trained. Searching for and testing tools and services, of course, takes time and effort. But the first step can be taken naturally: break the usual ties of the Apple ecosystem and, for example, stop buying gift cards at double the price just to pay for iCloud. Overall, I could still tolerate all of the above. So, let's move on to more serious developer arguments.

Linux Compatibility

In machine learning, Linux is everywhere: on servers, robots, and small computing boards. macOS inevitably imposes additional limitations in such an environment. For example, Docker runs through virtualization, not natively, which leads to overhead and other restrictions. In modern versions of Docker Desktop, host networking mode with TCP and UDP support has appeared, but it still doesn't work like native Linux networking: the container doesn't get direct access to the host's network interfaces, and low-level protocols are not supported. Therefore, ROS2 in a container on macOS, especially discovery and multicast when working with physical devices, requires additional configuration and can work unstably. There are also hardware limitations. A Linux container on macOS runs inside a virtual machine, while physical hardware is managed by macOS drivers and APIs. Therefore, to access USB devices, you need to configure separate port forwarding or a software bridge. There are also differences in file system handling. macOS doesn't natively support ext4, so you usually can't simply mount and view a Linux system partition or a Linux-formatted memory card without third-party drivers or a separate virtual machine. On a Linux laptop, you can open such a partition directly and modify system files if necessary. In general, many tools, especially new research ML projects, appear and are tested first on Linux. Therefore, instructions that work there "out of the box" often require additional steps on macOS. My example is the popular sparse matrix library spconv. Without CUDA on board, I'd like to be able to at least run the code in CPU-only mode to debug pipelines. But even this scenario is not properly supported on macOS: the project is heavily tied to Linux-specific dependencies and a C++ environment.

x86 Compatibility

There are still limitations with x86 programs on Apple Silicon. Most of them run through Rosetta, but not all. If you need to test low-level code, drivers, or applications that use specific CPU instructions, problems may arise. One could reasonably object here: but Mac offers native ARM architecture support. I still find an x86 laptop more convenient. ARM devices are typically compact with a rather specific software stack: Jetson and Raspberry Pi with their operating systems, drivers, and other peculiarities. You'll likely still need to test software directly on the target device, and if necessary, you can take the device with you. But you can't put an industrial x86 computer with a full desktop graphics card in a backpack. For those who want to work with a wide range of x86 devices, it will generally offer more freedom and mobility than ARM.

External GPUs, CUDA, ROCm

I don't think I need to explain in detail why CUDA is important for those who like to experiment with ML. It's not available on MacBook, nor is the ability to connect an external NVIDIA graphics card. At the same time, Mac has excellent hardware and an actively developing ecosystem around Metal. You can run local text generation models and AI image editing on it; Ollama, for instance, is well-optimized for Apple Silicon. But at the development level, the limitations become more noticeable. Despite the almost seamless switching of PyTorch from CUDA to MPS, you too often encounter specific CUDA optimizations, individual models, or entire projects designed specifically for NVIDIA. You can certainly build and even train something of your own in ML on a Mac — I've done it. But if you want to test code from another article, look into work-related things, or simply significantly speed up computations, you'll likely still have to go to a server or a computer with Linux and NVIDIA. A recent example from work: we have a compact object detection model, and to allow colleagues with MacBooks to run it locally, we had to additionally save Torch JIT files because ONNX somehow didn't convert for Metal. And finally, you can connect an AMD external graphics card to Linux. Such cards are already used for training models via ROCm, but compatibility, both in terms of hardware and research projects, still significantly lags behind the CUDA ecosystem.

Battery Life

Battery life is one of the killer features of MacBook, and it's something I really like too. When browsing websites, my MacBook Pro with M1 consumed about 5W from the battery, while a ThinkPad with an Intel Core i7 12th Gen consumed about 15-17W even in power-saving mode. However, modern Intel and AMD processors with power-efficient cores have significantly narrowed this gap. Under light load, many current laptops consume about 5-8W from the battery. This is already quite comparable to a MacBook and allows for about 7-9 hours of light work away from an outlet. In most scenarios, if you don't need to work in the field, more battery life isn't required. More details on the battery life of specific models will follow.

Upgradeability

I won't even talk about the possibility of upgrading RAM. Given how quickly hardware is developing, the need to replace the laptop itself will likely come sooner than the desire to increase RAM — especially if the old device can then be sold for about half its original price. But on a MacBook, you can't even replace the SSD! And that seems quite sad.

Windows as a Second OS

This is quite simple: by installing Windows as a second OS, you can get an almost universal device capable of running almost anything. Yes, you'll have to reboot to switch between Linux and Windows. But it's still a good way to access that one essential application that isn't available on Linux, without giving up Linux as your primary operating system.

Linux is Now for Everyone!

In the AI era, Linux no longer causes as much headache as it used to. I'm referring to all the typical Linux fuss with system configuration via the command line, driver installation, and specific software. Being the most open system for developers, Linux traditionally lacks the visual polish that macOS is generously covered with. Many settings that can be changed through neat menus on a Mac still need to be done through the terminal, configuration files, and system services in Linux. And here, perhaps, lies one of the main insights of this article, which I realized after switching to Linux: armed with even a not-so-powerful AI, you stop fearing this OS as much as before. Many issues with driver installation, system settings, and fixing incompatibilities are now resolved not by compiling applications from source code and studying command languages and configuration files, but through a normal dialogue with a personal artificial "computer master." As a result, instead of a semi-closed but meticulously polished macOS, you get an interesting alternative: a maximally open system plus an AI assistant that helps manage it.

How to Take the First Step

Two questions to help you decide:

1. Do you even need to run code on your own machine? If you do all your work on a remote server, and your side projects never descend from cloud servers into the physical world, then a laptop that functions as a typewriter, whose main task is to connect to various hosts and run code there, is quite sufficient. In this case, the MacBook probably remains one of the best options. Moreover, most people will be fine with an Air, and a Pro makes sense if you want to run local neural networks or need its other hardware advantages.

2. If the answer to the first question is "yes" and you need powerful and versatile hardware for work and hobbies, you should carefully go through all the applications you use on your Mac. Perhaps there are no indispensable Mac-only applications among them? If you find at least a couple of programs you absolutely cannot do without, parting with your MacBook might be too painful. And that's okay. However, for some, the option of Linux as the primary system and Windows as a secondary OS might be suitable — specifically for one or two needed applications.

If the answers to both questions are positive and your development involves a wide software stack, then it's time to switch to x86+Linux!

Here's my example. My work stack (Linux / ROS 2 / CUDA) requires regular running of projects on local machines (ROS 2 on a remote server is a separate challenge). Since the company allows working on devices other than corporate ones, I wanted a powerful yet mobile computer to work from home two to three times a week, take it to the office, and work on my personal projects simultaneously. Of course, there was an option to keep the MacBook and set up a small Linux server at home with a good desktop graphics card. But working on a remote machine isn't always convenient, especially when you need a graphical interface or direct hardware access. There might be connection issues during travel, the computer itself would take up space at home, and the "good laptop + powerful desktop" combination also ends up being not cheap. So, I simply went through the applications I used on my Mac and found that I was no longer doing almost anything on it except programming. DaVinci Resolve, CapCut, and Lightroom had been gathering dust for a long time: it turned out to be easier to process small videos and photos for social media directly on my smartphone. I only regretted buying RetouchMe a bit — a very good AI-powered retouching program. It did its job well and worked quite fast on the M1 GPU. For its sake alone, theoretically, I could have kept Windows as a second OS, but by that time, I was barely using it either. And the problems with iCloud payments finally made the decision much easier.

Which x86 Laptop to Choose

After several years of using a MacBook, the PC world seems like the Wild West, where it's easy to get lost. But if you think about it, not many options remain.

Linux Compatibility

This is the most important and logical criterion. You can find information online about how much each manufacturer cares about potential Linux users. Models from Dell (Latitude, Precision, XPS Developer Edition), Lenovo (ThinkPad, some ThinkBook), Framework, and System76 are certified for Linux. These are the models recommended for installing Linux. In practice, certification implies a more stable BIOS: all keys and computer control functions should work out of the box, the laptop should sleep and wake correctly, and power management and fans should be controlled properly. Spoiler: the issue with Linux certification remained the most controversial. Based on my experience, I haven't noticed a big difference, as I'll explain below.

Onboard Graphics Card

The second important criterion is having computational power for AI work. I identify two particularly interesting options:

  • Discrete NVIDIA graphics card. It's powerful, allows working with CUDA, but the memory is limited: usually, you can expect 8–16 GB. Additionally, it consumes a lot of power. On compatible laptops, Ampere and newer GPUs can automatically completely shut down when idle, reducing their power consumption to almost zero. This allows combining, for example, an energy-efficient Intel Core Ultra with NVIDIA's power in a thin and compact chassis;

  • Integrated graphics with unified memory. Currently, this primarily means AMD Ryzen AI Max+ 395: up to 128 GB of unified memory, performance on par with a laptop RTX 4060. No CUDA, but you can connect an external NVIDIA graphics card. Working in the same memory with the processor allows for more flexible graphics management. For example, simply opening a container with a passed-through NVIDIA GPU won't let it sleep, which will keep the laptop fans spinning even if no calculations are being performed. Integrated graphics, on the other hand, will calmly go to sleep until needed, preserving model weights in video memory.

I managed to test both of these ideas on two laptops. This is an extremely limited sample, but as I'm not a tech blogger, I don't have the opportunity to test more hardware :(.

Real-World Experience

Lenovo ThinkPad P1 Gen 5

A brief overview of this device, which brought more disappointment than fulfilled hopes. I got a configuration with an NVIDIA RTX A3000 and 64 GB of RAM on Avito for 120,000 rubles. I really wanted the Gen 7, but didn't want to spend more on an experiment.

Good points: it's truly thin and light for a 16-inch screen, a great keyboard, and the soft-touch body coating doesn't cut into my palms. After careful configuration, the graphics card started going into sleep mode when idle, and the laptop's total battery consumption dropped to 15W (it has an Intel Core 12th Gen processor, not Core Ultra). As soon as the card wakes up, for example, after the nvidia-smi command, consumption immediately increases to 25W. After 2-3 seconds of inactivity, it drops again. Everything is precise!
