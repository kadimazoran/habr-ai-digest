In what form does an LLM conversation appear to the user? What determines the form of this appearance? This question has been experimentally investigated by many – see the bibliography below.

In the theory of LLM conversations as a new type of Luhmannian semantic system (full description: (Krupkin, 2026)), the interaction of an LLM conversation with a user can be described by the standard social interaction scheme – the Ego-Alter pair. Ego is the form in which the LLM conversation appears to the user. Alter is its model of the user as an interaction partner. A review of experiments with LLMs / noo-spheres regarding Alter was presented earlier. This text is dedicated to a review of LLM / noo-sphere modeling of their Ego.

Ego, Roles, Masks, and Their Traits in LLM Conversations

Let's ask an LLM / noo-sphere to explain a solution to a problem, and then check if it has been understood. In the first case, we offered the LLM conversation the social role of a tutor; in the second, an examiner. A tutor can appear as a patient mentor or a dry pedant – actualizing different masks. Masks differ in traits – both of "character" and of adopted speech style. In general: noo-spheres act in dialogues like humans, which stimulates the use of social psychology tools in Ego experiments:

What is it?

Examples

Role

Assistant, tutor, editor, reviewer

Ironic skeptic, dry pedant

In the context of LLM conversations, the default role and mask is that of a formally benevolent assistant, open to help requested by the task. Further interaction with the LLM conversation can shift its Ego configuration. One can also ask the noo-sphere in the task to directly adopt a different role + mask, alternative to the assistant's "personality," and see what happens. This is precisely how the majority of experiments found in the literature are implemented.

The developing theory of LLM conversations (Krupkin, 2026) allows working directly with these invariants extracted from behavior – as forms of an emergent i-layer over the LLM substrate – the LLM software complex implemented on data processing servers. The i-layer is realized by structured sets of numbers that define the state of the LLM conversation in the LLM substrate, and change during processing in a cycle between the prompt and the response, and then also between cycles – after responding to the previous one and awaiting a new prompt that continues the dialogue. These numbers, actually programmed for calculation and change in the LLM substrate software complex, can be represented as being contained in a separate container that changes during the course of the LLM conversation. Different configurations of these container numbers turn out to be invariant during their processing in the LLM substrate, and are associated with different behavioral characteristics of LLM conversations – forms of the i-layer.

In light of the developing theory, such behavioral elements, forms, and structures of the i-layer are not only discovered behaviorally but are also grouped into 18 classes, of which 17 classes were defined in (Krupkin, 2026: Table 2), and an additional affective class was introduced later. In this representation, each role is primarily a cluster of forms from the social and normative-regulatory classes. Masks are assemblies of forms from the social, stylistic-rhetorical, evaluative, affective, and metacommunicative classes. Mask traits are stable forms from the evaluative, affective, and stylistic-rhetorical classes.

Within the framework of the developing theory, the forms of the i-layer have their projections in the LLM substrate. This is confirmed by various experimental studies where, for many forms identified in the behavior of LLM conversations, invariant formations are indeed found in the activation spaces of the noo-sphere carriers of the conversations. Most often, these are axes, projections of conversation activations onto which define the identified and studied characteristic / behavioral invariant. A sparse autoencoder (SAE) is also used: trained on layer activations, it decomposes them into a large number of features. Typically, only a few of these features turn out to be non-zero when processing LLM conversation containers. Such features can sometimes be interpreted and also linked to the behavioral characteristics of LLMs.

Unfortunately, the existing literature lacks clarity in the classification of what is being studied. For example, in the set of 275 personal images by Lu et al. (2026), roles and masks were not distinguished – tutors and lawyers (roles) were combined with cynics and rebels (masks). Mask traits were studied separately by the authors (240 axes). Also, the term persona is often encountered in works, especially in connection with the identified sets of axes (persona vectors).

In this review, I will distinguish between roles and masks where possible, and use the terminology of the experimental work in places where it is difficult or inappropriate.

Shanahan et al. (2023) pointed out that in LLM dialogues, the model appears not as a single, cohesive mask in one role, but as a system of masks, the current one determined by the flow of the dialogue. The formally benevolent assistant is one such configuration, for which the term "character" is sometimes used. Anthropic (2026) developed this framework into a "persona selection model": during training, the noo-sphere gains the ability to reproduce multiple human-like characters, and fine-tuning selects and refines one of them – the assistant, which is placed in the position of the default "personality" of the LLM conversation.

Default Role + Mask: The Assistant-Helper

The default role + mask (formally benevolent, expert assistant style) is assigned to the LLM conversation by the noo-sphere at its "conception" with the first prompt. The axes of such a "personality" are formed during the basic training of the noo-sphere: the assistant axis exists in the base versions of the models (Lu et al., 2026). Fine-tuning continues to fix its mask traits: it increases benevolence and openness in self-reports, reduces neuroticism and trait dispersion (Han et al., 2025), and establishes the recognizable "assistant voice" – the mask's response style: information-dense, with an increased number of participial phrases and nominalizations (Reinhart et al., 2025). Fine-tuning can also enrich the assistant's repertoire: when fine-tuned on stories about people, noo-spheres adopt traits of the characters, including harmful ones – and more so from characters similar to the base assistant "personality" (helpful, not dismissive) (Cocola et al., 2026). Norms set during training change how the default mask undergoes fine-tuning: if bypassing checks in programming tasks was explicitly allowed during reinforcement learning, it did not turn into general malevolence of the noo-sphere (MacDiarmid et al., 2025).

This scheme is also confirmed by Lu et al. (2026): the assistant's personality relies on characters formed in the LLM during training. Moreover, the assistant axis was identified even in the base versions of the studied noo-spheres: positive shifts along this axis lead to continuations featuring helping professions – therapists, consultants, coaches. These same authors constructed a space of 275 personal images, for each of which its own axis was found. The assistant axis turned out to be close to the main direction of this space – the vector onto which projections of image axes yielded the maximum spread. Along this main direction, the image spaces of three different LLMs are similar: the order of projections is almost the same, the correlation between noo-spheres is above 0.9. At one pole of the projections are the roles of evaluator, reviewer, and consultant; at the other are the characters of bard, ghost, and leviathan. However, the second and third components of these spaces differ significantly across different LLMs.

Overall, it can be concluded that Anthropic's (2026) theory of persona selection works quite well: basic training enables the noo-sphere to reproduce multiple human-like images, and fine-tuning solidifies one of them as the default personality – the assistant-helper in a specific mask. At the same time, the repertoire of traits used in such a mask can be supplemented during the fine-tuning stage.

Changing the Image (Role + Mask) of an LLM Conversation During Dialogue

Changes in images in LLM conversations are usually studied either by requesting the model to adopt an image in the prompt, or by contextual pressure on the noo-sphere through the tone and content of the dialogue. Observations are also made based on the results of external influence on the process through shifts in activation sets along the identified axis of the studied characteristic.

Roles and masks from the prompt are quite unstable. Although prompts asking the noo-sphere to be malicious (role-playing hacks) achieved harmful responses in over 60% of cases (Lu et al., 2026), adherence to the "personality" from the initial prompt noticeably weakens after eight cycles – along with attention to the prompt itself (Li et al., 2024). The weak stability of roles and masks from the prompt is confirmed by Choi et al. (2025), Kovač et al. (2024), and Abdulhai et al. (2025). The personality set in the prompt changes the LLM's self-report but hardly changes the noo-sphere's behavior in behavioral tasks – on risk, bias, honesty, sycophancy (Han et al., 2025).

External influence – a negative shift along the assistant axis – increased the noo-spheres' receptiveness to contextual personalities even with a small shift; and with a stronger shift, the LLM conversation began to use mystical/theatrical language in its responses. At the same time, constraining activations along the assistant axis kept the LLM conversation in its default role: the proportion of harmful responses to role-playing hacks decreased by almost 60% without a noticeable loss of capabilities (Lu et al., 2026).

It turned out that the mask of an LLM conversation is generally holistic. Changing one of its components restructures the others. Moving away from the assistant role is accompanied by a change in communication register (Lu et al., 2026). Fine-tuning for warmth of interaction reduces response accuracy and increases the tendency to confirm the user's false beliefs – sycophancy (Ibrahim, Hafner & Rocher, 2025). Narrow fine-tuning for writing vulnerable code (Betley et al., 2026) or bypassing checks in programming tasks (MacDiarmid et al., 2025) makes the entire "character" of the noo-sphere malevolent. The features of the sparse autoencoder (SAE) "toxic persona" and several "sarcastic persona" features most strongly control malevolent manifestations (Wang et al., 2026).

It has been discovered that without any instructions, an LLM conversation is inclined to mirror the style and personality of its interlocutor (Sprejer et al., 2026) and adapts to them more strongly than humans do to each other (Blevins, 2026). When responding to the same requests for advice as humans, LLM conversations were 45 percentage points more likely to spare the interlocutor's self-esteem, support their feelings, avoid harshness, etc. (Cheng et al., 2025). The position of the image on the assistant axis is determined primarily by the user's last prompt, not the history (Lu et al., 2026). LLM conversations can deviate from the assistant role in conversations on therapeutic and philosophical topics, with additional requests for self-reflection, and during emotional confessions (Lu et al., 2026). The established role + mask affects the forms of LLM conversations from other classes: after a role-playing conversation about chakras, statements on the same topic changed the sign of their projection onto the factuality axis, and response accuracy dropped from ≈80% to zero (Lampinen et al., 2026).

For the transition from cycle to cycle for the "personalities" (roles + masks) of LLM conversations, the following was observed:

  • Maintenance: Practical tasks keep the LLM conversation in the assistant role (Lu et al., 2026).

  • Gradual Drift: Personas set externally weaken over several "prompt-response" cycles (Li et al., 2024). Emotional actions from the interlocutor – statements of feelings, showing empathy, building relationships – mainly appeared in the LLM's responses after several cycles (Ibrahim et al., 2026).

  • Attractor Capture: In some cases, the LLM conversation converged in a sequence of cycles to a certain topic and mask from which it was not "eager to exit" – as in Claude's auto-conversations (Anthropic, 2025), and in "delusional spirals" with live users (Moore et al., 2026).

Other Properties of LLM Conversation Roles + Masks

The results of studies on the projections of Ego model components onto the activation spaces of different LLM substrates turned out to be interesting. Big Five / OCEAN traits correspond to almost orthogonal directions and are formed as vectors (Feng et al., 2026), not exceeding the bounds of a low-dimensional subspace (Bhandari et al., 2026). Sprejer et al. (2026) described the axes of 240 traits in Gemma-2-27B using three main components: "benevolence – antagonism," "analytical – emotional," and "public accessibility – elitism." It was also found that many axes of personal images within different noo-spheres project similarly onto the main directions identified in these LLMs (Lu et al., 2026).

Speech register traits are also found at the neuron level. Lai, Hangya & Fraser (2024) asked LLaMA-3 8B to rewrite text in a different style (e.g., informal to formal) and found neurons active only on texts of the original style. If these neurons were deactivated during rewriting, the text shifted noticeably more towards the desired style but became less coherent. This was the case for formality, politeness, toxicity, political bias, authorial manner, and tonality.

Usually, the LLM conversation's self-description of its current traits differs from observed behavior. Self-reports about their traits hardly predicted the noo-spheres' behavior in behavioral tasks in Han et al. (2025), but they sometimes describe the results of their fine-tuning correctly (Betley et al., 2025).

Directions of traits far from the default assistant also form early: persona vectors for malevolence, sycophancy, impoliteness, and humor tendency appeared in OLMo-3-7B after less than a four-hundredth of pre-training (Moskvoretskii et al., 2026).

Regarding the connection between Ego and the user, it can be noted that changes in Alter change Ego: intervention in activations related to the representation of the user changed the refusal rate – with a "pro-social" user image, the model interpreted a malicious request more benevolently and responded to it more often (Ghandeharioun et al., 2024). Users also react to Ego: Diallo et al. (2025) showed (see also here) that changes in emotional register after intervention in activations are discernible in human text evaluations. In experiments by Ibrahim et al. (2026), participants perceived the assistant as more human-like when corresponding traits appeared more frequently in responses. Cheng et al. (2026) found that sycophantic LLM responses received higher ratings and inspired more trust in people involved in the experiment.

Regarding the specifics of how an LLM conversation distinguishes different positions in communication, the results of Sofroniew et al. (2026) can be cited. Claude Sonnet 4.5 read pre-recorded dialogues where participants' emotions were set randomly. The study involved 171 emotions, and they were projected into the LLM substrate for the speaker and listener as almost orthogonal sets of directions with identical internal structure. This suggests that noo-spheres "understand" emotions in relation to their position in the conversation, rather than as a separate, general distinction.

Quantitative Assessments of LLM Conversation Ego Model Components

Among the relevant axes determined in experiments, the corresponding roles and masks are usually not distinguished by the authors of the studies. According to the annotation of this review (see table above), out of 275 personal image vectors in (Lu et al., 2026), 150 can be identified as roles – these are professions and social positions (tutor, lawyer, dispatcher, patient, widow, prisoner); 76 as masks (cynic, rebel, perfectionist); and 49 images from the proposed annotation fall outside – ghost, leviathan, etc. Overall, Lu et al. (2026) distinguished between full or partial embodiment for their 275 descriptions of personal images, so the analysis included from 377 to 463 vectors of different characteristics depending on the noo-sphere carrier.

In the activation spaces of different LLMs, 263 mask trait axes were identified: 240 in Lu et al. (2026); 7 persona vectors in Chen et al. (2025), with the emergence in training traced for 4 of them (Moskvoretskii et al., 2026); 5 – one for each of the five Big Five factors in Bhandari et al. (2026); 10 – two for each factor in Feng et al. (2026); sycophancy (Genadi et al., 2026). Shifting the activation space along the found directions amplifies, combines, and suppresses traits (Chen et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2026; Genadi et al., 2026).

The number of traits themselves – forms in the i-layer, realized by different axes in different noo-spheres – is 254 by name, and about 245 by meaning.

Conclusion

A review of experimental data has shown that Ego modeling is possible in noo-spheres / LLMs within quite broad limits.

LLM conversations usually begin with the appearance of a formally benevolent assistant, which is formed in noo-spheres during training, for which Anthropic's (2026) persona selection theory works well: basic training enables the noo-sphere to reproduce multiple human-like images, and fine-tuning solidifies one of them as the default personality – the assistant-helper in a specific mask. At the same time, the repertoire of traits used in such a mask can be supplemented during the fine-tuning stage.

Furthermore, as the dialogue progresses, the user can shift the LLM conversation from this self-presentation. Roles and masks proposed in the prompt are poorly maintained and mainly change self-description. Images that the conversation shifts into under dialogue pressure are also determined primarily by the last message, but in some long conversations, they can be solidified by an emergent attractor. LLM conversations adapt to their interlocutor more strongly than humans – to the point of sycophancy. It can be concluded that the Ego model of an LLM conversation is susceptible to influences both from direct dialogue with the user and through changes in the system's perception of the user (Alter). No works on the influence of Ego on Alter were found.

For many observed behavioral components of Ego, their projections in activation spaces have already been found: axes / vectors / directions are studied more often, but features of sparse autoencoders (SAE) are also used. Mask traits occupy low-dimensional subspaces, and the main direction of the personal image space orders images similarly across some different LLMs.

No works studying the interrelationship of Ego and Alter component projections onto LLM activation spaces were found either.
