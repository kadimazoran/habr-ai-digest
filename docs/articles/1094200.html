Over the past two years, large businesses' attitudes towards large language models have shifted. The debate about whether LLMs are needed in business has ended, and the technology has moved from being a fun toy to a strategic tool.

We at Content AI conducted over 50 in-depth interviews with IT and security representatives from medium and large businesses in retail, banking, oil and gas, energy, telecom, and other sectors. The discussions covered readiness for implementation, data transfer, initiators and blockers for LLM projects, pilot economics, and vendor requirements.

We will share the results and identified trends for the next 1-2 years in this article.

No Strategy Yet, But Cases Are Already in Production

The first result of the study was quite paradoxical. LLM implementation cases in business processes are emerging faster than companies are developing AI strategies.

Generative AI is already used in operational processes by 53% of respondents. Another 29% are conducting pilot projects. The remaining 18% are currently limited to employees using models for personal use.

At the same time, half of the companies participating in the survey do not have an approved AI strategy or it is in a nascent form, and policies and regulations are appearing late. A typical scenario: there is a point about AI development in the overall digitalization strategy, but no specific provisions for working with data, selecting models, and using agents.

The market assesses the maturity level cautiously. Almost a quarter of respondents believe they are at the initial stage, 41% consider themselves at "level 2" – those who are studying the effects of pilots but have not yet launched projects into production. Another 29% consider themselves at "level 3": they have working cases, internal models, or gateways, but systematic management is only being established. And only 6% of respondents believe they have reached "level 4-5" – with a hybrid of models, clear regulations, and measurable impact.

Choosing an Architecture: Own Infrastructure, Cloud, or Hybrid

The issue of LLM placement turned out to be one of the most complex. The spread of answers shows that the market has not yet agreed with itself.

41% of respondents preferred their own infrastructure for deploying models, 35% chose a hybrid scheme. Only 24% are ready to use cloud services as the main platform. No one mentioned a separate option of "private cloud," i.e., a dedicated environment with a provider: in practice, it is equated with the cloud, and information security services treat it with distrust.

The picture is different in pilots. 65% of respondents agree to launch in a public cloud with non-sensitive data or synthetic datasets. 21% are willing to transfer real information there. And 14% are categorically against using the cloud even in pilots.

The attitude towards Russian cloud LLMs is skeptical but pragmatic. 70% of respondents consider their quality insufficient, while 30% are already using or are ready to use such models.

The market is calmer about Chinese and open-source models in a closed environment. 56% perceive them positively or are already using them. A quarter of respondents are wary of them, 19% are still neutral and pragmatic. The origin of the model has not yet become a decisive factor; how data control is implemented is more important.

Foreign APIs cause concern. A direct internal ban on them is in place at 37% of surveyed companies, 50% allow limited use, and 13% use them under contract. The reasons for this caution are dictated by sanctions risks, fears of losing access, and unwillingness to transfer data to foreign jurisdictions.

Boundaries of Data Transfer Readiness

The survey results made it clear that the willingness to transfer data externally depends on the type of information and the importance of the process.

Opinions were almost equally divided regarding personal data. 31% of respondents reported a complete taboo on any transfer of personal data, another 31% allow processing exclusively within their own environment. The remaining 38% allow external models to work with personal data, but only with masking and a separate contract.

In total, 62% of respondents block the transfer of personal data to external services in one form or another.

The attitude towards trade secrets and know-how was even more acute. 56% allow working with them only within their own environment, 25% impose a complete ban on processing using LLMs, and only 19% are willing to transfer this to an external model.

Companies do outsource some tasks to the cloud. 46% of respondents are willing to perform service desk functions, process inquiries, and work with open data there, while 38% conditionally agree to such scenarios.

Financial and payment operations are conducted by AI agents with reservations from almost everyone: 80% of surveyed companies allow this only with mandatory human confirmation. The market does not yet accept full agent autonomy in financial matters.

In certain industries, the prohibitions are even stricter. For example, in the banking sector, the use of open cloud models and services based on them is completely prohibited. At the same time, experts note a side effect: a total ban without a secure alternative breeds "shadow AI," where employees secretly use public services, increasing the risks.

Information Security Hinders 76% of AI Projects

The initiative to launch an LLM project almost never comes from a single person. In 47% of surveyed companies, it is promoted by several departments simultaneously: business units expect cost savings, engineering departments are attracted by new technological capabilities, and top management sees it as a way for structural optimization. In 27% of respondent companies, the initiator is business, and in 26%, it is IT teams and top management.

The main blocker here is the information security department, which was indicated as an obstacle by 76% of respondents. Economics and ROI slow down the process in 6% of cases, bureaucracy and procurement – also in 6%. Another 12% of respondents reported a combination of factors.

As a result, the market is currently forced to navigate between information security policies and precedents. 53% of surveyed companies reported that they make decisions based on actual emerging cases, while 47% have pre-approved information security policies.

Companies Expect Easy Pilots and Quick Results

The question about capital expenditures turned out to be one of the most sensitive. According to 77% of respondents, high CAPEX significantly hinders pilot projects, 15% experience a moderate cooling effect, and only for 8% is money not a deterrent factor.

A typical market scenario looks like this: a vendor promises a quick return on investment but, to start, suggests purchasing a computing cluster for 20-30 million rubles. With shrinking IT budgets, this option is practically unfeasible. Most companies expect quick results and are not willing to tie up funds in equipment.

The market is almost unanimous on how to start pilots: 83% of respondents consider the ability to start with an easy cloud pilot, measure the effect in 2-3 months, and only then, if successful, move the solution to an internal environment, to be critically important. This approach allows demonstrating results before requesting significant investments.

The horizon of expectations also differs: medium-sized businesses want to see results within a quarter, while large businesses are prepared to calculate the total cost of ownership for years to come. But a common rule applies to everyone: without a quick demonstration of results, large sums will not be allocated to the project.

Customer Requirements for LLM Platform Vendors

The main criterion for choosing a vendor is unequivocally the ability to control data and ensure information security – 59% of respondents answered this way. In second place (in 35% of cases) are TCO, return on investment, and price, while launch speed is important for only 6% of respondents.

The ability to bring your own model (BYO-LLM) is important or desirable for 86% of respondents. Companies do not want to be tied to a specific vendor and prefer to choose the model themselves for the task.

Infrastructure assistance is needed by 56% of respondents. They expect a turnkey solution, especially when there are no in-house competencies for deploying large models. The remaining 44% are willing to manage the infrastructure themselves but would not refuse optional support.

Only 15% of Companies Are Ready to Pilot Autonomous Agents

The question of how ready businesses are for fully autonomous agents divided respondents into three groups. 47% were skeptical. 38% are ready to support agent use only with human involvement. Only 15% agree to pilot fully autonomous solutions.

In terms of information security tools, opinions were as follows. Data masking is considered the strongest protection tool by the market. 91% of respondents acknowledged the ability of this tool to remove some internal restrictions and open the way for hybrid schemes. However, some experts still believe that masking does not save the day by itself if the data still travels over an unsecured channel.

Companies combat "shadow AI" in various ways. 56% consider a corporate gateway, through which all external requests to LLMs pass, with logging and filtering, to be the most successful strategy. Another 22% have no information security policy on this issue. The remaining 22% try to block employee actions and admit that this solution does not work.

The market separately notes a new class of risks in agent automation: situations where agents "negotiate" with each other. According to 70% of respondents, information security services will have to revise policies considering this effect.

What Will Change in Two Years

Based on the collected opinions, 6 trends are clearly emerging.

  1. Security policies will catch up with actual implementations. Few people currently have strategies and regulations, and this does not hinder project launches. In two years, the market will catch up: standard risk models, vendor questionnaires, and ready-made regulations will appear.

  2. Hybrid architecture will become the standard. A third of companies already operate on a hybrid logic. Masking, recognized as effective by the majority of respondents, will become a universal approach to data handling.

  3. The information security department will remain the main barrier. Overcoming it will be achieved through a scheme that considers key information security requirements: a package of documents, a test zone, and a clear responsibility model.

  4. Easy start will be more important than large capital expenditures. Investments in internal environments will remain for production, while pilots will almost always start in the cloud or on leased resources.

  5. AI agents will take over routine document operations. However, processes will remain under human control (HITL) for a long time.

  6. Value will shift from the model itself to its surrounding infrastructure. The product around which an AI project is built will be evaluated by its management tools, quality control pipelines, logging, and responsibility system.
