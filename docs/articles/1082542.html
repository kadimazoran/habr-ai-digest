What Selectel wrote about

PCIe 6.0 SSDs: Speeds Approaching 30 GB/s Reach Servers

The first mass-produced PCIe 6.0 SSDs are entering the market in 2026 (server models only for now). Micron 9650 delivers up to 28 GB/s read speeds, and Samsung PM1763 even slightly more—up to 28.4 GB/s. Kioxia CM10, in turn, promises a 92% speed increase compared to the previous generation. The reason for their appearance now is the release of new platforms and the growth of AI workloads, where SSDs are needed to store model weights and KV cache, which is no longer fitting into the accelerators' HBM memory. The standard will reach home PCs much later.

Supermicro ASG-4116S-NU160R: A 4U Server with 166 NVMe Drives

Supermicro has introduced the Storage SuperServer ASG-4116S-NU160R. It features 160 U.2 NVMe slots plus four additional E1.S PCIe 5.0 x4 and two internal M.2 slots in a single four-unit chassis with AMD EPYC™ 9005 processors. The U.2 drives are connected via PCIe 4.0 x1, not x4—this is a compromise for density: the individual speed of each drive is limited, but it allows for a huge number of parallel I/O operations. Using 122 TB drives, the server's total raw capacity reaches 19.5 PB.

Flash Memory Summit: Petabyte on Two Drives and CXL Technology

At FMS 2026, DapuStor showcased the R6060 SSD with 512 TB in the E2 form factor. This is a record for drives of this form factor: two such drives provide a petabyte in two slots. Simultaneously, four companies—Marvell, Synopsys, Montage Technology, and Kioxia—demonstrated the direction of CXL. Various devices were presented: from controllers for DDR4/DDR5 to a complete IP stack for CXL 4.0 with a bandwidth of 128 GT/s. Offloading KV cache to CXL memory instead of SSD, according to Synopsys estimates, provides a three to sixfold performance increase and up to 50% savings on inference.

Cerebras CS-4: The First System with Three WSE-3 Turbo Chips

At Hot Chips 2026, Cerebras introduced the CS-4 rack with three WSE-3 Turbo™ processors, which are monolithic 300mm silicon wafers with 900,000 cores and 44 GB of on-chip SRAM each. Instead of HBM, memory is placed directly on the die with an aggregated bandwidth of 43,200 TB/s. The company claims that on heavy models, the system is 30 times faster than a comparable GPU cluster in inference speed.

The company also revealed more details about other devices. CS-5 promises up to 10,000 tokens per second per user (platform release scheduled for 2027), and CS-6 will move to 3D integration: SRAM and DRAM will be placed in a vertical layer above the compute cores directly within the wafer structure.

Samsung LPDDR5X-PIM: Computations Integrated Directly into Memory Banks

At Hot Chips 2026, Samsung showcased LPDDR5X-PIM memory. This is a standard 16 GB module in a familiar form factor, with MAC trees for matrix computations integrated into each bank. For Llama 3.1 8B inference, this resulted in a threefold increase in tokens per second compared to regular memory (81.3 vs. 27.0) and an increase in effective bandwidth from 76.8 to 614 GB/s (without changing the external interface). The main caveat is that the inference precision on PIM currently differs from the baseline—Samsung is still working on this.

What Else Appeared on the Market

Supermicro DCBBS: Racks for 2.5 Tons of Servers

Supermicro has expanded its server rack line with ten new models in the DCBBS family. These AI-load racks come pre-assembled: compute nodes, network equipment, power, and cooling are already installed at the factory. The mechanical load capacity per rack is up to 2,500 kg static, allowing it to be filled with GPU servers to capacity without structural concerns. Vibration and seismic resistance tests have been conducted. Solution options include racks for NVIDIA VR200 and GB300 MGX™ platforms.

Specifications:

  • Formats: 48U and 52U, 44OU and 48OU OCP ORv3 standard (21"), as well as traditional 19" in 48U and 52U;

  • Cooling: Support for various types of CDUs and rear-door heat exchangers (RDHx);

  • Power and network cables are routed in isolated channels.

The company plans to produce three thousand racks per month, two thousand of which will feature liquid cooling. This is an indicative figure for the market—Supermicro demonstrates the pace of mass production for heavy AI hardware.

SiFive BigSky SF-2U870 — The First RISC-V Server for Data Centers

SiFive has introduced the 2U BigSky SF-2U870 platform. This is the world's first RISC-V-based server for data centers. Currently, it's a development system for software testing, not production hardware.

Server specifications:

  • Processor: 32 P870-D cores (64-bit), clock speed 2 GHz;

  • Instruction set: RVA23 with Vector 1.0 and Vector Crypto;

  • Functionality: RAS, IOMMU, WorldGuard security subsystem;

  • Memory: 256 GB DDR5-5600;

  • Storage: two 7.68 TB U.2 NVMe SSDs;

  • Network: OCP 3.0 NIC (10/25GbE);

  • Expansion slots: four PCIe 5.0 x16 and one PCIe 3.0 x4, GPU support.

Support for Canonical Ubuntu 26.04 LTS and Red Hat RHEL10 operating systems is declared. SiFive is collaborating with NVIDIA on porting CUDA® and integrating NVLink® Fusion. Trial shipments have already begun. RISC-V development in the data center is slow but steady: BigSky is primarily needed by software developers who require a real platform, not an emulator.

Kioxia GP1: SSD with 10 Million IOPS

Kioxia has introduced the GP1 series SLC format drives. These are the first devices in the Super High IOPS SSD lineup. The target audience aligns with the 2026 trend: AI systems where SSDs are used not just for storage but as an extension of HBM directly from the GPU side.

Specifications:

  • Form factors: E3.S and E1.S (9.5 and 15 mm);

  • Interface: PCIe 6.0 with NVMe 2.2;

  • Flash: Second-generation XL-Flash with low latency;

  • DWPD 50, which is very high for a server SSD (and that's good);

  • Up to 10 million IOPS in random 512-byte block reads;

  • 9.5 mm versions support liquid cooling.

The architecture is designed for scaling up to 100 million IOPS in future generations. The GPU gets direct access to the drive, bypassing the host—this is what makes the GP1 a memory element in a hybrid topology. Capacity and other parameters have not yet been announced, but samples will be sent to select customers by the end of 2026. For Kioxia, this is a strategically important step, as the company has been working with NVIDIA for a long time on the concept of SSDs as an alternative to HBM memory. GP1 is the first commercial result of this work.

Smart IOPS + H3 Platform: 1 Billion IOPS in One Platform

The joint development by Smart IOPS and H3 Platform targets the most demanding segment of the AI storage market. This is a storage platform assembled from Unobtanium T50 drives in the E3.S 2T form factor, based on TLC NAND in pseudo-SLC mode. What does this mean?

Single drive:

  • PCIe 6.0 x4, NVMe 2.0;

  • Capacity 9/18/36 TB;

  • Sequential read speed 28 GB/s, write—24 GB/s;

  • Random 512-byte block read at 50 million IOPS, write—at 10 million.

The platform houses 20 such drives in E3.S 2T slots, as well as four NVIDIA RTX™ PRO 6000 Blackwell™ Server Edition GPUs with 96 GB of GDDR7 each. Additionally, there are four ConnectX®-8 network adapters. In total, this provides up to 1 billion IOPS of random read performance. Cooling is air-based.

Target workloads: graph neural networks, vector search, recommendation systems, RAG. In short, tasks where small random requests come in a stream. Trial shipments of the SSD and platform are scheduled for Q1 2027, with mass production in Q2. It's worth noting that such IOPS numbers were previously only seen in Storage Class Memory solutions, and now it's TLC NAND in pseudo-SLC mode.

Intel Xeon 7 Diamond Rapids: 256 P-cores, 1.28 GB L3, PCIe 6.0

At the Hot Chips 2026 conference, Intel revealed architectural details of the next generation of Xeon® 7 Diamond Rapids server processors.

Specifications:

  • Layout: Multi-Chip Package with two Fabric Hub Tiles (FHT) and four Base Tiles;

  • Each FHT carries eight DDR5 channels: a total of 16 channels per chip;

  • Each FHT provides 64 PCIe 6.0 lanes, configurable as CXL 3.0 or UPI 3: 128 lanes in total, plus eight PCIe 4.0 lanes;

  • Integrated hardware accelerators in FHT: TDX, SGX, QAT, DSA;

  • Supports DDR5-8000 and MRDIMM-12800.

The Base Tile combines four chiplets with 16 P-cores each: 64 cores per block, 256 cores per processor. L3: 320 MB in each Base Tile, totaling 1.28 GB per chip. Chiplets within the tile are connected via Foveros® 3D Direct. Panther Cove cores with support for AMX, AVX 10.2, FP8/FP16/BF16. Process nodes: cores—Intel 18A-P, Base Tile—Intel 3-T, FHT—Intel 3.

The layout approach is close to AMD EPYC, although Intel uses a more complex multi-level packaging with different process nodes for various functional blocks. Release is expected in the second half of 2027, with competition from AMD EPYC 9005 Venice.

Arm AGI: 136 Cores and 12 DDR5-8800 Channels

Arm has added details about the previously announced AGI server processors. The chips are built on the Neoverse® CSS V3 platform, manufactured using TSMC N3P. The design is dual-chiplet, with over 50 billion transistors in each chiplet.

Specifications:

  • Variants: 64, 128, or 136 Neoverse® V3 cores, based on the Armv9.2 ISA;

  • Base frequency 3.2 GHz, boost—up to 3.7 GHz;

  • L2: 2 MB per core (128–272 MB per processor);

  • SLC: 128 MB;

  • Each core contains two 128-bit SVE2 units;

  • Supports BF16 and INT8;

  • Memory: 12 channels of DDR5-8800, up to 6 TB total, bandwidth—up to 845 GB/s;

  • 96 PCIe 6.0 lanes with CXL 3.0 support plus four PCIe 4.0 lanes;

  • TDP: 300 W.

The philosophy differs from Intel and AMD: the focus is on low memory access latency (less than 100 ns), with no aggressive chiplet stacking. Each chiplet contains a CMN-S3 interconnect in an 8x9 topology with Super Home Node. The architecture and approach could be successful for LLM inference with a small number of parameters. Commercial shipments of Arm AGI are expected in the coming months.

Intel Crescent Island: 480 GB LPDDR5X Instead of HBM

At Hot Chips 2026, Intel again piqued interest and showed details of the Crescent Island accelerator. This is a PCIe 5.0 x16 GPU for inference in servers and workstations. Samples are expected in the current half-year.

Configuration:

  • 32 Xe3P™ cores;

  • 256 XMX256™ matrix engines;

  • 1 MB GRF and 512 KB L1 cache per core;

  • 32 MB shared L2;

  • Supported formats: FP64, FP16, BF16, TF32, INT8, INT4, FP8, FP4.

The key architectural decision is the abandonment of HBM in favor of LPDDR5X. The reference card holds 160 GB, and ODM manufacturers can install up to 480 GB. The TDP is 350 W, with air cooling. The main advantage was memory capacity: four cards with 480 GB provide 1.92 TB in a single workstation—enough for models with a large number of parameters, including KV cache. Software support includes vLLM, SGLang, llm-d, and NVIDIA® Dynamo™.

LPDDR5X is cheaper than HBM. Intel is not competing with NVIDIA in memory bandwidth—the new device competes on capacity and price. For the long-context inference market, this approach could prove quite effective. We are still awaiting the product's market release.

Primemas: CXL Modules with 4 TB DRAM and SLiM Architecture without Switches

Primemas has showcased CXL memory expanders for AI systems and announced a partnership with Micron as part of the Abaco™ project. Two modules were presented: PMA14 (14 RDIMM slots, up to 3.5 TB) and PMA16 (16 RDIMM slots, up to 4 TB using 256 GB DIMMs). The proprietary Hublet architecture is used to form large CXL memory pools. These modules will be part of the Abaco™ platform—a rack-mounted computing system with over 100 TB of DRAM for the Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) of the U.S. Department of Energy.

Additionally, the SLiM™ (Switchless Pooled Memory) architecture was announced—a 1U system without external CXL switches. Multiple servers simultaneously access tens of terabytes of shared DRAM with minimal latency. This directly addresses the problem of idle GPUs waiting for data from memory.

While CXL was previously mostly theoretical, Abaco™ for DoE is another real project with a specific customer. The switchless architecture reduces cost and latency—an interesting direction for those unwilling to pay for CXL switches.

KIT + Tsukuba: Cooling Without Electricity, Where Heat Itself Drives the Process

Researchers from the Karlsruhe Institute of Technology (KIT) and the University of Tsukuba have presented a prototype solid-state cooling system for data centers that operates without external power supply. It is based on the elastocaloric cooling method using materials with a shape memory effect.

The design consists of two ultra-thin films made of a nickel-titanium alloy. The first film contracts when heated, converting thermal energy into mechanical work. The second film cools due to reversible changes in its crystal lattice under cyclic load. Heat from the servers initiates this cycle without actuators or external power.

The results are modest so far: at a temperature of 86 °C for the first film, the prototype achieves a temperature difference of only 4°C. Traditional air and liquid cooling systems achieve 10–20°C, so replacing conventional coolers is still a long way off. However, conceptually, it's interesting: cooling in data centers accounts for up to 40% of total energy consumption, and any method to slightly shift this figure represents a potentially huge saving for hyperscalers. The developers are continuing to work on compact commercial versions.
