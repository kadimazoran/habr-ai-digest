Hello, Habr! My name is Rodion, and I lead the data analysis department at the Data Laboratory of Cinimex company.

In this article, I want to share our practical experience and the evolution of approaches to applying various LLM use cases in our work. Spoiler alert – it wasn't without its bumps and bruises!)

Currently, the IT industry is experiencing an obvious hype around vibe-coding and agent-based development. The term “vibe-coding” appeared in 2025, while our first substantial “approach to the task” dates back to 2023.

2023: Trying Metrics

Case 1. CDT

Our company has a product called CDT, also known as Cinimex Development Tool. It's a tool for optimizing software development processes, allowing large businesses and IT departments to reduce the cost of creating software products and accelerate solution delivery.

In 2023, LLMs started gaining popularity, and we had an idea to explore LLM use cases within our product.

We started discussing:

— What exactly can be done?
— Where can artificial intelligence truly help?

A hypothesis emerged: our clients have a vast amount of technical specifications. These documents can be quite lengthy, written in different styles and with varying structures. Analysts have to spend hours analyzing the text to extract requirements and entities, often from multiple files.

Currently, such tasks are solved by LLMs “out of the box,” but at that time, we decided to use an NER (Named Entity Recognition) approach, take a large language model, and train it to automatically parse technical specifications:

  • extract requirements;

  • identify key entities;

  • assist analysts and architects.

We gathered a substantial dataset of technical specifications – large and small; well-structured and with a lot of “fluff.” We performed annotation and conducted several dozen experiments.

Internal tests showed ROC-AUC metrics of 0.7 and F1 of 0.61. The metrics weren't mind-blowing, but it was a start. “Not bad,” we thought, and went to present the solution to the target audience – analysts and architects. They looked at it and cooled us down, saying something like:

“Guys, we understand you have good metrics. You might even improve them further. But if the system makes mistakes even in twenty percent of cases, we still have to manually re-check the entire result. If everything needs to be checked manually, there's almost no practical value.”

This means that from the researchers' perspective, things were developing successfully, but from the end-user's perspective, they were not. Even good metrics didn't mean the product was actually helping people work faster.

Users weren't trying to interpret our ML metrics. Their criterion was simpler: if the result couldn't be accepted without a full manual re-check, the tool wasn't saving time. Of course, it was a bit disappointing, but that's when the first important conclusion emerged:

Good model metrics do not yet equate to high business value, and the quality of an AI function should be measured not only by the quality of the model's response but also by the cost of a human verifying that response.

Case 2. CTT

After the CDT experience, for another product, CTT, also known as Cinimex Test Tool, we decided to approach it with the “generative” capabilities of LLMs, starting with direct dialogue with the potential target audience – testers.

We held brainstorms, selected scenarios based on business value and feasibility, created a prototype using Open Source models and ChatGPT, and prepared documents for approval. Next, we planned to connect the project Wiki and integrate the module with CDT.

The prototype worked, tests were generated, but the development costs for the solution outweighed the business value. The project did not move forward.

Case 3. Knowledge Base Assistant

This time, we worked with a digital assistant for the first and second lines of support for a Top-20 bank – these lines were drowning in internal regulations. We started with a PoC: we took regulatory documentation from open sources and tested the hypothesis – could a neural network answer questions from a knowledge base? Essentially, this was a classic RAG system before such solutions became mainstream.

The approach proved viable, and we moved to an MVP with real bank data: we collected internal instructions and regulations from one department, fine-tuned the model on this dataset, and gave it to employees for testing. The department started using the system, and the project initiator said, “It works.” However, attempts to scale the solution to neighboring departments did not generate enthusiasm: “Why do we need it?”. The pilot did not receive support and stalled.

Summary of 2023. We optimized the model, but we should have optimized human work

By the end of 2023, we had learned two lessons:

  1. good metrics do not equal the value of the finished solution

  2. a successful pilot is not yet proof of a scenario's scalability

2024: From Local PoCs to Corporate AI Use

Case 1. Niche Languages

We decided to look for value from the development side and create an LLM-based tool integrated into the IDE for generating code in RPGLE and COBOL.

The problem is that specialists in such “niche” languages are becoming fewer. The stack exists, as do a considerable number of systems written in it. They continue to be used, but developing and maintaining them is becoming increasingly difficult.

The idea was to develop a code generation tool integrated directly into the IDE. This would allow developers to get:

  • autocompletion;

  • code generation;

  • assistance with writing tests;

  • the ability to fine-tune the model for the specifics of a particular project.

We analyzed the LLMs available at the time, assembled a dataset for fine-tuning (sources included GitHub repositories with RPGLE, The Stack v2 datasets, and internal Cinimex GitLab repositories), and worked out a fine-tuning methodology. And here, for the first time, we got a result we were truly satisfied with. It turned out to be a full-fledged Proof of Concept – the model was integrated into the IDE, and it genuinely started helping developers.

It seems this was our first success in this direction. Value delivery was achieved!

Case 2. AI Strategy

At the same time, LLMs were already making a creeping expansion into the minds and daily lives of not just developers. Our company is large, with many teams, and everyone started using AI in their own way. Some work with ChatGPT, some try GitHub Copilot, some use local models, and some use nothing at all (outrageous in the era of AI Adoption).

As long as these are experiments, it's not a problem. But considering that our projects' code belongs to clients, and repositories contain commercial information, industrial-scale development using public services is absolutely out of the question.

A large internal survey was conducted within the company; below is a snapshot from our department (Data Laboratory). The results were contradictory (see Fig. 2):

  • 69% of employees saw the benefit in LLMs, with 45% using LLMs weekly.

  • Main use cases: copywriting, brainstorming, research.

  • However, it unexpectedly turned out that various Code Assistants worked poorly. The average experience was limited to “tried it a few times.” Developers generally said: “It writes code at a junior level. It's easier for me to write this piece of code myself than to figure out what the model generated later.”

Summary of 2024. AI became useful – and immediately the problem of scale arose

Teams were already using AI independently, but the set of tools, scenarios, and operating rules varied. This means adoption had already begun without centralized management.

2025: Prompt Engineering and Context

Case 1. AI Trainer for Sales Support

In 2025, we began working on an applied task far removed from coding – we needed to improve the communication skills of sales representatives, people who talk to customers, present brands, and handle objections.

We developed an AI trainer: a virtual client that conducts a dialogue from greeting to final agreement, raises objections, checks arguments, and after the conversation, analyzes mistakes – what went well, where there was a failure, what needs improvement.

The product reached the MVP stage with measurable KPIs: the frequency of offers from consultants increased by at least 15%, and the conversion from offer to pilot increased by at least 20%.

This was the first time we spoke not of a subjective feeling of usefulness, but of real business metrics.

Of course, not everything went smoothly on the project. For example, the architecture had to be rewritten several times because the models were updated faster than we could assemble the solution. Moreover, when we ranked the emerging problems, it turned out that architecture was only in 3rd place. RAG took 2nd, and prompt tuning took 1st.

Thus, we concluded that changing the model or reassembling the architecture was easier than precisely formulating the task for it. This means the quality of the final dialogue was determined not so much by the model “under the hood” as by the precise descriptions in the prompts of the trainer's role, objection rules, and the format for analyzing mistakes.

Spec-Driven is getting closer, and soon we will have an opportunity to test this conclusion again, this time in one of the most closed ecosystems. If the AI trainer case interested you, you can find details in Vadim's publication on Habr.

Case 2. 1C

Typically, agent-based development is associated with Python or JavaScript, but there are not only niche programming languages, which I mentioned earlier. There are popular ecosystems in Russia whose codebases you won't find on GitHub. In the enterprise sector, a significant portion of business processes are automated using the 1C family of products. This platform does not welcome direct interference with its internal structure, and I was quite surprised by the availability of modern tools within 1C.

New features appeared:

  1. the EDT (Enterprise Development Tools) mechanism, which exposes the project structure, source code, and the entire engineering context to the agent,

  2. integration via MCP, transforming the agent from a chat participant into a development process participant.

If you are interested in the symbiosis of 1C and agents, I recommend reading my colleague's article.

Summary of 2025. The model is no longer the main thing

Generation itself has ceased to be a deficit. Value has shifted to the quality of task definition, context, and agent operating rules.

2026: Spec-Driven

By 2026, AI had evolved from an experiment into a part of everyday work. Here are a few recent quotes from colleagues of various roles and grades, all based on real events:

  • The LLM is a deceiver, of course, but it also helps well; it's a good assistant for an experienced developer.

  • System Analyst tasks, such as writing technical specifications, preparing High-Level Design (HLD), formulating requirements, preparing diagrams, etc., have started taking much less time. The time to prepare HLD for one feature has decreased by 1.5-2 times. The role of the SA is increasingly shifting towards the point where the most complex work is gathering the correct context from stakeholders.

  • Using LLM for code reviews identifies vulnerabilities and improves code quality. Code review time has significantly decreased, and the quality of remarks is quite adequate.

  • It's possible to develop small projects solo.

We do not deny the vibe-coding approach itself and the flight of fancy in pair programming with AI; in its pure form, this approach is suitable for prototyping: where the cost of error is rework. In a production project, it leads to disproportionate costs for debugging and maintenance.

By 2026, we were able to draw three conclusions:

  1. The result depends on the accuracy of the task definition – this was shown by the ranked list of problems during the development of the AI trainer.

  2. The agent needs project context, not just a text field – this was confirmed by the experience of colleagues working with 1C.

  3. Mass use without a common engineering loop proved to be fragmented and poorly scalable – this we understood from the respondents' answers.

All three conclusions point to one idea: task definition, context, and rules must cease to be one-off prompts and become project artifacts.

With this in place, code ceases to be the primary development asset. The cheaper its generation and modification become, the more valuable formalized requirements, constraints, and architectural decisions become.

Specifications become the single context and synchronization point between humans and AI: architecture, specifications, and implementation strategy remain the engineer's responsibility, and code generation becomes the final step, not the starting point.

There are many publications on Spec-Driven, and without claiming to reinvent the wheel, I will present a thesis-based approach, and colleagues will elaborate in future publications with examples from production projects.

Suppose we need to implement a document upload service. In the prompt-driven approach, the developer describes the task to the agent in text. In Spec-Driven, roles, constraints, NFRs (non-functional requirements), interfaces, and acceptance criteria are first fixed, after which the agent operates within this set of artifacts.

The steps and artifacts may differ – the principle is the same: specification first, then code.

Results. Context becomes part of the project

Remember the analysts who refused to check others' mistakes in 2023? Vibe-coding without an engineering loop turns every developer into such an analyst: instead of writing code, they review generated code, and debugging consumes more time than generation saves.

From the 2023 approach of “let's add a bit of ML,” we have arrived at a process where the model is an executor within defined boundaries.

In other words, it's not so much the model that works as the framework around it.
