New Priorities

Firstly, target metrics are changing. If previously, when evaluating a data center, we usually talked about peak performance in FLOPS, storage capacity in TBytes, energy efficiency PUE, and the cost of computation in FLOPS per $ or ₽, then for AI, this is no longer enough. It becomes more important how many tokens a model can generate per second (tokens/s), how quickly it starts responding (time-to-first-token), how fast it outputs subsequent tokens (time-per-output-token), how many tokens can be processed per Watt of power (tokens/W), and what the cost of generating one token is ($/token).

Now we are interested not in the abstract power of the compute unit, but in the efficiency of a specific model on specific infrastructure.

New Processors

The processors themselves are also changing. After several decades of architectural stagnation and the almost total dominance of the x64 architecture, we are again seeing a surge of engineering creativity. Nvidia still dominates the market, having successfully been in the right place at the right time twice: gaming GPUs were initially well-suited for mining, and then even better for AI.

But many alternative architectures are rapidly growing alongside: Chinese Huawei Ascend, Cambricon MLU, Biren, etc., proprietary accelerators from American hyperscalers – Google TPU, Amazon Trainium and Inferentia, Microsoft Maia, Meta MTIA. By the way, we are actively working with several types of Chinese GPUs, know how to program them, and, if necessary, can use them in the design of our AI System Scala^r.

There are also completely unusual solutions like Cerebras, which instead of cutting a silicon wafer into many individual chips, makes one giant processor out of it (as long as I can remember, there have always been companies in the world trying to create wafer-scale processors, but Cerebras has finally managed to turn wafer-scale computing into a practically working commercial product).

The architectural excitement has led to IT companies actively trying to poach university professors of fundamental mathematics, in the hope of improving the algorithms implemented in silicon.

Memory and Interconnects

Large models have to store hundreds of gigabytes and terabytes of weights and caches and continuously transfer this data to compute devices. Therefore, a whole new hierarchy of memory and interconnects has emerged.

Chiplets within a single micro-assembly are connected by the UCIe interface, and ultra-fast HBM memory is located next to the GPU. New generations of PCIe 6.0/7.0 provide exchange with accelerators, network cards, and storage devices, while CXL allows connecting and pooling additional memory. Coherent interconnects like NVLink-C2C and AMD Infinity Fabric allow CPUs and GPUs to work together with memory, increasingly turning it into a shared system resource (you know, such architectures were popular about forty years ago in the supercomputers of that time, but at speeds 1000 times or more slower than now). NVLink/NVSwitch and UALink connect dozens of accelerators, while InfiniBand and Ultra Ethernet connect servers and racks; RDMA technology allows accessing data in the memory of remote nodes almost directly, without unnecessary copying through the CPU.

The boundary between memory and the network is gradually blurring: the overall system performance increasingly depends on how quickly data moves between compute units.

Definitions of terms used

  • Chiplet – a functional block of a complex microcircuit (e.g., a processor) physically located on a separate die. Chiplets can be standard – components of typical CPUs and GPUs, or “custom” (custom or semi-custom, which are integrated into micro-assemblies with standard chiplets to create specialized devices).

  • Micro-assembly – a package/module that combines several chiplets and/or other physical components on a common substrate to solve a specific functional task.

  • UCIe – an open standard for high-speed chiplet interconnection within a package/micro-assembly.

  • HBM memory – High Bandwidth Memory, high-speed DRAM with a 3D layout, placed next to the main GPU/accelerator unit.

  • PCIe – Peripheral Component Interconnect Express, a standard high-speed bus for connecting boards and devices to a processor. The transition from the current PCIe 5.0 version to PCIe 6.0/7.0 is underway – new PCIe generations with extended functionality, higher throughput, and improved encoding.

  • CXL – Compute Express Link, an open cache-coherent interconnect for connecting memory and accelerators and pooling them together.

  • Coherent interconnect – a connection that maintains cache/memory consistency between multiple processors/GPUs and custom chiplets.

  • NVLink-C2C – NVIDIA’s proprietary coherent chip-to-chip interconnect for connecting GPUs, CPUs, and chiplets.

  • AMD Infinity Fabric – AMD’s scalable coherent interconnect fabric for connecting CPU cores, GPUs, chiplets, and memory.

  • InfiniBand – an open standard for high-speed switched fabric, traditionally used in supercomputers and data centers for data exchange between servers and storage devices with very low latency and high throughput. Effectively controlled by Nvidia (after its acquisition of Mellanox).

  • Ultra Ethernet – a new open standard based on Ethernet, being developed by the UEC consortium to improve the performance, scalability, and compatibility of networks in artificial intelligence and high-performance computing systems.

Electrical Ethernet at speeds of 800 Gb/s and above is only possible over very short distances and involves significant power loss, so co-packaged optics (CPO, optics integrated into the same package with the network chip) and silicon photonics (i.e., data transmission via optical signals using silicon-based photonic components) move optical interfaces almost to the network processor itself – the switch ASIC (specialized network switch chip).

The Rack as a Computer (or rather, as a System)

The very concept of a computer is gradually changing. Previously, its natural unit was a server. Now, dozens of CPUs and GPUs within a rack are connected by such fast interconnects that they begin to work as practically a single computing machine. The next level is a group of racks, and ultimately – the entire data center. Therefore, processors, memory, networks, storage, power, and cooling can no longer be designed independently. Modern data centers are no longer created from a “scattering of commodity components,” but from carefully designed and balanced software and hardware complexes for typical infrastructure tasks.

Inference and Agents

The nature of typical computational load is changing. Previously (literally just a year ago), a huge cluster was needed primarily for training a model, and its subsequent use in a corporate environment seemed like a relatively cheap task. With the spread of reasoning models and AI agents, the situation is changing. The model may perform many intermediate computation cycles with each request, access other models, tools, and databases. As a result, it is mass inference – the daily operation of already trained models – that becomes one of the main consumers of computing resources. One could even say that in the near future, AI agents will become the primary customers and consumers of data center resources.

Power Consumption and Cooling

The depth of changes is particularly noticeable in power supply and cooling. The power of a modern AI rack already exceeds 100 kW, and in the future, Megawatts per rack are being discussed. All this energy is ultimately converted into heat. It is impossible to manage with air cooling at such densities, so liquid direct-to-chip cooling becomes part of the computing system architecture. At the same time, the power supply scheme also needs to be changed: high currents, numerous voltage conversions, and associated losses become too expensive. It is indicative that in 2026, Google, Microsoft, and NVIDIA, through the Open Compute Project (OCP), began standardizing a new 800 VDC (800 Volts Direct Current) power scheme for the next generation of AI data centers.

Geography and Energy

The geography of data centers is changing. If previously the location was chosen based on land cost, connectivity, and proximity to users, then for new data centers, the main limitation becomes available electrical power. Data centers are located near large generation sources, supplemented with energy storage and even their own power systems.

Projects to deploy AI data centers into space look spectacular and, although technically feasible, remain problematic both financially and technically. One prospective Megawatt rack placed in space would require 5.6 thousand m² of solar panels and 2.5 thousand m² of radiators in an optimal sun-oriented orbit. For comparison, the ISS has six radiators, each weighing a ton, and they dissipate only about 70 kW of heat.

Personally, partly from my own experience, I like underwater data centers more. It is particularly impressive that the reliability of a well-designed and redundant system in an underwater container turns out to be several times higher than its analogue located in a conventional data center (the reason, probably, is that no one bothers it underwater). But it must be admitted that power supply issues for such data centers are solved quite difficultly, and planned updates are expensive.

More realistic today are AI data centers near large hydroelectric power plants (bonus – free cooling), with compact next-generation nuclear reactors, with their own microgrids based on gas turbine and gas piston generators.

Data

Finally, data requirements are sharply increasing. Not long ago, we thought we had figured out the architecture, hardware, and software of Big Data. It turned out that our previous big data was small. For industrial AI solutions, much more data is needed. It is necessary to store training corpora, images and videos, vector representations of documents, request and response history, AI agent data, telemetry, and various caches. It seems that corporations no longer have "junk data" – AI is potentially capable of drawing valuable conclusions from any information. Therefore, data storage, data lakes and lakehouses, streaming systems like Kafka, object storage, and NVMe caches accompany AI systems as an obligatory component of the infrastructure and are gradually forming a unified multi-level data system. Without such a high-load data management system, the best GPU accelerators are completely useless.

Management

All this, of course, complicates the software management of the infrastructure. It is no longer enough to simply find a free server and run an application on it. It is necessary to consider where the required model is located (stored or running), how much free memory the accelerator has, how much or what fraction of the GPU to allocate for which inference task and for what time quantum, which interconnect connects the GPUs, where the required cache is located and what response latency needs to be ensured, where the data to be processed is stored, what its structure is, and how to optimize the speed of its retrieval. Therefore, alongside traditional data center management tools, specialized schedulers and systems for managing distributed execution of AI workloads are emerging.

System as the Result

As a result, AI transforms a data center from a room with a large number of relatively independent servers into one huge distributed computer. Its processors, memory, network, storage systems, power supply, cooling, and software must be designed jointly. Therefore, the natural form of organization and delivery of such infrastructure becomes software and hardware complexes (from Nvidia, Dell, Huawei – abroad, from Scala^r of the Rubytech group and others – in Russia): it is becoming increasingly difficult to assemble a truly balanced AI system even from the best individual components.
