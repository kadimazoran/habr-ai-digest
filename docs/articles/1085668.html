Real-time kernels, network card drivers, protocols used by essentially one vendor, payment hardware with custom firmware. There are almost no examples of this online, and you see errors on an oscilloscope; they might not appear in logs at all. That's what I'll talk about: why it worked for me and what's needed for it.

What Happened to the Company

The company I work for makes equipment and software for self-service car washes. For many years, I was responsible for the software architecture, and in my daily work, I mainly supported the software for vending terminals. Then things went south, leading to bankruptcy. Production halted, developers were laid off one by one, and at some point, I was the only one left for all development.

Meanwhile, the car washes themselves didn't disappear. There are over two hundred of them in Russia, Kazakhstan, and Belarus. They accept money every day, each has an owner, and the owner couldn't care less how many employees the manufacturer has left. If their terminal freezes, or the coin acceptor stops being recognized, or the payment service changes its protocol, someone has to fix it. There was no one to fix it but me, and at all levels simultaneously, from the microcontroller to the cloud.

How Support Saved the Business

At first, I only focused on keeping the system from crashing. But when a system works, people notice. Orders started coming in, rebranding began, and new features and integrations were needed. As a result, it was the live, working software that helped attract investment and relaunch the company under a new brand, with different goals and a larger scale.

The only issue was that there was still no one to do it. Since the end of 2024, I've been working in conjunction with AI.

The B&R Controller Story

This is best illustrated by a task from industrial automation. Many Russian car wash manufacturers, including us, built their automation systems using controllers from the Austrian company B&R. After the vendor left, it became impossible to buy this hardware, but it didn't stop aging. Usually, the controller dies first, while the I/O racks continue to function. A used controller through gray imports costs from half a million to three and a half million rubles. Per unit, and that's for each failure.

We came up with another solution: replace only the 'head.' Instead of a controller, we install a standard mini-PC, which is tens of times cheaper, while the original racks remain in place. The automation project, its logic, and visualization stay as they were; only the configuration changes. The problem is that the racks communicate via POWERLINK. This is a hard real-time protocol with a cycle time of two milliseconds, and it won't work on a standard PC by itself. It requires a real-time network card driver and a software gateway that polls the racks via POWERLINK and provides data in the format the project expects. An open-source stack for this protocol exists, but it was abandoned in 2019. It doesn't support modern network cards and doesn't compile for new Linux kernels.

This is what engineers call serious work: real-time kernel, driver, low-level protocol. Previously, for such a task, I would have looked for a rare specialist. Whether I would have found one or not was a question, and several months of their work would have cost roughly the same millions as the controller. Meaning, it was more profitable to simply buy used controllers as long as they were available.

With AI, it turned out differently. The driver for Intel I226-V, the gateway, and parsing the project took less than a week. We spent another week testing everything on a bench with actual B&R racks, and the very first long run achieved nearly three hundred thousand communication cycles without a single missed packet. I'll detail how the driver works and why the old stack doesn't work on new cards in the second article, which will be purely technical. Here, I'll discuss the division of roles. The AI wrote the code. I decided what to write, in what sequence, and how to test it. The test bench was also my responsibility because real-time performance can't be verified in words; you need live racks and devices.

Interestingly, this works both ways. There are areas where my experience prevents me from missing an error, which I'll discuss below. And there's the opposite situation, where AI combines things that I've done separately but never together. I've dealt with real-time kernels. I've done CPU core isolation for tasks. I've written drivers. But I had never managed to combine all of this into one working project before.

And one more difference, perhaps the most important for me. Previously, such work involved searching Google for code snippets on how to build a kernel, how to set up a driver, stitching them together, and then spending weeks figuring out why it wasn't working. With AI, you immediately get a cohesive foundation to build upon. Essentially, it replaces several different specialists because it can integrate code from various domains into a single project. Provided you define the architecture and context for it.

What Else I Had to Do Alone

The rest of the work, previously done by the laid-off employees, turned out similarly. Now, I'm responsible for microcontroller firmware, drivers, operating system images for terminals, and panel binaries for the new brand. A cloud application for dispatching and remote management of car washes, with statistics, analytics, and role-based access. A loyalty system using MIFARE cards with a cloud backend, personal accounts, transactions, and accounting. Billing integrations with Yandex.Zaporovki, SBP, and other services. Notification bots. Plus, compiling automation projects in Automation Studio, which is the B&R development environment. I barely used it before, and it turned out not to be a hindrance: it's enough for me to understand what needs to be achieved on the rack and know how to verify it.

Previously, such a volume would have been a year's plan for an entire team. The language and stack became irrelevant. Before, they were determined by the type of programmers we could hire; now, they are determined by the task. I can take someone else's code or an existing technology at any stage, understand it, modify it, and deliver it in working condition. For me, this is more valuable than any cost savings.

About the Mushroom

I didn't come up with this meme. This is how AI sees most people, and until recently, I saw it the same way.

But if you look closely, this is what's happening in the picture. The person asked a question that can only be answered with 'yes' or 'no,' and provided nothing but the question. AI is designed to produce some kind of result regardless. It's the same in development. If you ask, 'Will this driver work?' you'll get a 'yes.' But if you ask it to list which card registers the driver accesses, in what order, and what happens if the transceiver (PHY) doesn't respond, it can't just agree; it has to figure it out. When a person understands what they're asking, there's no room for a simple 'you are absolutely right.'

This, by the way, is an old story about an engineer and a non-engineer. Give both a thick manual and ask them to find a transistor. One will flip through all thousand pages; the other will open the table of contents.

I had to test myself to see if I was fit for this role: setting tasks in a way that couldn't be misunderstood, correcting them as we went, verifying the results, and sending them back with comments.

It turns out I am. In practice, it looks like this. First, the AI does what I asked. Then, through iterations, we bring it to a working state. We both find errors: I spot some, it finds others on its own, and we uncover some together. We don't have to argue. You bring it what you got from the live hardware, a measurement, or a log, and it finds its own mistakes.

An example from the B&R project. The first version of the driver compiled without a single warning, yet the card wouldn't have sent a single frame over the network. The transmit buffer was zero-sized, and the hardware scheduler's 'gates' were closed. Even before the first run on hardware, we compared the driver line by line with the reference driver from the Linux kernel and found thirteen errors. Five of them were precisely the kind where everything compiles, but nothing works. We fixed them in the same conversation.

This method allows you to arrive at things that no one remembers in the mushroom story: security, optimization, architectural choices, balancing. And ensuring that new code doesn't break anything nearby. The kernel, system services, and other processes run on the same machine, and your code shouldn't interfere with their memory or processor time, nor should it corrupt what worked before. AI solves the task it's given. I have to ensure it doesn't introduce regressions elsewhere.

So, the mushroom from the meme is dangerous to those who can't verify it. The result must have verification criteria, and the human sets them. The AI's capabilities depend on the completeness of the knowledge and experience of the person guiding the dialogue, assuming the result is something that actually works and can be verified.

Therefore, I always design the test architecture myself. I build them based on the entire environment: hardware, network, operating system, a person with sweaty hands at a terminal in minus twenty degrees Celsius. AI doesn't know what's important on-site and what can be neglected unless you tell it.

About Context

When people talk about 'programming,' they usually mean code. In automation, the problem could easily be electromagnetic compatibility: interference from power equipment on a communication line looks exactly like a protocol error. Or it could be the cable, the grounding, or the temperature inside the cabinet.

Or it could be a violation of the standard. Due to sanctions, our coin and banknote acceptors are reprogrammed by local developers with their non-standard code to accept new bills and coins, which continue to appear. And here, you can't figure it out easily. The device is visible, it seems to communicate via the protocol, but it works incorrectly, and there's no answer in the documentation because the standard has already been violated. Previously, resolving such issues took days with an oscilloscope and exchange dumps. With AI agents, analyzing such situations and finding the necessary modifications has become several times faster. But where to dig and the final verification on live hardware remain my responsibility.

AI only sees what it's given. If it doesn't know where a power line runs on-site, it will choose the simplest path, which might not be suitable for that particular site. It won't ask about it itself; the person assigning the task must consider it.

Therefore, I don't think it's about 'prompt engineering.' Rather, this kind of work will eventually evolve into a different kind of knowledge: what a specialist in their field must master for AI to yield results in their hands.

About Design

What I absolutely didn't expect from AI was help with design. I'm a perfectionist. This is useful in algorithms. You set a goal, say, processing within two milliseconds without any hangs, and you achieve it because you understand how everything works, and either the number is met or it isn't. With design and interfaces, it doesn't work that way. There's no 'done' criterion; you can rework it endlessly and never be satisfied. AI freed me from this. It immediately suggests a familiar solution, and it's hard to argue with a familiar solution; it's familiar precisely because it's understandable to everyone. At the same time, I realized about myself that my perfectionism isn't about aesthetics at all. I need uniformity, symmetry, and logical transitions, and these can be demanded and verified just like two milliseconds.

Where My Experience Comes From

In the 2000s, I wrote software for ship control systems and managed their development. Under my leadership, we created our own PLC platform, a universal set of I/O modules on which any control and automation device could be assembled. I wrote all the software for it. It was configured, by the way, using a single-line seven-segment display. Equipment with my software has been installed on military ships since the mid-2000s and continues to be installed today.

This experience made me an architect. I don't have a software architect diploma; I have twenty-five years of working systems. The problem was that I could only prove it through others' work. First, convince someone, then hire people, then wait for them to do it.

By nature, I need to test hypotheses and break patterns, and previously, each test took months. Now, from idea to verification takes hours. I no longer have to defend architectural decisions verbally. They can be implemented and demonstrated within a reasonable timeframe and without a full-time staff.

I wouldn't have chosen the circumstances that led me into all this. But it's precisely because of them that I've become who I am: an architect who brings their solutions to a working result themselves. Honestly, I'm enjoying my work now more than ever.

What I Learned From This

AI's knowledge didn't replace mine. It replaced the hands that were lacking. That, for me, is the difference from vibe coding. I know exactly what needs to be achieved. I know the architecture, the protocols, how it breaks, and how to test it, and AI writes code within those boundaries. Without twenty-five years of experience, I wouldn't have been able to set the task for it or verify the result. So, the bottleneck in this partnership is one thing, and it's me. AI multiplies what a person already has, and if there's nothing to multiply, there will be no benefit.

Hence, the main thing I've taken away. The limit of what can be done with AI is set by the person who assigns the task and accepts the result. AI itself does not set this limit. Every engineer has their own, as I call it, cone of vision: how broadly they have developed, what they've been interested in, whether they understand how things work, from the physics of a signal in a wire to the psychology of a person at a terminal. Everything that falls within this cone, AI will do, because you'll be able to assign it, verify it, and spot errors. Everything outside its limits will remain unverified. You simply won't have the criteria to distinguish what works from what looks like it works.

The broader the experience, the fewer tasks remain that cannot be delegated to AI. I believe that with such a verifier, it can replace any executor. It cannot replace the verifier themselves.

One could probably call all of this vibe coding. Only when you've spent a quarter of a century working towards it, it becomes ordinary, current programming—the kind that, I think, has been awaited since programming first appeared. This tool came to me at fifty. There's no time to regret it; there's a lot of work to do.
