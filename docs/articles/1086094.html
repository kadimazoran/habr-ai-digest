Most conversations about AI in business currently end up pretty much the same: writing an email, summarizing a document, creating an image, or analyzing an Excel file.

But we were more interested in exploring a different scenario: what happens if we give AI direct access to a company's accounting system, not just a data export? What if it could check sales itself, verify inventory, find debtors, create purchase orders, fill in product cards, and even investigate who changed prices and by how much? To find out, we connected MoySklad to AI assistants via MCP and started assigning them typical entrepreneur tasks.

This article is based on an interview with Yuri Kaya, founder of SOLOpreneur

Here's what we discovered.

First, Why MCP?

You could export an Excel file, upload it to a chat, and ask for an analysis. But within a few hours, the data might already be outdated.

MCP (Model Context Protocol) allows for a different approach. It's a standard that gives AI access to external systems and their tools.

In our case, the assistant gains the ability to directly access MoySklad data.

The process looks something like this:

Human → ChatGPT / Claude → MCP → MoySklad

And the interaction is bidirectional.

The AI can not only ask the system, "How much of product X is currently in stock?" but also perform actions: create a counterparty, modify a product card, or prepare a document.

Currently, about 340 operations are available through the MCP server.

But we weren't interested in the number of API methods; we had a more practical question: What tasks of an entrepreneur can be delegated to AI today?

Case #1. 30 New Products and Several Hours of Manual Work

Imagine a typical delivery.

30 new items arrive. The accounting system has the product names, but no proper product cards yet.

For each, you need to find characteristics, create a description, add a SKU, upload photos, and assign a category.

This is usually a tedious conveyor belt process.

We gave the AI this task:

Take products from the latest receipt that lack descriptions. For each, find its description and photos online, write a text of 300–400 characters in your own words, fill in the SKU and characteristics, upload three photos, and categorize the products according to the catalog.

Then, the interesting part begins.

The assistant first gets the list of products from MoySklad, then searches for information, generates descriptions, and writes the results back into the product cards.

This means the human doesn't have to go through the chain:

MoySklad → Google → manufacturer's website → save image → write text → back to MoySklad.

All of this is condensed into a single request.

There's a nuance: you can't completely let the AI run unsupervised here.

Descriptions should be reviewed, and photos found should be checked for usage rights. Therefore, a reasonable scenario today isn't "AI has completely replaced the content manager," but rather:

AI does 90% of the mechanical work → a human reviews the result.

And this is perhaps one of the main takeaways from our experiment.

Case #2. Counterparty from a Telegram Photo

Another completely common situation.

A client sends a company's details to a manager. Sometimes it's a PDF. Sometimes a scan. Sometimes a photo of the document in a messenger.

The manager opens MoySklad and starts transferring:

  • Name;

  • INN (Taxpayer Identification Number);

  • KPP (Reason Code for Registration);

  • OGRN (Primary State Registration Number);

  • Address;

  • Bank account;

  • BIK (Bank Identification Code);

  • Phone;

  • Email.

We simply sent the file to the assistant and wrote:

Here are the client's details. Check if this counterparty already exists in MoySklad. If not, create them. If any details are missing, list them. At the end, provide a link to the counterparty's card.

Here, the AI proved useful in another area we hadn't initially considered.

Before creating a new entry, it can check the database for duplicates.

If the company already exists, instead of creating a second "Romashka LLC," the assistant will find the first one and suggest adding missing details.

As a result, the "open document → retype details → check → save" scenario is reduced to almost a single command.

Case #3. Morning Telegram Check – Your Business Report is Already There

This experiment turned out to be one of the most illustrative.

Accounting systems accumulate a vast amount of reports. But the owner of a small business often doesn't need a report.

They need an answer.

For example:

How much did we earn yesterday?

Are sales growing or falling?

What's running out of stock?

Who owes us money?

We set up a daily task:

Every day at 8:00 PM, compile the revenue and profit for the day, compare it with yesterday and the same day last week, show the five most profitable products, unfulfilled orders, and customers with debts over 50,000 rubles.

The assistant independently retrieves the current metrics and compiles a concise summary. If connected to Telegram, it can be delivered directly to the messenger. This represents a significant change in how business management interfaces work.

Previously, a person had to log into the system and search for the necessary data. Now, the data comes to the person.

And the request can be changed daily:

Add the current cash balance.

or:

Show only customers with overdue payments longer than a week.

This doesn't require redesigning a dashboard or asking a developer to add a new report.

Case #4. AI Calculated It Was Time to Order from the Supplier

Purchasing is more interesting.

Here, AI doesn't just display information; it helps make operational decisions.

We give the command:

Look at sales over the last 30 days and stock levels in the main warehouse. Find items that will last less than two weeks and prepare a purchase order for the supplier with a month's supply. Group products by supplier.

To fulfill the request, the assistant has to correlate several types of data:

sales → product consumption rate → current stock → supplier → required quantity.

After calculation, it can create a draft purchase order.

And here, we deliberately keep a human in the loop.

The AI does not send the order independently.

The manager or owner opens the document, reviews the quantities, and only then confirms the purchase.

Case #5. Find the Money Owed to You

Another request:

Show customers with debts over 30,000 rubles and overdue payments longer than ten days. For each, show the amount, invoice number, and shipment date. Write a polite reminder for payment.

The output is not just an accounts receivable report but a practically ready list of actions for the manager:

"Alpha" LLC — 87,000 ₽ — overdue 14 days → here's a message for the client.

"Beta" LLC — 54,000 ₽ — overdue 19 days → here's a message for the client.

The message itself can then be sent via the appropriate communication channel.

This means AI is gradually starting to connect data with actions.

Case #6. "Who Changed the Price?"

There's a category of questions that only arise when something has gone wrong.

For example:

Why is this product now 7,990 instead of 6,990?

Or:

Where did the document go?

The system has an audit log. But when there are thousands of operations, a person has to search for the relevant change.

You can ask the AI:

Who changed prices for products in the "Tools" category in the last week? Show the old and new prices. Also, check for deleted documents separately.

The assistant analyzes the events and returns a clear answer instead of a list of technical entries.

This is an interesting example of how LLM works not as a replacement for a database, but as a human interface on top of it.

Case #7. "Increase Prices by 7%, But Not for Everything"

Finally, we tried a scenario for mass data modification:

Increase prices in the "Accessories" category by 7%, excluding products with less than three units in stock.

Technically, the AI can do this.

But this is precisely where the line between "can" and "should" becomes clear.

Making a mistake in an analytical query is unpleasant. Making a mistake when changing hundreds of prices is already costly.

Therefore, for such operations, we use a different principle: first, the assistant shows exactly what it intends to change, and then the user confirms the action.

And the most critical operations, like deletions, should require separate confirmation.

So, What Has Changed?

After these experiments, we realized one thing.

The most interesting aspect of connecting LLMs to business systems is not the ability to ask questions in natural language.

That's just the first level. At the second level, AI starts gathering information from multiple sources:

sales + inventory + cash + documents.

At the third, it prepares an action:

create an order, fill in product details, add a customer.

And then the next scenario emerges:

doing this automatically on a schedule or based on an event.

For example:

Every morning, check inventory levels. If stock is less than a seven-day supply, prepare an order.

Or:

Every evening, find overdue payments and prepare a list of clients for the manager.

Or:

Once a week, perform an ABC analysis and report which products have moved from group A to group B.

In this way, the accounting system gradually stops being a place where a person constantly goes for information.

It becomes a data and action source for the assistant.

Isn't It Scary to Give AI Access to Business Operations?

This was the second question that arose almost immediately after the "Wow, it works!" moment.

Because it's one thing to allow a neural network to view sales data. It's another to allow it to change prices or delete documents. Therefore, access can be restricted.

For example, an employee can be given a separate connection that can only read data and generate reports, but not modify them.

You can separately allow document creation while keeping deletions inaccessible. And potentially irreversible actions require confirmation.

We would generally recommend implementing such things gradually:

first, reading → then analytics → then creating drafts → and only then modifying data.

What Shouldn't Be Fully Delegated to AI Yet

After our experiments, we have a fairly simple list.

AI excels where extensive searching, comparison, data transfer, and calculation are needed.

However, it's still better to leave the following to humans:

  • Verification of information found online;

  • Financially significant decisions;

  • Mass data modifications;

  • Irreversible operations;

  • Non-standard situations where the cost of error is high.

Therefore, the most realistic model today is not "autonomous AI manages the company."

Rather:

AI acts as a very fast operational assistant that has access to data and knows how to use business tools.

And this is already a significant change.

You Can Try It on Your Own Account

We've created an MCP server for MoySklad that can be connected to ChatGPT, Claude, and other MCP-compatible clients.

The setup takes about ten minutes. After that, instead of studying documentation, it's easier to start with a question:

How many products are currently in my MoySklad?

If the assistant responds with an up-to-date figure, the connection is working.

Then you can try:

Which products generated the most profit this month?

And then:

Which of them will run out soon?

And at this point, it becomes clear why you would give a language model access to an accounting system.

Not so that it can converse better. But so that it can finally start doing something.
