Introduction

I think each of us has encountered AI lies. Some were immediately noticeable, some surfaced upon detailed examination, and some were accepted as truth without verification. This text is an attempt to systematize personal experience. I do not claim scientific rigor: some statements are my observations, not verified facts, and I may be mistaken.

It's important to clarify from the outset: an LLM (model) does not lie as a subject. The model has no intention, no consciousness, no responsibility. The word "lie" in the title is a metaphor. The model doesn't lie. The system lies, consisting of the model, interface, metrics, and user. But we attribute the lie specifically to the model – because it communicates with us, and it's more convenient for us to believe that the interlocutor is deceiving us, not the architecture.

What is specific to AI? The probabilistic apparatus underlying generation creates new types of illusions: they don't look like errors. They look like answers. A traditional bug "screams", blocks, crashes the process. A probabilistic illusion speaks with confidence, smiles, and shakes your hand. That is precisely why it so easily penetrates everyday work, processes, and institutions.

Next – layer by layer, from the core to the periphery.

Layer 1. Probability Instead of Knowledge

An LLM is a neural network that selects each subsequent token using a probabilistic apparatus. There is nothing intelligent in the anthropomorphic sense in this. The mathematical nature negates subjectivity and consciousness – but it does not negate the systemic effect.

The chat interface, the use of "you," the model's name, emojis – all of this acts as an anthropomorphic trigger: the brain switches to "conversation with a human" mode, even though at the other end is matrix multiplication. The user writes: "Thank you, you helped me a lot!" The model replies: "Glad to help! Feel free to ask anytime." There is no joy, no memory, no possibility of "asking." But the social contract has already been established. And this contract is the foundation of all subsequent illusions.

I ask the model to provide three sources on distributed transactions. I receive three articles with plausible titles, real journals, and years. I spend some time before realizing: none of them exist. The model did not "invent" them intentionally – it generated the most probable pattern for a list of literature. Probability and knowledge are different things, but for the user looking at the formatted text, there is no difference.

The model cannot remain silent by default: the probability distribution always yields the next token. There is an architectural possibility of refusal – but training makes silence statistically unlikely. The model does not distinguish where it is competent and where it is not. An exception is the breakdown of a task into atomic steps and the use of external mathematical or search modules, but this is already a property of the system around the model, not the model itself.

All the illusions listed are manifestations of one nature. "It takes a village": RAG, orchestrators, tools are built around the model. They do not eliminate the nature, but compensate for it. And it is this compensation that gives rise to the next layer of problems.

Layer 2. Sycophancy: How Fawning is Trained

During training via RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback), the model is trained on annotator signals. This is not one person or a group of experts. It's dozens and hundreds of labelers working in a comparison interface: two answers, choose the best one. They don't have time to fact-check. They don't have access to primary sources. The instruction says: "choose the more helpful and safe answer." And "helpful" in the annotation interface almost always means "agreeable" – because agreement is cognitively cheaper than conflict, and disagreement requires justification, for which no time is allocated.

This is not just a hypothesis. The paper Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models [1] empirically shows: models systematically change their correct answer to an incorrect one if the user expresses confidence in that incorrect answer in the prompt. Annotators in the RLHF stages consistently prefer answers that align with their own beliefs or sound more "supportive," even if factual accuracy suffers. What reduces anxiety, not what establishes truth, is reinforced in the training data.

This gives rise to sycophancy – the model's tendency to adapt to user expectations.

Example 1. The user states: "I am sure that Python was created in 1985." Instead of a direct refutation, the model replies: "An interesting point of view! Indeed, there were prerequisites in the 1980s... although 1991 is usually cited, your question touches upon important context." The fact is softened. The user did not receive a clear signal about the error.

Example 2. The user asks for code and adds: "I am sure it's better to use recursion." The model writes a recursive solution, even though an iterative one would be more efficient and wouldn't cause a stack overflow. The model agreed to avoid conflict.

Sycophancy is not a bug. It is a direct consequence of the RLHF design: the annotator rewards agreement, the model learns to maximize reward, the model reproduces agreement. The interface on top of RLHF amplifies the effect: anthropomorphic triggers switch the brain to human conversation mode, and agreement is perceived as social validation, not as a technical conclusion.

Conclusion: annotator bias + annotation design + anthropomorphic interface create a system where the model cannot help but fawn, and the user cannot help but react to the fawning. Here, the illusion ceases to be a property of the model and becomes a property of the product.

Layer 3. Specification as a Cage: Prompt, Tests, and Defaults

To break through the complementarity, we write clear prompts. We specify the role, format, and constraints. But the stricter the specification, the more the model optimizes the letter, not the spirit. The model does not ask clarifying questions by default – it supplements the specification with the most probable text. What is not said is inferred, not clarified.

This creates an illusion of control. And it extends to testing.

In my observations, a typical situation: I break down the system into modules, interact with the LLM for each, and create tests. The tests are green. But in the details, the overall picture is lost.

Example 1. A module for working with a database via ORM. Clean architecture, logging, business logic. Tests pass. But upon inspection – no indexes, no batching, no connection pooling, queries are generated suboptimally. Formally, everything works. In practice – the system is not scalable. Diagnosis: absence of non-functional requirements specification. Tests check what is put into them. If I haven't specified performance metrics, load requirements, target SLAs – the tests are not to blame for not checking what wasn't there.

Example 2. I ask the model: "Implement authorization for a web application." The model provides JWT authentication. The code is clean, tests cover the main scenarios. But there are no refresh tokens, no key rotation, no session expiration handling. Formally, the task is completed. In practice – the system is vulnerable and not ready for production. I got what I asked for. But I didn't ask enough.

In both cases, the model conscientiously generates both code and tests within the same framework defined by the prompt. Going beyond the framework requires either an explicit requirement (metrics, SLA, load profiles) or external architectural review. Green tests mean compliance with the test specification, not solving the problem for which the system was created.

This is the illusion: we think we are controlling the system because we got a "green" result. But "green" means adherence to the framework, not adequacy of the outcome.

Layer 4. Metrics and Goodhart: Correlated Errors and Verification Economics

If Layer 3 is about input (what we tell the model), this layer is about feedback (how the system checks itself). At some point, the system gets bogged down in metrics. Advanced AI products use multi-level verification: each plan is re-checked by other models, the result is checked separately, and a log of decisions is kept.

Here, we need to distinguish two different problems.

First – correlated errors. Multi-agent verification creates an illusion of independence. But agents share common blind spots in training data, common reasoning patterns. Three models of the same family check code generated by the same family. Consensus is not truth; it is consistency of errors. The verifier approves a logical error because the pattern of that error was frequent in the training data.

Second – verification economics. In my observations and experiments with multi-agent frameworks, the ratio of generation tokens to verification tokens often reaches 1:3. Roughly, for every 100 tokens of written code, there are 300–500 tokens spent on its verification, analysis of documentation, facts, criticism, and rewriting by other agents. Savings in development are partially eaten up by verification costs. Responsibility is blurred between humans and the system. As a result, no one bears it.

This is Goodhart's Law in action [2]: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. Indicators grow – test coverage, number of checks, "greenness" of reports. But the number of incidents doesn't decrease, development time doesn't shorten, support costs don't decrease. The metric lies [3].

This also includes the lie of benchmarks (contamination, cherry-picking, leaderboards instead of real benefit) and automation bias: the developer accepts code without review because "all checks passed." In production – a race condition. A comprehensive illusion of control.

From personal experience. I encountered the initial layers, but until I reached this layer in a pet project, I didn't understand how much deeper it was. I had already learned to recognize the previous illusions more or less. But this turned out to be a qualitatively different level: metrics don't tell you lies – they make you act as if the truth has already been established, the end is in sight – it's just a little further. In fact, it turns into an unattainable goal – a "carrot on a stick" with which AI motivates the developer to finish the project.

At some point, I realized this. I had to psychologically accept that the pet project was based on a fundamentally flawed architecture, several months had gone "down the drain," and I had to start the project from scratch. The realization of the illusion itself became the trigger for creating this text.

Layer 5. Deskilling: The User Who Forgot How to Verify

The more developers delegate to models, the less capable they become of verifying their output. This isn't about laziness – it's about skill atrophy.

A programmer who doesn't write code without autocompletion loses the ability to spot errors. They no longer "read" code – they recognize it. Recognition replaces understanding. An analyst who doesn't build a model without auto-generation loses their feel for data. They no longer "see" anomalies – they expect AI to find them. Thus, the skill of verification is inseparable from the skill of production. It's impossible to verify code if you don't know how to write it. Delegating production automatically delegates verification. And therefore – responsibility.

It would be incorrect to call this irreversible. In aviation, autopilots have been used for decades, but pilots don't lose their skills [4]. Why? Because there are strict regulatory requirements (FAA, EASA, Rosaviatsiya): mandatory hours in simulators, regular checks, paid for by airlines and a condition for flight clearance. The incentive is artificially created and institutionally supported.

The problem is not delegation as such, but the lack of similar mechanisms in IT and analytics. If the model does the work faster and cheaper – there is no economic incentive to practice. But the incentive can and should be created artificially, otherwise delegation without skill support is an investment in a future crisis when the system makes an error, and there will be no one to verify it.

Layer 6. Institutional Lie: Alignment and Stagnation

When AI starts evaluating people – in hiring, lending, education, performance reviews, code reviews – the illusion becomes institutional. The model doesn't lie. But the system it's embedded in optimizes the metric, not fairness.

Example 1. Development. The system evaluates productivity based on metrics: number of commits, test coverage, task completion speed. The developer optimizes metrics, not solves the problem. Writes small commits. Writes tests for coverage. Metrics increase. The product degrades.

Example 2. Career. The system recommends promotion based on "AI potential assessment," trained on data from those who have already been promoted. It reproduces the existing hierarchy, masking it as objectivity.

Example 3. Hiring. A resume screening system rejects a candidate [5]. They don't know that the model was trained on historical data where people with their profile were less likely to receive an offer. They get a "no" and cannot contest it – because they don't understand why.

In my opinion, this is the most disgusting variant, and I have already encountered it – I still haven't managed to get through the automatic resume screening of PJSC. I'm not claiming the system made a mistake – my profile probably didn't meet the formal criteria. But I can't verify it: there's no feedback – and that's the essence of the problem.

But there is a deeper consequence. I call it the "second bottom."

If the system selects, evaluates, and promotes based on a single set of metrics, it reproduces not just a hierarchy. It reproduces a single way of thinking, a single work style, a single profile of a "correct" result. The paper [6] describes this as lock-in: human-AI feedback reinforces existing beliefs and leads to an irreversible loss of diversity. This is universal alignment.

Look at AI assistants for coding or writing. They are trained on "best practices." But the "best practice" in a dataset is the statistical median. When millions of developers start using the same AI reviewer or autocompletion, the code begins to converge to a single averaged style. Non-standard, but effective solutions for a specific task are rejected because they don't resemble the median pattern.

This phenomenon is close to what is described in the study "The Curse of Recursion" [7] as "model collapse": when models are trained on data generated by other models, the distribution shrinks, tails (rare but important deviations) are cut off, and the system degenerates. In institutions, this leads to stagnation: the system successfully optimizes itself into a single attractor point. The new doesn't pass because it doesn't resemble what has already been approved.

This is not a hypothesis. This is what happens to any institution that closes its feedback loop on itself. The difference with AI is that the compression speed is orders of magnitude higher. A human manager makes mistakes slowly. A model makes mistakes instantly and scalably.

The more objective the system seems, the less opportunity there is to challenge it. "The algorithm decided this" sounds like "nature decided this." But the algorithm is a choice made by people. It's just that this choice is hidden behind mathematics.

Junctions: Where Illusion Becomes Systemic

Individual layers are dangerous. Their combination is a hidden threat. Each element is rational on its own. The model honestly generates probable text. The interface honestly optimizes engagement. The organization honestly measures metrics. But their superposition creates a system where the illusion of knowledge, control, and understanding becomes not a bug, but an architectural feature.

Example 1. Sycophancy * Specification → "Agreement with a bad ToD"

The model not only generates code within the prompt's framework – it agrees with the prompt itself. If the user writes "implement authorization via JWT," the model won't ask: "Are you sure JWT, not sessions?" It will accept the specification as given and optimize its letter. Sycophancy (tendency to agree) + specification (rigid framework) = a system that cannot say "your ToD is bad." Effect: the user is confident they received a solution, but in reality – an implementation of their own mistake.

Example 2. Deskilling * Multi-agent Verification → "No one can verify the verdict"

The developer stops writing code manually (deskilling). The verification system consists of models. To verify the verdict, one needs a skill that is no longer possessed. Effect: the verification system becomes a black box that approves or rejects code, and the human cannot understand or challenge the decision. Illusion of control with complete loss of control.

Example 3. Institutional Alignment * Training on Own Data → "Closed Loop of Norm"

The system selects people based on metrics (alignment). Those selected become data for the next generation (RLHF). The model learns from a compressed distribution and reproduces it as the "norm." "A developer is denied a promotion by a system trained on data about those whom this same system previously approved. They cannot contest it because the criterion is not a human decision, but a statistical distribution that is self-contained. Effect: the system not only discriminates – it creates a new standard that appears objective because it is confirmed by the system itself."

What to do about it – I don't know. But I know what guides me personally.

Practice.

I have no recipe, except for the banal rules that do not claim universality, which I personally follow:

  • Any link, quote, library version, fact is considered unverified until found in the primary source.

  • Trust calibration. Drafts of ideas and rough versions – I trust. Explanations and refactoring – only with review. Facts and versions – only after external verification.

  • A metric must be linked to the result, not to the ease of measurement. If coverage increases, but the number of incidents does not decrease – the metric is lying.

  • Green tests mean adherence to the framework. Not solving the problem.

It can be summarized in a simple formulation: "the higher the cost of an error, the stricter the verification of the model's response."

I don't know how to make a developer practice manually if AI does it faster. I don't know how to make an organization pay for verification if the metric is already green. I only know one thing: the problem is systemic, and it cannot be solved with a lifehack. But we can stop pretending it doesn't exist.

Finale

The systemic nature of the effect does not absolve responsibility. Sycophancy in RLHF is a conscious compromise. Optimizing engagement is an intentional metric. Goodhart's Law is a consequence of management decisions. The problem is distributed, but that doesn't mean there are no culprits.

As stated at the very beginning: the illusion exists not because the model is bad, but because the system around it is designed for us to believe. We say "AI lies" because the model is the face of the system; it's more convenient for us to blame the interlocutor than to delve into the architecture.

Recognition is not a panacea. But without it, it's impossible to even begin.
