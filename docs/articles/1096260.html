Hello everyone, this is the team behind the SimpleOne ITSM product. The title is a provocation, of course – we are not suggesting shutting down support. However, if we look at how employees solve minor IT problems in 2026, it might seem like the service desk is no longer particularly necessary.

No Ticket, No Incident, But a Problem Exists

Twenty minutes before a client call, a manager opens the CRM, but instead of a report, they see an error message about a connection to the information base with a code that means nothing to them. They could go to the portal, find the relevant service in the catalog (somewhere between "Access to Information Systems" and "Other"), describe the problem, and wait for the first line to process the queue. Or, they could copy the error text into a neural network, get step-by-step instructions in seconds, clear the cache, restart the client, and make it to the call on time. The latter option wins in terms of time.

In the article by "Computerra", shadow AI is defined as the use of public AI services by employees for processing corporate data without IT and information security approval. It only requires a browser tab, making it spread faster than shadow IT. A confidential document fed into a public neural network could end up in its training data. In the context of support, this means every error, log snippet, or configuration file pasted into a chat goes to external servers and is processed by an external model.

There's no malicious intent here: the employee chooses what's fastest. A neural network doesn't have a request form or a queue, and it doesn't send an auto-reply like "Your request has been received, resolution time is 8 business hours." The service desk is built for manageability and traceability (registration, classification, assignment, resolution), but the employee needs to be ready for a call, and each step in this chain consumes time they don't have.

But What Is Lost Along With the Ticket?

The problem is solved, and the user continues working, but along with the ticket, everything that support could have extracted from it disappears. This affects three areas:

  1. Problem Management. Suppose after a CRM server update, the same error appears for forty employees, and each resolves it themselves via ChatGPT. There are no incidents in the system, so the second line doesn't see a pattern that would indicate a server configuration issue. If server monitoring doesn't detect this error, tickets are the only signal. Instead of forty workarounds for one problem with a found root cause, the knowledge base of known errors remains empty where it's most needed.

  2. Security Operations. If a user has local administrator rights, a SOC analyst might notice an anomaly on their workstation: a registry parameter has changed, a service has restarted, or a new rule has appeared in the local firewall. Normally, such an event would be cross-referenced with an incident or a change request, making it clear who did what and why. Here, there's nothing to cross-reference: the change was made by the user themselves, following the neural network's advice, and the only record might be in their chat history. Each such event has to be investigated from scratch, the team handles extra alerts, and an attack could go unnoticed.

  3. Data Control. The content of the request poses the most significant risks. For the neural network to help, it needs context, and the employee conscientiously provides it: the full error text with the server name, a snippet of a configuration file, a log with IP addresses and colleagues' account details, and sometimes even a password, "to make it clearer." The model asks clarifying questions, the employee answers them, and this data cannot be retrieved.

In all three cases, the reason is the same: the request is not logged anywhere, and neither the second line nor the security team becomes aware of it.

Russian Specifics

For foreign companies, shadow AI remains a matter of hygiene and internal policies. In Russia, we also have Federal Law 152-FZ. If a log sent to a neural network contains the employee's full name, work email, or phone number, it constitutes personal data. Transferring it to an external service is only permissible with legal grounds, typically a contract where the data controller commissions processing to a contractor. If the service is foreign, cross-border transfer also requires notification to Roskomnadzor. An employee pasting a log into a chat five minutes before a call doesn't meet these conditions, and the company, as the personal data operator, will be held responsible.

Since May 30, 2025, fines for leaks have increased, with repeat leaks incurring turnover-based fines of up to 3% of annual revenue. Passwords and configurations are not considered personal data, but almost every large company has an internal policy prohibiting such information from leaving the corporate network, and a chat with an external model violates it.

The scale is already being measured: according to Anti-Malware.ru, Solar Group analyzed the traffic of 150 Russian organizations and found that in 2025, the volume of data employees sent to public neural networks increased 30-fold. This data included source code, financial reports, legal documents, and client databases. Approximately 60% of organizations still lack formal rules for working with AI services. These figures cover all AI usage, and IT support is only a part of it. According to "Informzashchita," cited by RUБЕЖ, 20% of organizations that suffered data leaks linked the incidents to uncontrolled AI use (up from about 12% the previous year). In 42% of cases, data was exfiltrated through regular chatbot web interfaces – the same way our manager solved the CRM problem.

There's also access restriction. ChatGPT, Gemini, and Claude are officially unavailable in Russia, so employees using them connect via personal VPNs and personal accounts. Corporate security tools see this traffic only partially at best, and chat histories are stored in accounts inaccessible to the company, disappearing with the employee after termination. Domestic VPN services don't require VPNs, but data is still transmitted to external operators.

Prohibit or Allow?

The security department's first reaction is understandable: block access to neural networks or enable DLP and traffic control. Such measures are necessary, but they don't eliminate the need. An employee might photograph an error with their personal phone and ask the same question over their mobile internet, and now neither problem management, SOC, nor the cybersecurity department is aware of it. There's still no ticket, and visibility has decreased even further.

Cyber hygiene training is useful, and a policy for working with AI services should be written: employees will learn that they shouldn't paste passwords and configurations into chats. However, under time pressure, people choose the fastest answer, no matter how thoroughly they were explained the rules during onboarding, and a record of the interaction is still not created.

One could hire more first-line agents to speed up the queue. This is expensive, and the result is limited: even if the first line responds in minutes, a neural network responds in seconds. If the employee still turns to a neural network, the additional agents receive neither tickets nor data for problem management.

Neither prohibition, nor policy, nor hiring brings back what is lost with the ticket: a signal of recurring failures, context for SOC, and control over what goes out. These will only return if the fastest solution is within the company's perimeter and leaves a record of the interaction.

AI Guide-Assistant: How to Simplify Support Requests and Bring Employees Back

Let's imagine our manager again, whose CRM doesn't open before a client call. Now, on their company's service portal, there's a chat that responds as quickly as the familiar neural network. We released such an AI guide-assistant in July 2026 in the SimpleOne ITSM 2.0.0 version. The manager types in natural language: "CRM won't open, connection error to the database," and they don't need to know the service's name in the catalog. The assistant is built on RAG: before the model generates a response, it searches corporate data, including the service catalog, typical requests, knowledge base articles, announcements, and known error databases, and then compiles an answer with links to sources.

The scenario then depends on what was found. If there's already an entry in the known error database for this error with a workaround, the assistant will show it in the chat. When the service has already published an announcement about a failure (e.g., "CRM server unavailable until 11:00"), the employee will be informed immediately, and the first line won't receive a duplicate incident. The assistant briefly retells the answer from the knowledge base and suggests opening the full article. If nothing suitable is found, it asks for more details, determines that an incident is needed, and prepares a request form with pre-filled fields: the employee only needs to review and submit it.

According to our team's estimates, in a typical scenario, a person receives an answer or a filled-out form in 30-60 seconds, without needing a VPN or a personal account. More details on scenarios and economics can be found in Andrey Vishnyakov's article on ITWeek.

The main difference from an external neural network: every dialogue with the assistant is saved within the platform (there's a separate chat table for this), and messages are linked to tickets if the request reaches that stage. Even when a question is resolved in the chat, forty identical questions about the CRM after a server update are visible in the system as a recurring request that can be used for problem management. The SOC analyst gets a record to cross-reference workstation changes, and the knowledge base owner gets a list of questions for which there are no articles yet.

The assistant utilizes the GenAI capabilities of the SimpleOne platform, which can be deployed on the customer's infrastructure and connected to domestic models or the company's own LLM. It's still best not to paste logs with IP addresses anywhere, but if they do end up in the assistant's chat, and the model is hosted on the customer's infrastructure, they remain within the company's perimeter.

Where Are the Boundaries of This Approach?

Information security measures remain in force: a policy for external AI services, DLP, and traffic control are still needed, and the assistant removes the main reason for bypassing them.

The assistant itself also carries a risk: all data employees paste into the chat, including passwords and logs, ends up in its dialogues. Therefore, chat retention periods and access rights for searching the knowledge base must be configured before users start using the assistant. Data masking, which works in SimpleOne, mitigates some of the risk. If data requirements are even stricter, a local LLM can be connected to the assistant, and employee requests will not leave the company's infrastructure.

The quality of answers depends on what's in the knowledge base. RAG only searches existing materials, so if the articles are from 2021 and the known error database is not maintained, the assistant will be retelling outdated information. Content owners will have to keep it up-to-date, although this is beneficial even without AI. We discussed who in the company is responsible for the knowledge base's currency and the AI's answer accuracy, and how to measure it, in an article on Habr.

The AI guide-assistant reduces the time from question to answer or filled-out request. The second line remains in place: engineers find the root cause, they also configure the CRM server, and data about interactions is now available in the system.

Summary

Prohibition won't solve the problem: employees will go where they are completely invisible. The AI guide-assistant on the service portal solves it by responding just as quickly and leaving the interaction within the platform. This makes recurring questions visible as patterns for problem management, provides security teams with a record of the advice an employee received, and, when the model is hosted on the company's infrastructure, prevents logs with passwords from going to external servers via personal VPNs.
